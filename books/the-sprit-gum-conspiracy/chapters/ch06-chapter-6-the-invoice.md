# Chapter 6: The Invoice

Spirit gum, it turns out, is basically pine sap dissolved in alcohol. I learned that at two in the morning on a cosplay forum, and it saved my life.

Which was lucky, because by that afternoon, the second emergency on my lesson plan had arrived by courier, on a scroll, with a wax seal and a late fee.

But I didn't know any of that yet when I woke up the morning after my presentation and felt my left ear peeling away from my head.

I must have rolled onto it in my sleep. The whole top edge had come loose, a good half inch of latex flapping free like the corner of a peeling sticker. I pressed it down with my palm and held it, and counted to thirty, and let go.

It lifted right back up.

I got out the travel bottle and squeezed it anyway, out of hope and habit. It wheezed. I sat on the edge of the parking-space bed with one hand clamped to the side of my head and looked at my lesson plan on the desk across the room, at the word EMERGENCIES at the top of the page, underlined twice.

Panic is a funny thing. It makes you stupid for a minute, and then, if you're lucky, it shakes something loose.

What it shook loose was July.

It was two in the morning, about a month into building the costume, and I couldn't sleep, so I was doing what I always do when I can't sleep, which is read cosplay forums until my eyes give out. And I'd clicked on a thread titled *DIY SPIRIT GUM (cheap!!!)*, posted by someone called glue\_goblin\_77. I remembered the first line of it almost word for word, because it had made me laugh: *Spirit gum is literally just tree sap and booze, you guys.*

Resin, the thread said. Pine rosin, specifically, dissolved in the strongest alcohol you could get your hands on. I'd never tried it, because a bottle of the real thing costs six dollars at the party store and I am lazy. But I'd read the whole thread. Twice. It was two in the morning.

Tree sap and booze.

I was living in a palace made of trees. And I knew a dwarf.

Thessa came in with my breakfast tray a few minutes later and found me standing in the middle of the room, holding my ear and grinning like an idiot.

"Thessa," I said. "Do you know a tree with sticky sap? The kind that goes hard, like amber?"

I'd never seen her face do what it did then. All the nervousness went out of it at once, and something sharp and bright came in to replace it.

"Weepwood," she said instantly. "Silver bark. It bleeds amber when the bark is cut. My clan uses the resin to fix the feathers on our arrows. It holds through anything." Her charms jingled. "There's a grove at the edge of the palace grounds, Highness. By the old wall."

"Can you show me?"

She was already picking up her satchel.

Brakka escorted us there, walking ahead with one hand resting on the hilt of a sword I had never seen him draw. The grove sat in a quiet corner of the grounds, against a wall so old it had ivy growing on its ivy. There were about a dozen weepwood trees, tall and straight, with bark like hammered silver. Little beads of golden resin clung to their trunks wherever a branch had broken, glowing in the morning sun like drops of honey.

I took out the butter knife I'd liberated from breakfast and started scraping at the nearest bead, and Thessa caught my wrist.

"Not like that, Highness." She said it before she could stop herself, and then she looked horrified at her own boldness. But she didn't let go. "You'll hurt the tree. You make a small cut, here, at an angle, and let it give what it gives. Then you come back tomorrow. You don't take more than it can spare."

She showed me. Her hands were completely steady, and her voice was completely sure. I'd never heard her sound like that. I made a mental note of it: Thessa, talking about trees. That was who she was when she wasn't scared.

We worked for an hour, filling a teacup with resin. Brakka stood guard at the edge of the grove, watching us with deep approval.

"Arrow glue," he rumbled, nodding to himself. "The Prince prepares for war."

"Sure," I said.

"Strategy."

\* \* \*

Resin was half of it. The other half was the booze, and according to glue\_goblin\_77, it had to be strong. Wine wasn't going to cut it.

I found Durgan in his usual alcove, eating a breakfast that consisted mostly of a ham, and asked him what the strongest drink in the Silverwood was.

He looked at me with deep respect.

"The elves don't have anything worth the name, Highness," he said. He reached into his robe and drew out a squat, dark-glass bottle stoppered with wax. "But *this*. This is firewater. Distilled in the deep forges of Under-Karrak, aged in barrels of black oak for nine years, and strong enough to strip paint, dissolve rust, and cure all sorrow." He considered the bottle fondly. "Not necessarily in that order."

"Can I have some?"

Durgan looked at the bottle. Then he looked at me. Then, with the air of a man who has never once missed an opportunity, he reached into his robe again.

It was Form 14-C, Acknowledgment of Receipt of Ritual Spirits. I signed it *Aelindor* in my whiteboard capitals. He handed over the bottle with a little bow.

Back in my chambers, I set up my laboratory. A small copper pot from the tea service. A floating lamp, pulled down to table height. The teacup of resin. The bottle of firewater. A silver spoon. Thessa sat on a stool to my left, watching intently, and Brakka stood in the doorway, watching the hallway, though he kept glancing back over his shoulder at the pot.

I melted the resin first, over the lamp. It went soft and then liquid, and the whole room filled with a sharp, clean smell of pine. Then I uncorked the firewater.

I want to be very clear that I had never in my life smelled anything like dwarven firewater. My eyes watered from three feet away. Brakka coughed in the doorway. Out in the hall, I heard one of the elven guards gasp, and a moment later, a murmur traveled down the corridor and out of earshot: *the Prince is practicing the alchemy of the Old Kings.*

I poured a little into the melted resin and stirred.

I should have thought about what happens when you put very strong alcohol into a hot pot sitting right over an open flame. My chemistry teacher, Mr. Hadley, would have thought about it. But I was thinking about my ear.

There was a soft *whump*, and a sheet of pale blue fire rose off the surface of the pot.

It wasn't big, maybe six inches high, and it would have burned off on its own in a second or two. But Thessa didn't know that. She went rigid on her stool. I heard her breath catch. Her hands, folded in her lap, began to glow silver-white.

And then, through her teeth, she said: "*Pineapple!*"

I didn't think. I grabbed the pot by the handle with a towel, swept it off the lamp, and dove behind the couch.

I heard her move. I heard her turn on the stool. And I heard the flash go off, not at me or at the pot, but straight up, a deep breath of sound rushing toward the ceiling.

Then it was quiet. The pine and alcohol smell was gone. In its place, the air smelled like clean laundry and lightning.

I peeked over the back of the couch.

Thessa was standing in the middle of the room with her hands still raised over her head, staring up. The ceiling of my chambers was dark old wood, stained from centuries of lamp smoke. Right above her, there was now a perfect circle of gleaming, honey-colored wood about ten feet across, as clean as the day the tree was cut. It looked like someone had installed a skylight.

Thessa lowered her hands slowly. She looked at the ceiling. She looked at me, crouched behind the couch with the pot still clutched against my chest. Then she looked at her own hands as if she'd never seen them before.

"It worked," she whispered.

"It worked," I said.

And she laughed. It was the first time I'd heard her laugh: a startled, delighted little sound, like a bell. Her charms jingled along with it.

In the doorway, Brakka nodded, deeply satisfied. "Strategy."

"That one was all her," I said.

Thessa went red to the tips of her tusks and looked happier than I'd ever seen her.

The pot had survived, and so had the mixture inside it. When it cooled, I strained it through a square of Thessa's herb cloth into the empty spirit gum bottle and a little glass jar. It came out a clear, deep amber. I dabbed some on my fingertip and pressed my thumb against it, and pulled them apart. It stretched into a thin, sticky string.

Tacky. Exactly right.

I brushed it under the edge of my left ear, waited for it to go tacky, pressed the ear into place, and counted to thirty. Then I let go.

It held.

I took a long, deep breath. The smell of it drifted up to me: sharp and sweet and slightly ridiculous, like a pine tree that had been drinking.

It smelled exactly like spirit gum.

I got out my lesson plan, crossed out *Tree sap? Research*, and wrote underneath it: *Hypothesis confirmed.*

\* \* \*

The courier arrived that afternoon.

He was an elf named Lord Aldwin Merrow, and he was the Lord Treasurer of the Silverwood, which as far as I could tell meant he kept the High Houses' books. He was small for an elf, prim and neat, with ink stains on his long fingers and the anxious, apologetic air of a man who delivers bad news for a living and has never once gotten used to it.

He bowed (the seventh kind, for a royal audience) and held out a scroll sealed with silver wax.

"With the compliments of the High Houses, Highness," he said, and winced slightly, as if the words tasted bad.

I broke the seal and let the scroll unroll.

It kept unrolling. I knew where this was going. I'd seen a scroll like this before, on my first day, when Durgan had produced his contract in the summoning chamber, and I watched this one hit the floor, roll across the rug, and come to rest against Brakka's boot.

At the very bottom, in large, clear numbers, was a total.

*40,000 crowns.*

"It's an invoice," Lord Merrow said quietly. "From the Guild of Arcane Conveyance. For the summoning. The Guild billed the High Houses, and the High Houses have... forwarded it. To you, Highness. As the beneficiary."

"As the beneficiary," I repeated. "Of my own kidnapping."

He didn't answer that. To his credit, he looked like he wanted to.

"How much is a crown?" I asked.

Lord Merrow thought about it. "A skilled craftsman might earn three hundred crowns in a good year, Highness."

I did the math. I teach seventh grade, not twelfth, but I can still divide. It came out to about a hundred and thirty years of a skilled craftsman's wages.

"All right," I said slowly. "And the Prince's treasury?"

Lord Merrow coughed into his fist.

It turned out that the royal estates of the First Line had been distributed among the High Houses some centuries ago. For safekeeping. Until such time as the Prince returned. The Houses had kept them very safe, Lord Merrow assured me, as he studied a point on the floor about a foot to the left of my boots. The Prince's lands, his revenues, his treasury: all of it had been kept extremely safe, in the vaults of the High Houses, where it had been for three hundred years.

"So the Prince has..." I prompted.

Lord Merrow looked at my bedsheet cape, my foam sword, and my bathtub-dyed tunic. "His garments, Highness," he said gently. "And the Vow."

I'd started this week with $3.12 in my checking account. I'd been a little worried I might not make rent.

Tucked inside the scroll was a smaller note, on heavy silver paper, in a beautiful flowing hand.

> *Highness,*
>
> *The High Houses would never dream of pressing such a debt upon our Prince. The Guild, alas, is less patient. Do let me know if I can help.*
>
> *C.*

I read it twice.

I knew what it was. I'd seen this exact trick before, in a slightly different outfit. My first week of student teaching, the district gave me a classroom with no paper, no markers, no glue sticks, and a broken pencil sharpener. And then, very kindly, they mentioned that the PTA sometimes helped out teachers who stayed on its good side.

*Do let me know if I can help.*

It wasn't an offer. It was a leash. Caelith didn't want my money. I didn't have any. He wanted me to owe him, and to have to come to him, and to know that I had to.

I'd been in this world for less than a week, and I'd already been put into crushing debt by the people who'd kidnapped me.

Honestly, it felt very American. It felt like student loans.

Lord Merrow bowed and backed out of the room, leaving the scroll on the floor. Brakka looked down at the end of it, still resting against his boot, then up at me.

"Is that a lot?" he asked.

"Yeah, Brakka," I said. "That's a lot."

\* \* \*

Lady Ithrien came by around sunset with the day's gossip, which she'd started delivering to me personally, like a newspaper. I let her get through all of it (who had fainted at the presentation, who had faked fainting, who had greeted whom with the goose word and been cut dead at dinner) before I asked my question.

"Lady Ithrien. How does a prince earn money?"

She looked at me as though I'd asked how a prince grows wings.

"Earn, Highness?"

"Hypothetically."

"Princes don't *earn*, Highness. Princes..." She trailed off, frowning, and I could see her going back through centuries of court gossip, looking for an answer. Then her face lit up.

"Oh! The Petition Wall!"

In the age of the Old Kings, she explained, nobles with a problem too large or too strange to solve themselves would write it down and post it on a wall in the old palace courtyard, along with the reward they were offering. And the princes of the First Line would read the petitions and choose the ones they'd answer, and ride out to slay the beast or lift the curse, and come back with the purse and the glory.

"No one has answered a petition in three hundred years, of course," she added. "These days, we have the army."

She took me there herself.

The Petition Wall stood in a forgotten courtyard on the palace's north side, where the paving stones had cracked and moss grew in the cracks. It was a long stone wall about twelve feet high, and it was covered from end to end in paper. There were layers of it, petitions pinned on top of petitions with little silver tacks, centuries deep. The oldest ones at the bottom had gone brown and soft and as fragile as lace.

I started reading.

*LOST: one cat, gray. Answers to Whiskerlord. Reward offered.* The date at the bottom was two hundred and ninety years ago.

*Something lives in the north cistern. It sings. Please make it stop.*

*A troll has taken up residence beneath the Moonford Bridge and demands a toll of all who cross. Please send a hero.* That one was dated the Year of the Purge. Underneath it, in at least four different hands, someone had added notes over the centuries: *Still there.* Then: *STILL there.* Then, more recently: *He's actually quite nice now. Bring him cake.*

And then, on top of everything, pinned at eye level with a fresh silver tack, the ink so new it was still glossy:

> *My wine cellar has been overrun by geese. I do not know how. I do not know why. There are a great many of them and they are very angry. Reward: 200 crowns.*
>
> *Lord Thandril of House Velloran.*

I stood there and looked at the wall, and I felt something I hadn't felt since the floor opened up under me in Raleigh. I felt like I knew exactly what I was doing.

It was a quest board.

I've spent probably a thousand hours of my life standing in front of quest boards in video games. I know how they work. You start with the easy ones. You build up gold and gear and reputation. You don't go after the dragon until you've cleared out the cellar rats.

Or, in this case, the cellar geese.

I reached up and pulled down Lord Thandril's petition. Behind me, Lady Ithrien gasped, and then clapped her hands together.

"The Old Kings return!" she breathed.

I folded the petition and put it in my belt pouch, next to the empty spirit gum bottle. Two hundred crowns. I did the math on the walk back. It was half of one percent of what I owed. At this rate, I could pay off the whole invoice in another hundred and ninety-nine geese-based emergencies.

It was a start.

On the way back to my chambers, Caelith passed me in a corridor, walking with three elves in silver armor whose cloaks marked them as generals. He paused, smiled, and inclined his head.

"Highness. You'll be pleased to know the preparations are going well." He gestured toward the generals. "The northern passes are impassable during the long rains, so we'll use the time to make ready. The army will march north the day the rains end." He smiled again. "With you at its head."

Then he moved on, his generals sweeping after him.

I stood in the corridor and did that math too. The long rains would start in a few weeks, Brakka had said. They'd last two months. And on the day they ended, I'd be leading an army north to kill the last human alive.

I'd always wondered what it felt like to have a deadline you couldn't extend. Now I knew.

\* \* \*

I got up before dawn the next morning, glued my ears with my new homemade spirit gum, which held beautifully, put on my bedsheet cape, picked up my foam sword, and opened my door.

Brakka was at his post, as always. Thessa was already waiting in the hall with her satchel over her shoulder, packed so full of herbs it was spilling, and a look on her face that I can only describe as determined terror.

And beside them stood Durgan Pell.

He was wearing a traveling cloak over his purple robe. His staff with the cracked crystal was in one hand, and his flask was in the other, and on his back was a pack so enormous that it rose a good two feet above his head. Several things were tied to the outside of it, including a frying pan, a small folding stool, and what appeared to be a second, larger flask.

"Ritual advisor," Durgan announced, "reporting for duty."

I looked at him. "How did you even know—"

"Lord Thandril's wine cellar," Durgan said, "is the worst-kept secret in the Silverwood. The geese have been honking through the ventilation shafts for a week." He tapped the side of his nose. "And I reasoned as follows. My bonus depends upon Your Highness paying my invoice. Your Highness's invoice depends upon your geese. Therefore, I go where the geese go."

I opened my mouth to tell him no. Then I closed it again.

Because there was something he hadn't mentioned, and that he didn't know, which was that my ears now depended on him too. The bottle of firewater he'd given me would make maybe three more batches of glue. After that, I'd need more. And there was only one source of dwarven firewater in the entire Silverwood, and he was standing in front of me with a frying pan tied to his back.

"Fine," I said.

Brakka made a noise deep in his chest, like a boulder settling. "The dwarf will slow us down."

"The dwarf," said Durgan, unbothered, "will be at the back, where it's safest." He tapped his nose again. "Strategy."

Brakka turned his head and stared at him with an expression of naked betrayal. I don't think anyone had ever used his word against him before.

I looked at the four of us, standing in a palace corridor in the gray light before dawn.

I've played a lot of RPGs. And there's something every single one of them has in common: they all start with a bad party. You get the rogue who steals from you. The healer who can't heal. The knight who's afraid of horses. The game hands you a bunch of misfits who would never in a million years choose to stand next to each other, and then it makes you go out and fight something together.

And you hate them, for a little while. And then, somewhere along the way, without noticing exactly when, you'd die for them.

We set out at dawn: a prince who wasn't, a bodyguard who thought lunch was strategy, an attendant who could erase me, and the dwarf who'd kidnapped me.

Every RPG starts with a bad party. I'd just never been in one where the bad party was the good news.
