# Chapter 11: The Royal Archives

I've taught the history of a lot of wars. I had never had to find out who was lying about one.

It started with a wedding.

Lady Ithrien's niece was getting married at the end of the month, and Lady Ithrien, who by then had appointed herself the court's leading authority on the customs of the Old Blood, wanted to know how the Old Kings had done weddings. She came to my chambers to ask, with a notebook.

I'd gotten good at this. By my fourth week as Prince, I'd invented the Vow of the Moon, the Garments of the Journey, the Sword-Bearer's Plate, and about a dozen smaller customs I'd made up on the spot and then had to remember. So I leaned back in my chair and told her, with great confidence, that at Old Blood weddings, the bride and groom shared a single slice of pie, from opposite ends, until they met in the middle.

Lady Ithrien wrote it down. Then she frowned at her notebook.

"How curious, Highness," she said. "The Codex says the Old Kings were wed beneath a living oak, with a crown of acorns."

I'd never heard of the Codex saying any such thing. I realized, with a cold feeling in my stomach, that I had no idea what the Codex said about anything except archery and appetite.

"Both," I said. "The pie comes after the oak."

She brightened and wrote that down too. But I'd felt the floor shift under me. I'd been making up customs for weeks in a court that had a library full of the real ones. Sooner or later, someone was going to check.

And someone was already trying. That same week, the Society for the Preservation of Starlight Fasting pinned a notice to the Petition Wall, in Venrick's elegant handwriting, titled *Questions for the Prince Regarding the True Customs of the First Line*. There were thirty-one questions. I knew the answer to none of them.

A few of them, to give you the flavor:

> *Question 4: Did the Old Kings eat with their hands, or with tongs?*
>
> *Question 17: Is the Prince aware that pie is not mentioned anywhere in the Codex?*
>
> *Question 31: If the Prince is truly of the First Line, why can he not answer Questions 1 through 30?*

The Veil excuse was wearing thin. You can only claim amnesia so many times before people start to wonder what else you've forgotten.

So I started going to the archives. Every day.

Master Quillon Ferreth greeted this news the way some people greet a lottery win. On my first morning, he met me at the bottom of the spiral stair with his hands clasped and his spectacles fogged up with emotion, and gave me a tour that lasted three hours and covered roughly one percent of the collection. On the second morning, he had a desk cleared for me. On the third, there was a cushion on the chair, and a pot of tea waiting, and a little card in his trembling hand that said *RESERVED: H.R.H.*

He had two apprentices, the same two young elves in gray who had pushed his book cart into the summoning chamber on my first day. One was a cheerful girl named Isolde, who talked constantly and dropped things. The other was a quiet, polite young man named Fenn, who never dropped anything and almost never spoke. Fenn had a gift for finding the book you wanted before you'd finished asking for it. He'd appear at my elbow with it, set it down, nod, and drift away between the shelves, and a little while later I'd look up and find him reshelving something nearby.

I thought he was just good at his job. That was how quiet he was.

\* \* \*

I meant to study weddings. I really did. But you can't put a history teacher in a library this size and expect him to stay in one aisle.

By the end of the first week, I'd drifted from the Customs of the First Line to the Chronicles of the Concordance, and from there to the shelf that every history teacher eventually ends up at, whether he means to or not. The war shelf.

There were about forty books on the Purge, and I read most of them. They had titles like *The Glorious Cleansing*, and *The Triumph of the Peoples*, and *How the Forest Was Saved*. They were beautifully written. They had maps with arrows on them, and illustrations of elven heroes in silver armor with their hair streaming in the wind. They were very sure of themselves.

I looked up *humans* in the index of *The Glorious Cleansing*. It said: *see Blight*. Under *Blight*, it said: *see Vermin*. Under *Vermin*, it said: *see Cleansing, Glorious*. I went around the index twice before I realized there wasn't a single page where they were people.

And I'd seen them before.

Not these exact books. But I've taught units on the worst chapters in human history, and if you teach those units long enough, you start to recognize the way the winners write about them. The patterns are always the same. The enemy is never people; they're a disease, or vermin, or a flood. The victory is always inevitable, as if it was written into the world from the beginning. The heroes have no flaws. And there's always a gentle, clean word for the terrible part, like the one here, *the Cleansing*, so that nobody has to say what actually happened.

I teach my seventh graders four questions to ask about any primary source. I make them write the questions on an index card and tape it to their desks. *Who made this? When did they make it? Why did they make it?* And the most important one, the one they always forget: *What's missing?*

So I asked the questions.

Who made these books? High House scribes. Every single one.

When? All of them, without exception, after the war. The oldest was written fifteen years after the Purge ended.

Why? To celebrate it. To explain why it had been necessary. To honor the Houses that had won it.

And what was missing?

I looked for an hour before I was sure. Then I asked Quillon, and he looked too, and when he came back, he was quieter than I'd ever seen him.

"There's nothing, Highness," he said. "Nothing written by the Blight. Not a letter. Not a ledger. Not one word in their own hand." He looked at the shelf. "The histories say that all of their writings were cleansed, along with them."

Forty books about a people, and not one word from the people.

The next day, I found the maps.

They were rolled up in a bin at the bottom of the war shelf, old land surveys from before the Purge, drawn on thick, yellowed paper. I unrolled one across my desk and weighted the corners with teacups. It showed the country to the east of the palace grounds, every road and stream and field marked in faded brown ink, with tiny careful drawings of houses. And the houses had names, in a script I couldn't read but the tongue-binding could: *Hollowell. Brennan's Ford. Ashmere. Little Thorncross.*

They were villages. Human villages.

I found the palace on the map. I found the Court of Fountains, and the archery green. And then I traced my finger east across the paper to the place where three great trees now leaned toward each other like old friends sharing a secret, where Lord Thandril's manor stood, with its wine cellar, and its tunnel, and its little round-eared family scratched into the mortar.

On the old map, it was a village called Hollowell.

I sat and looked at it until my tea went cold. Fenn came and refilled my tea, without being asked, and went away again.

\* \* \*

"I want to see what's behind the iron door," I told Quillon the next morning.

He went pale. On an elf as old as Quillon, that's quite a journey; he went from the color of old parchment to the color of new parchment. "Highness. It's sealed. By order of the High Houses. Nobody has—"

"Nobody has been through it in three hundred years. I know." I opened the Codex of the First Line, which by then I'd started keeping on my desk the way some people keep a dictionary, and turned to a page I'd found the night before. "Can I show you something?"

It was the founding charter of the royal archive. It had been written by the very first King of the First Line, long before the Purge, before any of the High Houses even existed, and it was short, the way the oldest laws usually are.

> *This house of memory belongs to all the peoples of the forest. Its keys shall be held by the heir of the First Line, and none shall bar the heir from any record within it.*

Quillon read it. Then he read it again, his nose about an inch from the page, his lips moving.

"That's older than the seal," he whispered.

"A lot older."

"The heir holds the keys." He looked up at me, and his eyes behind the enormous spectacles were very bright. "Nobody has read this in a very long time, Highness."

"Nobody reads the rubric," I said. "That's the whole problem."

He agreed, finally, with his hands shaking. But there was a problem, he said. The key to the iron door had been lost three hundred years ago. Some said it was melted down. Some said it had been thrown into the river. And there was a seal on the lock as well, a ward set by the High Houses, a pale silver mark that glowed very faintly in the dark.

I looked over at Thessa.

She'd come down with me that morning, along with Brakka, Durgan, and the goat, because by then the five of us went most places together. She was standing a little apart, by the end of a shelf, turning one of her charms between her fingers.

"Thessa," I said. "Is a ward a kind of curse?"

She thought about it seriously. "A small one, Highness. A binding." Then she saw where I was going, and her eyes went wide. "It's a thing that doesn't belong."

"And rust?"

"Doesn't belong either."

We all walked down to the end of the archive together. The iron door stood in the stone wall exactly as I'd left it: black and heavy, the chains crossing it in a big X, the lock the size of my fist, and above it the plaque: *RECORDS OF THE CLEANSING. SEALED BY ORDER OF THE HIGH HOUSES.* In the gloom, the ward on the lock glowed a faint, cold silver, like frost on a windshield.

Thessa stepped up to it. She didn't say "pineapple." She didn't panic. She took one slow breath, the way she had over Durgan's wound, and laid her hand flat in the air in front of the lock, and called it, on purpose.

The white light gathered around her fingers and went out from them, small and controlled and steady, like a flashlight instead of a flashbulb. Where it touched the lock, three hundred years of rust simply let go. It fell away in a soft orange powder and drifted to the floor. The silver ward flickered once, faded, and was gone. And with a heavy, grating *clunk* that echoed all the way down the length of the archive, the lock fell open.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Brakka stepped forward, put one enormous shoulder against the iron door, and leaned. It groaned like something waking up. Then it swung inward, slowly, and a breath of cold, dry, dusty air rolled out of the dark beyond it and over us.

Durgan was the first to recover. He reached into his robe and produced a form.

"Form 31-A," he said briskly. "Retroactive Request for Access to a Sealed Archive. Master Ferreth, if you'd countersign. Here, and here."

Quillon signed it with tremendous happiness, in two places, and then initialed it a third time for good measure.

It was at about that point that we noticed the goat had wandered off, and found it a little way back along the shelves, contentedly chewing the last few inches of a very old, very brittle scroll.

Quillon made a sound I had never heard an elf make. "That," he said faintly, "was the only surviving copy—"

"Of what?" I said.

"Of the menu," said Quillon, "for the coronation feast of King Elthrandir the Second."

Nobody knew what to say to that. The goat finished the menu, looked at us, and burped.

\* \* \*

The room behind the iron door was small and low and very cold. Fenn brought lamps. He set them down without a word and stood back by the doorway, and in their light we saw shelves of plain wooden boxes, stacked floor to ceiling, each one labeled in faded ink with a year. The dust on them was so thick that when Brakka brushed against a shelf, it fell like snow.

We were in there for six hours.

I'll tell you what I found in the order I found it, because the order matters. It's the order I'd teach it in. You don't hand students a conclusion. You hand them documents, one at a time, and let them see what the documents add up to.

The first box I opened held reports from before the war. They'd been written by elven foresters and Concordance observers, sent out to the borderlands to keep an eye on the humans, and they were not propaganda. I could tell the difference almost at once. Propaganda is loud and certain. These were tired and specific, the way real reports always are. A logging camp that had cleared an entire valley in a single season, past the treaty line. A tannery upriver from a beastfolk village that had poured its waste into the water until the fish died and the children got sick. Boundary stones moved in the night, three separate times, a quarter-mile at a time. Dozens of complaints, from dozens of places, over years.

I read some of them aloud. Nobody interrupted me.

When I'd finished, Brakka was standing very still in the corner with his arms folded. "Orcs fought in the Purge," he said quietly. "My grandfather's grandfather. I have heard the songs." He looked at the box of reports. "I never once asked why."

Thessa had been going through a box of her own on the other side of the room, quietly, the way she did everything when she wasn't scared. It was full of muster rolls: long lists, in the same neat black ink as the maps, of every clan and people in the Concordance and how many fighters each one had sent to the Purge. I saw her find her clan's name. I saw her stop.

I read it too, over her shoulder. *Clan Ashtusk: three hundred spears.* And beside it, in the margin, a short note in a different hand: *Compliance secured by grain embargo. House Vaunt.*

They had starved her clan until it agreed to fight.

Thessa read it twice. Then she put the roll back in the box, exactly where she'd found it, and closed the lid, and didn't say anything at all. I didn't ask her about it. Not yet.

The humans of Verathis had done terrible things. That much was true. The elves hadn't made it up.

Then I opened the second box.

It was full of maps. They were the same kind of land surveys I'd found on the war shelf, of the same human country to the east: the same roads and streams and little drawings of houses, with names like Hollowell and Brennan's Ford. Except these had been drawn on. Over each village, over each field and farm and stretch of forest, somebody had written, in neat black ink, the name of an elven House. *Vaunt.* *Aldervane.* *Velloran.* *Calloway.* The whole of the human country had been carved up into pieces, and every piece had been assigned.

I turned one over and found the date in the corner. Then I went and checked it against the chronicles, twice, to be sure.

The maps were dated two years before the Purge began.

The Grants had been drawn up before the war that was supposed to have made them necessary. Somebody had decided who would get the land, and then, two years later, they'd gone and taken it.

The third thing I found was in a box by itself, on the bottom shelf, at the very back. It was a report on the Burning of the Sister Trees.

Every history I'd read upstairs mentioned the Burning. It was where the Purge began: a fire in a grove of ancient trees, sisters to the Eldertree itself, deliberately set by the Blight, which the Concordance could not forgive. Every one of the forty books told it the same way.

There were two copies of the report in the box. Two drafts.

The first draft was written in a plain, quick hand, the hand of someone writing what they'd seen. It said the fire had begun in the night and spread fast in the dry season, and that its cause was unknown. And it said where it had started: *near the timber camp of House Vaunt.*

The second draft was the same report, copied out neatly. Except that the words *of unknown origin, near the timber camp of House Vaunt* had been crossed out with a single, firm line. And in the margin beside them, in a different ink, in a different hand, someone had written three new words.

*Set by the Blight.*

The correction was initialed. A single letter, elegantly made, with a flourish on its tail.

*V.*

I sat down on the floor of that cold little room with the two drafts in my lap, and I didn't say anything for a while.

Here's what I'm trying to tell you. I didn't find a simple story down there, with heroes on one side and villains on the other. I'd have liked to. It would have been easier. The humans of Verathis really had cut down forests, and fouled rivers, and broken treaties. People had suffered for it. Those reports were real.

And the Houses had drawn up the maps to divide human land two years before they went to war. And the war had started with a fire that the first report said nobody could explain, and that someone with the initial V had decided, with a single stroke of a pen, to explain.

Both of those things were true at the same time. Neither one canceled out the other. I'd spent a whole semester trying to teach twelve-year-olds that history is usually like that. I'd never had to sit on a cold floor and feel it.

Durgan, for once in his life, didn't say anything at all. Thessa had sat down beside me, close, without asking. And Quillon was standing in the doorway with one hand over his mouth, looking at the shelves of boxes.

"I kept these," he said, in a voice like paper tearing. "For three hundred years. I dusted the door." He shook his head slowly. "I never read them."

\* \* \*

It was dark by the time we came back up into the main archive. None of us were talking much. Brakka carried the two drafts of the fire report in a flat wooden box under one arm, because I hadn't been able to make myself leave them behind. Quillon locked nothing behind us. There was nothing left to lock.

At the bottom of the spiral stair, he stopped me.

"Highness," he said. "Wait. Please. I want to show you something."

He shuffled to his desk, pulled open the bottom drawer, and took out a single folded sheet of paper, handling it very carefully, the way you'd handle a pressed flower. I think he needed, after that room, to show someone something that made him happy.

"It's my favorite curiosity," he said. "I've kept it for forty years. A young scholar came to the archive, once. From the provinces, he said, though I never learned which. Quiet. Polite. He had an odd accent, and he read here every day for a whole season, and he asked a great many questions." Quillon's voice dropped. "Mostly about the iron door."

I went very still.

"When he left, he forgot this in one of the books. Or perhaps he left it." Quillon unfolded the paper and held it out. "It's in a script of no known people, Highness. I've shown it to every scholar who's come through in forty years. Nobody has ever been able to read a word."

I took it from him.

It was a sheet of lined paper. Lined. Thin blue lines, ruled by a machine, with a red margin down the left side and three little holes punched along the edge, like a page torn out of a spiral notebook. The handwriting was small and neat and slanted slightly to the right, in blue ink that had faded a little but hadn't run or blotted, the way it does when it comes from a ballpoint pen.

And it was in English.

> *If you can read this, you're from Earth too.*
>
> *Don't trust the High Houses. The truth is behind the iron door. I couldn't get in.*
>
> *Good luck.*
>
> *E.M.*

I read it three times. My hands had started to shake, a little, so I put the paper flat on the desk and read it a fourth time there.

*E.M.*

I thought about a banner the length of a minivan in the dealers' room of a Radisson in Raleigh. About a one-line quote on a tote bag. About a codex passage that matched the real one word for word, and a battle cry that was a filthy joke in a language nobody on Earth was supposed to know.

The man who wrote my favorite game had sat at this desk. He had asked the same questions I'd asked. He'd been stopped by the same door. And then, somehow, he had gotten home, and he'd gone and told the whole story to millions of people, in a way none of them would ever believe.

"Can you read it, Highness?" Quillon whispered.

I nodded. I couldn't quite talk yet.

"What does it say?"

I thought about how to answer that. "It's a note," I said finally. "From someone who was here before me."

Quillon's whole face lit up. He clasped his hands together, delighted, forty years of curiosity answered in one sentence, and didn't ask anything else. I loved him a little for that.

Looking back, I remember that Fenn was there, too, while I read it, a few shelves away, reshelving something. He'd been there all day. He was always there. I didn't think anything of it at the time.

*Don't trust the High Houses.* It was good advice, forty years late. But it was the first time since the floor opened that I knew I wasn't the only one.

I folded the page and put it in my belt pouch, next to the empty bottle of spirit gum. It was the first letter I'd gotten from home.

## Interlude: The Apprentice

The High Lord's private study was at the top of the tallest tree in the palace, and at night, with the lamps turned low, it was so quiet that Fenn could hear his own heartbeat.

He stood just inside the door with his hands folded and gave his report the way he'd been taught to: plainly, in order, leaving nothing out. The Prince had opened the iron door. The Prince had read the reports from the borderlands, and the surveys of the Grants. And the Prince had found the box on the bottom shelf, and read both drafts of the report on the Burning of the Sister Trees, and he had taken them with him when he left.

And one more thing. Master Ferreth had shown the Prince his curiosity, the page in a script that no one could read. The Prince had read it.

Caelith Vaunt sat at his desk by the window and listened to all of it without any expression at all. When Fenn had finished, he was quiet for a while. Outside, the wind moved through the leaves of the Silverwood, and the floating lights drifted through them like slow, sleepy fish.

Then he asked only one question.

"How did he seem? Angry, or afraid?"

Fenn thought about it carefully, because the High Lord did not like careless answers.

"Neither, my lord," he said. "He seemed sad."

Caelith looked at him for a long moment. Then he took a coin from the dish on his desk and held it out, and Fenn came and took it, and bowed, and went.

When the door had closed, the High Lord of the Silverwood sat alone in the lamplight.

He had read those two drafts himself, once. He'd been younger than Fenn was now. His great-grandmother had still been alive then, very old, very beautiful, very proud, the first Lady of House Vaunt to hold the eastern Grants. She'd shown him the box on the bottom shelf herself, on the day before it was sealed, and she had put her elegant finger on the initial in the margin, the V with the flourish on its tail, and said: *This is how a House is made.*

He had not thought about that day in a very long time.

A Prince who didn't know the truth was useful. Caelith had always known that. A Prince who knew the truth and was angry would be dangerous. A Prince who knew it and was afraid would be easy.

But a Prince who knew the truth, and was sad about it, and kept quiet, could be the most useful of all. Sad people rarely fought. They mourned. They waited. They told themselves there was nothing to be done.

Caelith poured himself a glass of wine, and did not drink it, and sat for a long time looking at the lights in the trees, thinking about a Prince who was sad.
