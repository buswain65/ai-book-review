# Chapter 9: The Archery Trial

I have never in my life hit a target with an arrow. I want that understood before I tell you how I won the Trial of the First Line.

Lord Venrick, it turned out, had found a new way to get rid of me. It involved a bow.

He sprang it on me at a meeting of the High Houses, about a week after the parade that never happened. I'd been attending these meetings as the Prince, which mostly meant sitting at the head of a long table in a room full of tree roots and nodding while elves argued about grain. That morning's agenda was war preparations: provisions, tents, how many wagons it takes to move an army north. I was learning a lot about wagons. I was trying very hard not to think about why.

Then Venrick stood up.

He'd changed since the parade. The sneer was still there, but something underneath it had gone quiet and patient, like a cat that has stopped batting at a string and started watching it.

"My lords," he said. "Before we speak further of the march, I would remind the Houses of the law."

He unrolled a scroll. Of course he did. Everybody in this world had a scroll.

"By the ancient law of the Silverwood," he read, "no heir of the First Line may command the host until he has proven his blood before the court in the Trial of the First Line, by splitting an arrow at two hundred paces." He lowered the scroll and looked at me. "Should the heir fail the Trial, command of the host passes to the next eligible House."

There was a pause.

"Which House is next?" I asked, though I had a pretty good idea.

Venrick smiled. It wasn't a nice smile. "By the reckoning of the Grants, Highness, House Aldervane."

His House.

I understood it all at once. Durgan's Form 12-B had made me legally non-refundable. Venrick couldn't have me unmade anymore. So he'd gone looking for another way to get rid of me, and he'd found one that was even better. He didn't need me gone. He just needed me to fail, publicly, in front of the whole court, and then he would ride north at the head of the army himself.

Every head at the table turned toward Caelith.

Caelith steepled his long fingers and considered the scroll for a moment, and then me, with that look I was starting to know well, the one that made me feel like a chess piece he hadn't decided where to move yet.

"The law," he said pleasantly, "is the law."

The Trial was set for five days' time.

Walking back to my chambers afterward, I remembered something. A week earlier, at the consultation table, a servant in Venrick's colors had paid forty crowns to find out whether he should bring a cloak to the Prince's archery trial. Days before Venrick had ever said a word about it in council. Days before anyone else knew there would be a trial at all.

He'd been planning this since before I predicted a single drop of rain.

I'd never held a bow. The closest I'd ever come was summer camp, when I was eleven, and that had ended with an arrow in the camp director's windshield. They didn't let me back the next year.

\* \* \*

Brakka took me to the orc guards' practice range the next morning.

It was behind the barracks, on the far edge of the palace grounds, where the elves didn't go: a long strip of packed dirt with straw targets at one end and a split-rail fence along the side. Thessa came with a quiver of arrows she'd fletched herself the night before, with weepwood resin. The goat came because it went everywhere now. Durgan came because there was a crowd to be had.

Brakka handed me a bow. It was an orc bow, short and thick and recurved, about as heavy as a crowbar.

"Stance," he said, and kicked my feet gently apart. "Breathe. Draw to your cheek. Look at the target, not the arrow. The arrow knows where it is."

I drew. I breathed. I looked at the target. I let go.

The arrow went into the dirt about four feet in front of me, with the dull *thump* of a garden trowel.

"Again," said Brakka.

I'll spare you most of the next three days. I'll give you the highlights.

I shot the dirt. I shot the fence, many times; by the second afternoon, it looked like a porcupine. I shot the barracks door, which opened a moment later to reveal a very confused orc in his underwear. I shot the goat's breakfast, a pile of hay on the far side of the range, and the goat moved about six inches to the left and kept eating around the arrow without looking up.

I tried physics. I'd actually taught projectile motion once, briefly, to my seventh graders, using water balloons in the school parking lot. I knew about angles. I knew about parabolas. I drew one in the dirt with the end of an arrow and explained it to Brakka, who listened with great patience and then asked if the parabola was going to hold the bow for me.

It wasn't. That was the problem. I knew exactly where the arrow was supposed to go. I just couldn't make my hands agree. The string slapped my forearm every single time, until the skin there was red and then purple. My fingers shook. My eyes closed at the last second, every time, no matter how hard I told them not to.

On the second day, Brakka watched me put an arrow into the top rail of the fence for the ninth time. Then he walked over, took the bow out of my hands, very gently, and looked at me with enormous kindness.

"Highness," he said. "A bow is not a frying pan."

By the end of the third day, I had shot something like two hundred arrows. I had not hit the target once. Not the bull's-eye, not the edge, not the straw. Not one.

Durgan, meanwhile, had started selling tickets.

"Seating for the Trial," he explained, when I found him at a folding table outside the barracks with a stack of hand-lettered cards. "Tiered. Front row's fifty crowns. It's going to be the event of the season."

"You're selling tickets to my humiliation."

"To your *triumph*, Highness," Durgan said firmly. He tapped a sign at the edge of the table that I hadn't noticed. It said, *ALL SALES FINAL.* "Refunds are not available."

\* \* \*

That night, I sat at my desk with my lesson plan and my purple forearm and stared at the wall.

Two days left. I was not going to learn archery in two days. I'd been doing it for three, and I was, if anything, getting worse; the goat had started to flinch when I picked up the bow. Whatever saved me was not going to be skill.

So I thought about what I did know. And what I know, more than anything, is tests.

In eighth grade, North Carolina gave every student in my grade a standardized writing test. You had forty-five minutes to write an essay on a prompt, and it was graded on a four-point scale. The night before, my English teacher, Ms. Delgado, handed us the actual rubric the graders would use, and said, "Read this. Not the prompt. This." So I did. And I noticed that the rubric gave points for using five vocabulary words from a list, for having exactly five paragraphs, and for restating the prompt in your conclusion. It said nothing at all about whether the essay made sense.

I wrote an essay about a sandwich. It used all five vocabulary words. It had five paragraphs. It restated the prompt, which was about the importance of community, in the conclusion. It got a perfect four.

It was the most important thing I learned in middle school, and I've been teaching it ever since: before you take any test, read the rubric. The rubric tells you what the test is actually measuring. It's almost never what it says on the front.

I put on my cape and went to find the archives.

They were in the oldest part of the palace, down a long spiral stair: a vast, dim, silent hall where the bookshelves weren't built, but grown, tree trunks rising from the floor with shelves curving out of them like fungus on a log. Floating lights drifted between them at the pace of a slow walk. It smelled like dust and old paper and, faintly, tea.

Master Quillon Ferreth was sitting at a desk near the entrance, hunched over a book with his nose about an inch from the page. When I said his name, he looked up, squinted, recognized the glitter, and lit up like a child at a birthday party.

"Highness! A *visitor*!" He clutched the edge of the desk. "Twice in one season! Someone wants to *read* something!"

I told him what I wanted: the original text of the Trial of the First Line. Not a summary. Not the version on Venrick's scroll. The original.

Quillon was so happy he had to sit down for a moment.

It took him twenty minutes to find it, shuffling between the shelves with a floating light bobbing over his head. When he came back, he was carrying a book I recognized. It was the same enormous volume he'd read from in the summoning chamber, on my first day: the Codex of the First Line. He laid it open on the desk, licked one ancient finger, and turned the pages until he found it.

I read it twice. Then I read it a third time, very slowly, the way I'd read that rubric in eighth grade.

> *Let the heir loose one arrow, and let it find its true mark, as the forest wills; and let the seers declare what the forest has said.*

That was it. That was the whole Trial.

"Where's the part about splitting an arrow?" I asked. "At two hundred paces?"

Quillon waved a hand. "Oh, that was added later, Highness. Much later. By King Elthrandir the Fourth, about eleven hundred years ago." He leaned in, confidentially. "He was a superb archer. He wanted everyone to know."

I sat back in my chair.

The Trial didn't say the arrow had to hit anything. It didn't say anything about aim, or distance, or targets. It said the arrow had to find its true mark, *as the forest wills*, and then the seers would decide what that meant.

It wasn't a test of archery. It never had been. It was a test of whatever the seers already believed.

I knew that kind of test. I'd aced one with a sandwich.

I thanked Quillon, which made him so happy he started to cry a little, and headed for the stairs. On the way, I passed the far end of the archive, where the shelves stopped and the hall ended in a stone wall.

There was a door in it. It was the only door I'd seen in the Silverwood that wasn't made of wood. It was iron, black and heavy, crossed with chains, and the chains were fastened with a lock the size of my fist. Above it, set into the stone, was a plaque.

*RECORDS OF THE CLEANSING. SEALED BY ORDER OF THE HIGH HOUSES.*

I stopped. Behind me, I heard Quillon shuffle up.

"Nobody's been through that door in three hundred years, Highness," he said, and his voice had gone quiet, quieter than I'd ever heard it. "Not even me."

I looked at the lock for a long time. Then I went back up the stairs. I had a trial to win first.

\* \* \*

The Trial of the First Line was held on the archery green, a long, perfectly flat lawn at the southern edge of the palace grounds, and Durgan had been right: it was the event of the season.

He'd built tiered seating along one side out of borrowed benches and planks. Every seat was taken. The High Houses sat in the front rows in silk and silver. Behind them sat lesser nobles, and behind them, standing, servants and guards and a row of orcs from the barracks, who I suspected had come to see if I'd hit the barracks door again. The miller from the consultation line was there. So was the wolf-folk shepherd, without the lamb.

At the far end of the green, exactly two hundred paces away, stood a single wooden post with an arrow already embedded in its center, waiting to be split.

Master Feyhallow raised his leaf to the sun and announced clear skies, which he was right about, because I'd sold him the forecast two days before. Lady Ithrien waved at me from the front row with a handkerchief. Durgan, having sold every seat, had taken up a position at a small table of refreshments near the edge of the green, where the wine was, with his back to the field.

And at the center of it all, on a little raised dais, sat the three seers.

They were the oldest elves I'd seen since Quillon: three thin figures in robes of plain gray, with long white hair and long white fingers and eyes like still water. They didn't speak. They didn't move. They just watched. The one in the middle, the eldest, had a face like a dried apple. Her name was Seer Ondraleth, and she had read the omens of the Silverwood for longer than the United States had existed.

Venrick went first.

He was very good. To be fair to him, he was excellent. He took the bow, a tall, elegant elven longbow of pale wood, and set his feet, and drew in one smooth motion, and held it for exactly one breath. Then he let go.

Two hundred paces away, the arrow in the post split down the middle with a sharp *crack* that carried all the way back to the stands.

The crowd went wild. Venrick lowered the bow, turned to me, and inclined his head, about a quarter of an inch, with a contempt so precise you could have measured it with a ruler.

Then they handed the bow to me.

The stands went quiet. Not all at once, but row by row, the way a classroom does when the kid who's never once answered a question stands up to come to the board.

I didn't nock an arrow. Instead, I turned to face the seers' dais, and I raised my voice so the whole green could hear me.

"The Codex of the First Line," I said, "says that the heir's arrow must find its true mark, *as the forest wills*."

I saw it happen. All three seers sat up a little straighter. Seer Ondraleth's still-water eyes came into focus on me for the first time. Behind me, I heard a murmur go through the stands, and I heard Venrick go very still.

"So I won't choose my mark," I said. "I'll let the forest choose it."

Venrick took a step forward before I'd finished. "With respect, Highness," he said, in a voice like a closing door, "tradition holds that the heir must split the arrow."

Seer Ondraleth didn't turn her head. She didn't even blink.

"Tradition," she said, "is younger than I am, Lord Venrick."

Venrick stepped back.

I closed my eyes. I raised my left hand and laid it gently over my left ear, and I stood there, listening, for five long seconds. Somewhere in the front row, Lady Ithrien gasped, and I didn't have to look to know she'd put her hand over her ear too.

Then I lowered my hand, nocked an arrow, and drew the bow, and aimed it straight up, directly at the sky.

My plan, to be completely honest with you, ended there. My plan was that the arrow would go up, and come down somewhere in the empty middle of the archery green, and stick in the grass, and I would announce that this was where the forest had chosen. I'd done the physics. I had drawn a parabola in the dirt. Straight up, straight down. I was fairly confident. I even had a speech ready for when it landed. It was about roots, and how the forest had chosen the earth itself, the foundation of all things. I'd practiced it in the mirror.

I let go.

The arrow went up. Every head on the green tilted back to follow it. It climbed into the blue sky until it was a tiny dark scratch against the sun. It seemed to hang there, for one long moment, perfectly still.

Then it turned over and started to fall.

I did not account for the wind. There was a breeze that afternoon, a light, pleasant, steady breeze coming in off the forest from the west. It wasn't much. It didn't need to be much. It caught the arrow on the way down and carried it, gently, gracefully, about forty feet to the east.

It came down with a whistle.

There was a soft, solid *thunk*.

And then, from the refreshment table at the edge of the green, there was a howl that I can only describe as the sound a dwarf makes when he has been standing with his back to an archery range, holding a goblet of wine, and has been struck squarely in the backside by an arrow from heaven.

The goblet went up in the air, turning end over end, and came down on the grass. Durgan went down with it.

Nobody moved.

Then Venrick found his voice. He turned on the stands, white to the lips, and pointed at the refreshment table. "I demand," he said, "the return of my House's seat money."

From the grass beside the table, face down, with an arrow standing straight up out of him, Durgan Pell raised one hand.

"All sales final," he said.

Then the three seers bent their heads together, and conferred in rapid, whispering voices, for what felt like about an hour and was probably fifteen seconds. Then they straightened up. Seer Ondraleth rose to her feet, slowly, with tremendous dignity, and lifted one long white hand.

"The arrow," she said, in a voice like dry leaves, "has found the one who summoned the Prince across the Veil."

A murmur rippled through the stands.

"The circle is closed," said Seer Ondraleth. "The forest has sealed his claim."

The stands erupted.

I've never heard a sound like it. Elves were on their feet, cheering, throwing flowers that they'd brought for exactly this purpose. Lady Ithrien was weeping into her handkerchief. In the back row, the orcs from the barracks were pounding each other on the shoulders. The wolf-folk shepherd was howling, which I think was cheering.

Venrick was standing at the edge of the field, completely still, the longbow hanging forgotten from one hand. He had gone the color of old cheese.

And on the far side of the dais, Caelith rose to his feet. He began to applaud. Slowly, deliberately. He was smiling.

I'd won. I'd kept command of the army. I never did get to give my speech about roots.

But the High Lord didn't look like a man who'd just watched someone else win. He looked like a man who'd just watched his piece land exactly on the square he wanted.

\* \* \*

An hour later, Durgan Pell was lying face down on the couch in my sitting room, with his robe hitched up and a cushion over the part of him that had been involved.

The arrow was out. Brakka had removed it, in the end, with one quick pull and a grunt of professional approval ("Clean. Good depth."), while Durgan said a series of words in Dwarvish that the tongue-binding declined to translate for me. Now Thessa was kneeling beside the couch with a bowl of water and a cloth, staring at the wound.

She wasn't panicking. That was the first thing I noticed. Her hands were steady. Her charms were quiet.

"Highness," she said, without looking up. "In the cellar. When you made the roles. You asked if I could clean a wound."

"I remember."

"I said yes." She took a slow breath. "I didn't know if it was true."

She laid her hand flat above the wound, not touching it. And for the first time since I'd known her, she didn't wait to get scared. She didn't say "pineapple." She just closed her eyes and called it, on purpose, small and careful and controlled.

A soft, silver glow spread out from her palm, about the size of a saucer. It lasted three seconds. When it faded, the wound underneath was perfectly clean. Not healed; she couldn't do that. But clean. Every trace of dirt, every fiber from the arrow, every speck of whatever had been on the refreshment table was gone. It was the cleanest wound I had ever seen. It would never, ever get infected.

Thessa sat back on her heels and stared at her own hand.

I didn't say anything. I didn't need to. I just nodded at her, the way you nod at a kid who's just gotten something right for the first time, and she went pink to the tips of her tusks.

Durgan, meanwhile, had decided how he was going to tell the story.

"It was always part of the ritual," he said into the couch cushion, with great dignity. "The original summoning ritual. Section fourteen. The closing of the circle. Very rare. Very advanced."

"Durgan," I said, "you were standing at the wine table."

"I was *positioned*," said Durgan, "at the wine table."

He held out one hand, without lifting his face from the cushion, and Thessa, who was learning fast, put a quill into it. Then a sheet of paper. Lying face down on my couch with an arrow wound in his backside, Durgan Pell began to draft an invoice. I read the top line over his shoulder: *Hazard pay: struck by the will of the forest (posterior).*

In the doorway, Brakka was humming.

It was the song. My dad's song, the one from the balcony. He had the fiddle part almost right now, and every few bars he would stop, and murmur something under his breath, and try a line, and shake his head, and try another. I didn't have to ask what he was doing. I could hear words in it: *arrow*, and *forest*, and, I'm fairly sure, *dwarf*.

I went and stood at the window and looked out at the palace, toward the old wing, where the archives were. I thought about a long, dim hall of grown shelves. And at the end of it, an iron door, and a chain, and a lock the size of my fist.

*Records of the Cleansing.* Sealed by the High Houses. Nobody through it in three hundred years.

I'd already learned one thing that day: the only way to win a test is to read the original text. And I had a feeling the most important original text in the Silverwood was behind that door.

Durgan billed me for the arrow. Brakka was already singing. And somewhere in the palace, Lord Venrick was reading old laws by candlelight, looking for the next one.

I had never once hit a target. I'd just learned to read the rubric.

## Interlude: The Arrow of the Forest

*A war-song, composed by Brakka, Sword-Bearer to the Prince, and sung to the Prince's song, loudly, in the guardroom, until the guardroom asked him to stop*

Well, the High Houses came to the archery green\
In the finest of silks that the forest has seen,\
And Lord Venrick stood up with his nose in the air,\
And he split that old arrow. I'll grant him, that's fair.

Now some folks will tell you the Prince couldn't shoot,\
That he hit the barracks door, and the fence, and a boot,\
That the goat moved its breakfast each time that he drew.\
Well, the goat is a liar, and so, friends, are you.

*Then the Prince closed his eyes, and he heard the trees call,*\
*And he shot at the sky, and the sky let it fall,*\
*And it came to the dwarf by the wine table, true,*\
*And the seers stood up, and they said what they knew:*\
*And the Prince did not miss.*\
*The Prince decreed.*

Then the three seers in gray put their old heads together,\
And they spoke of the Veil, and of fate, and of weather,\
And they said, "Now the circle is closed and complete,"\
And the dwarf, who bore the forest's will, has not since found his seat.

So raise up your cups to the Prince and his bow,\
Who has never once hit what he aimed at, I know,\
But the forest decides where an arrow will land,\
And the forest has clearly been holding his hand.

*And the Prince did not miss.*\
*The Prince decreed.*
