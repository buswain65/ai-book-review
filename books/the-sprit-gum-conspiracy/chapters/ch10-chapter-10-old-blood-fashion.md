# Chapter 10: Old Blood Fashion

The first sign was a duke eating toast with his hands.

His name was Duke Halvaren, and he was the oldest and most dignified of the High Lords, a silver-haired elf who had sat through three centuries of court breakfasts without once, as far as anyone knew, touching food with his fingers. The court used tiny silver tongs for everything. They used tongs for grapes. They used tongs for other tongs.

That morning, in the breakfast gallery, I watched Duke Halvaren pick up a slice of toast, look at it, glance down the table at me, and then, with a sort of grim courage, take a bite out of it, holding it in his bare hand. A small gasp went around the gallery. He chewed slowly. He swallowed. Then he took a second bite, with the defiant air of a man who has decided that if he's going to fall, he'll fall with toast.

By the end of breakfast, half the table was eating with their hands.

The second sign was in the corridor afterward, where I passed two young lords from House Calloway leaning against a pillar. One of them caught sight of me, straightened up, and turned to his friend with an expression of great self-consciousness.

"Good morning," he said, "*my dude*."

"And to you," said his friend, very carefully, "*my dude*."

They both looked at me to see how they'd done. I gave them a thumbs-up. They looked at my thumb with deep interest. I would see thumbs-up being given all over the palace by dinnertime.

Then came the tipping.

I should have seen it coming. I'd been tipping twenty percent since my first week, to every servant who brought me anything, because my mother raised me right, and because Durgan's consultation table meant that for the first time since Raleigh, I actually had coins to tip with. And somebody had noticed. And then everybody had noticed.

The problem was that the elves didn't understand what tipping was *for*.

They'd seen the Prince hand coins to people. They knew it was something the Old Blood did. They didn't know that you tip someone for a service, so they started tipping everyone, for everything, all the time. Lady Ithrien tipped her maid for existing, and the maid burst into tears. Lords tipped the guards at every door they walked through. Courtiers tipped each other in the hallways, which led to a sort of endless circular exchange of the same coins that I think may have constituted the Silverwood's first stock market. During the morning Blessing, someone dropped a silver crown into the High Dewkeeper's bowl of sacred dew, and he looked at it for a long moment and then, uncertainly, kept it.

And on the third day, I saw Lord Aestrin of House Calloway walk up to an ancient oak in the palace gardens, consider it, and wedge two coins carefully into a crack in its bark.

I asked him why.

"For the shade, Highness," he said, as if it were obvious.

Within a week, the palace servants were the richest people in the Silverwood. A footman I'd seen around the kitchens bought a house. Brakka, standing at his post outside my door, had coins pressed into his hand by every elf who walked past, which he accepted with an expression of profound confusion and then quietly gave to Thessa, who gave them to the goat, who ate several before we noticed.

I tried, once, to explain that you're only supposed to tip for a service. That made it worse. Now they tipped for *everything* that could be described as a service, including, as far as I could tell, the sun coming up.

\* \* \*

Then Lady Ithrien came to visit me in the new look.

She swept into my sitting room in a gown of pearl-gray silk that had clearly been cut for her usual slender frame and then let out, with tremendous effort, around a large embroidered cushion tied about her middle. Over her shoulders, pinned at the throat with a silver brooch, was a bedsheet. It had been dyed a mottled midnight blue. It was, unmistakably, a copy of my cape.

"Highness," she said, and turned in a slow circle so I could see all of it. "The Old Blood silhouette."

I didn't know what to say. So I said the thing you say to a kid who's just shown you a drawing and you can't tell what it's of.

"Tell me about it."

She told me about it. The cushion was goose-down, which nobody at court would call goose-down anymore; they called it "Prince's fill." The cape was a real bedsheet, from her own bed. She'd dyed it herself, in her own bathtub, to be authentic. "And now," she said, lowering her voice as if confessing something wonderful, "my bathtub is blue too, Highness."

"So is mine," I said.

She clasped her hands to her chest. You'd have thought I'd given her a medal.

By the end of the week, every lady at court had a cushion. Every lord had a bedsheet. Some of the more committed ones had both. The royal tailors, who on my second day had stood in a corner of my room whispering in horror that they only knew how to make one size, came to see me at the end of the week, beaming, with the air of men reporting a great scientific breakthrough.

"Highness," said the head tailor, bowing. "We have a second size now."

"What's it called?"

He looked surprised I needed to ask. "Prince, Highness."

The glitter went next. Every pot of it in the city was bought up in three days, and when it was gone, somebody discovered that the shells of a certain kind of iridescent green beetle, dried and crushed, made a very convincing substitute. Within a week there was a beetle-shell trade in the lower market, with prices printed on boards. I bought some myself, quietly. My own supply was running low, and it was, I'll admit, pretty good glitter.

Then the ears.

I'd had to re-glue my left ear with homemade resin twice that week, and the second time, it had set very slightly crooked: tilted out from my head at a few degrees off true, like a picture frame somebody had brushed against. I'd hoped nobody would notice. Within two days, I passed a lady in the corridor with her own left ear pinned out at exactly the same angle with a little silver clip. By the end of the week there was a fashionable name for the clips. They were called "Aelindors."

And starlight fasting, which the High Houses of the Silverwood had practiced faithfully for four hundred years, collapsed in about ten days. Nobody ate dew anymore. Nobody ate petals. Master Orrin hired forty new cooks, and Thessa told me she'd passed him in the kitchen corridor one morning, simply standing there with a ladle, weeping with joy at the smell of roasting onions.

I watched all of it happen with the horrified fascination of someone watching a pot boil over that he'd personally put on the stove.

I've taught a unit on fads. Pet rocks. Mood rings. Fidget spinners. My seventh graders thought the pet rock was the funniest thing in human history. I'd explained it the way all the textbooks explain it: a fad happens when people want to belong to something, and a symbol comes along that lets them, and it doesn't really matter what the symbol is.

I'd never been the symbol before. I was a guy in a costume, trying not to get killed. I hadn't meant for any of this to happen. I'd just been hungry, and bad at manners, and broke enough to remember what it was like to work for tips.

\* \* \*

Not everyone was happy about it.

Lord Venrick, it turned out, had been waiting since the archery trial for a cause, and the Old Blood craze gave him one. In the second week, he announced the founding of the Society for the Preservation of Starlight Fasting.

Its members wore robes of pale silver, plain and unpadded, with no capes. Their ears were not pinned. They ate dewdrops in public, on purpose, in the corridors and gardens, slowly and with tremendous ceremony, while making pointed eye contact with anyone holding bread. And on the Society's third evening, they held what Venrick called a "fast-in": forty elves in pale silver robes sitting in perfect silence along one side of the Hall of the Eldertree during a banquet, eating nothing, staring at the pies.

It worked, sort of. People noticed. People always notice a protest.

After that, dinner became a war.

The Old Blood faction sat at the tables on the left side of the hall, in their cushions and capes, eating roast boar with their hands. The Starlight faction sat on the right, in silver, with a single dewdrop each, and an air of grim moral superiority. There were glares. There were pointed remarks about "the vulgar appetites of certain Houses," answered by pointed remarks about "certain Houses who look like they haven't eaten since the Purge." Lady Ithrien and Lord Venrick, who sat at the heads of opposing tables, did not speak to each other at all, except once, when she asked if he'd like her to pass the salt, in a tone that suggested she'd like to throw it.

I'd seen this before. Every middle school in America has a cafeteria, and every cafeteria has two tables that hate each other over something nobody can remember. And the one thing a teacher never does, when two tables are at war, is pick a table.

Naturally, both factions wanted me to pick a table.

They came to me separately. Lady Ithrien came on behalf of the Old Blood, glowing, sure I'd take her side, since the whole thing was, after all, about me. Venrick sent a messenger, since he wasn't speaking to me either, with a stiff note informing the Prince that the true traditions of the First Line were plainly austere, and inviting the Prince to "return to the ways of his ancestors." I thought that was a bit rich, coming from a man who'd tried to have me unmade, but I took the point.

At the next banquet, the whole Hall of the Eldertree watched to see what I'd eat.

I ate a dewdrop. Slowly, with ceremony, the Starlight way. The right side of the hall let out a breath of triumph.

Then I ate a large slice of pie, with my hands. The left side of the hall erupted.

Both factions claimed it as a victory. The Starlights said that the Prince had *begun* with the dew, which was clearly the meaningful part. The Old Bloods said that the Prince had *finished* with pie, which was clearly the part that counted. They argued about it for a week. Neither of them ever stopped to consider that a dewdrop and a pie might both just be dinner.

There was something else I noticed, though, that week. Something that wasn't funny.

The Old Blood faction held its biggest banquets in the Hall of Silver Boughs. The Starlight faction held its fast-ins in the Moonlit Gallery. I asked Lord Merrow, the Treasurer, about both halls one afternoon, just making conversation. He went a little pale, the way he always did, and studied a spot on the floor to the left of my boots.

Both halls, he told me, belonged to House Vaunt. Caelith's House. And both were being rented out, that season, at a considerable premium.

Whoever won the war over dinner, Caelith was collecting the rent.

\* \* \*

Durgan, of course, had seen all of this coming before anybody, and he'd been ready.

Old Blood Outfitters opened in the lower market on a Tuesday (it turned out they do have Tuesdays; I'd asked) under a striped awning, with a hand-painted sign. The sign said, in gold letters, *PRINCE-APPROVED*. I had not approved anything. That didn't seem to matter.

The stall sold capes: real bedsheets, dyed midnight blue in vats in the palace laundry, where Durgan was paying the laundry staff in a mixture of crowns and tips. It sold small leather pouches of beetle-shell glitter. It sold wooden swords, painted silver, sized for children. The children of the Silverwood had begun playing a game called "Prince and Geese," in which one child was the Prince and the rest were the geese, and the Prince had to run away. Durgan had the swords on a rack. They sold faster than he could get them painted.

The money was extraordinary. At the end of the first week, Durgan came to my chambers with his ledger, opened it with a flourish, and read out the total: two thousand, one hundred crowns. With the consultation table, it put me under thirty-seven thousand. For the first time since Lord Merrow had walked into my room with that scroll, I could see the bottom of the debt from where I was standing.

Then, in the second week, Durgan added the best seller of all.

They were small glass jars with cork stoppers, full of clear amber resin, and the label on each one said, in Durgan's best lettering, *THE PRINCE'S RESIN. Glue Your Ears Like Royalty.* It was weepwood glue. My recipe. He'd watched me make it in my chambers, and he'd gone and made more. A lot more. The court had decided that pinning your ear crooked with a little silver clip was for amateurs; the truly devoted glued their ears, at an angle, with the same resin the Prince used.

They sold out the first morning. And the next. And the next.

On the fourth day, Thessa came to find me.

She didn't say "pineapple." She didn't drop anything. She just stood in the doorway of my sitting room, very pale, with her satchel clutched against her chest, and said, "Highness. Please come."

She took me to the weepwood grove.

I almost didn't recognize it. Two weeks earlier, it had been a quiet corner of the palace grounds by an old wall: a dozen tall, straight trees with bark like hammered silver, and little beads of gold resin glowing at the places where branches had broken. Now the ground under the trees was trampled to mud and littered with discarded buckets. The trunks had been hacked open, all of them, top to bottom, with deep, ragged axe-cuts, so that the resin would run faster. The bark hung off them in long silver strips like peeling skin. And every one of the trees was weeping: thick gold resin running down from a hundred wounds and pooling, uselessly, in the mud at its roots.

Thessa walked to the nearest tree, the first one she'd ever shown me, and knelt down in the mud in front of it, and put her hand flat against its torn trunk. She didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she said, very quietly, the thing she'd told me the first day, as if she were saying it to the tree:

"You don't take more than it can spare."

I stood there and thought about the banquet, on my second night in the Silverwood. I thought about Lady Ithrien, explaining with relish what the Blight had done. *They cut down the Eldertree's sisters for firewood,* she'd said. *They burned whole forests.*

I thought about the tapestry over my bed: elves in silver armor, driving a horde out of a burning wood.

The elves had hated humans for three hundred years for doing exactly this. And they'd done it in two weeks, to a grove in their own palace, so they could glue their ears on at an angle. To look like me. To look like the Old Blood. To look, if they'd only known it, like a human.

On the way back, we passed through the lower market. And I stopped in front of the striped awning of Old Blood Outfitters, and looked at the capes, and the glitter, and the wooden swords, and the little glass jars with my name on them. And for a second, I wasn't in the Silverwood at all. I was in the dealers' room of the Radisson in downtown Raleigh, standing in front of the *Elderlight* booth, looking at a banner the length of a minivan and a stack of twenty-five-dollar tote bags.

I had become the tote bag.

I went and found Durgan that afternoon and told him the resin was done. No more jars.

He looked at me like I'd announced I was going to burn down the market. "Highness. It's the *best seller*."

"I know."

"It's *paying off your invoice*."

"I know."

We argued about it for an hour. In the end, we compromised. The capes stayed. The glitter stayed, since beetle shells grow back. The wooden swords stayed, because I couldn't bring myself to take swords away from children playing Prince and Geese. But the resin went, all of it, that day.

The debt slowed down again. And there was one other cost, which I didn't mention to Durgan. The grove was where my own glue came from. With the trees in that condition, I was going to have to find another source, and soon, or start rationing.

I decided I'd worry about that tomorrow.

Thessa went back to the grove that evening, by herself, with a bag of moss and a pot of something she'd mixed up from her satchel. When I asked what she was doing, she said she was going to bind the cuts. She said it the way you'd say you were going to sit with a friend in the hospital.

\* \* \*

The day Silvaine's crystal came back from repair, I woke up before dawn, went out to the balcony, and checked the bottle.

The water had climbed three lines overnight. It was the biggest jump I'd seen since the procession. Rain was coming, and not a little of it. It would be here by noon.

And two weeks earlier, at my consultation table, I had looked Silvaine of House Thistlevane in her hawk-gray eyes and told her that on the day her crystal came back, the skies would be clear.

I'd had no way of knowing. A barometer can't see two weeks ahead. I'd said it because she frightened me, and I wanted her to go away. And now the Weather Prophet was about to be wrong for the very first time, in front of the one person in the Silverwood who was keeping a ledger.

I sat down at my desk and thought about it for about ten minutes. Then I wrote a note.

> *The forest advises the Inquisitor to carry her crystal in a closed case today.*

I folded it, sealed it with a drop of candle wax, and woke Thessa. She'd never run an errand for me alone before. She took the note in both hands, the way you'd carry something that might break, and went out into the gray morning with her charms jingling. She came back an hour later, a little out of breath, and reported that the Inquisitor's servant had accepted it at the door. That was all. The Inquisitor had not come out.

At noon, almost to the minute, the sky opened.

It was a big one, as big as the storm on procession day: the same sudden bruise-green darkness, the same solid gray sheets of water hammering off the stone. I stayed well back from the windows. Brakka stood beside me, watching the courtyard below.

"Watch," he said. "I told you."

And they came. Out of every door and archway around the courtyard, the elves of the Silverwood came pouring out into the downpour. They weren't sheltering from it. They were running *into* it, with their faces turned up, laughing. Within a minute, the courtyard was full of them, dancing in the rain: spinning with their arms out, their silver hair plastered flat, their bathtub-dyed bedsheet capes soaked dark and swinging heavily around their legs, their padded gowns sagging. Some of them were holding hands in long, swaying lines. Some of them were tipping each other, still, out of pure habit, pressing wet coins into wet hands.

I watched them for a long time. I'd never seen anything so happy. I'd also never been so glad to be indoors.

At about one o'clock, there was a knock on my door.

Silvaine of House Thistlevane stood on the threshold, soaked to the skin.

Water ran off the end of her nose. Her black and silver robes clung to her. Her braid, which I had never seen with a single hair out of place, had come loose at the end and was dripping steadily onto my floor. Tucked under her arm was a flat case of dark, oiled leather, about the size of a book.

"You told me," she said, very evenly, "clear skies, Highness."

The corridor behind her was crowded with courtiers sheltering from the rain, the ones who hadn't gone out to dance: Old Blood loyalists in damp cushions and dripping capes, with their left ears pinned out crooked. Every one of them had gone silent, watching.

"I told you," I said, "that it wouldn't rain on your crystal."

Silvaine looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked down at the leather case under her arm. She ran one finger along its seam. It was completely dry.

She considered. I could see her considering. It was like watching a judge reread a statute.

"Technically," Silvaine said at last, "correct."

And she looked at me then, for the first time since I'd met her, with something that wasn't suspicion. It wasn't warmth, either. Silvaine didn't do warmth. But it was close to respect. It was the look of one person who reads the fine print, recognizing another one.

Then she opened the case.

She did it right there in the corridor, before she'd stepped into my room, with all those Old Blood courtiers pressed around her: padded and glittering and crooked-eared, the court's most devoted imitators of the Prince. The crystal lay on a bed of black velvet, clear and new and perfectly still.

It stayed dark.

I saw her notice. I saw her eyes move from the crystal to the courtiers, and back again. Then she stepped across my threshold, into my sitting room.

The crystal blazed.

It went off like a flare, brighter than I had ever seen it, brighter than in the Hall of Crowns, so bright that the courtiers in the corridor behind her threw up their hands and turned away. It lit the whole room blue. It threw her shadow across the floor and up the far wall, enormous.

Silvaine stood very still, holding it, and looked at it for a long time.

She had told me, in the Hall of Crowns, that the Old Blood overloaded her crystal. I remembered her saying it. I remembered her reasoning it out, step by step, like a math problem on a board. And she'd just walked through a corridor full of the most Old Blood-looking people in the Silverwood, and the crystal hadn't so much as flickered.

She didn't say any of that. She didn't say anything at all for almost a minute. Then she closed the case, and the blue light went out, and she looked up at me with no expression whatsoever.

"This crystal," she said, "has been repaired incorrectly. Or it has been near you too long, Highness. I'll requisition another. Grown fresh, from the seed." She tucked the case back under her arm. "One that you have never been near."

She gave me the correct bow for a Prince, precise to the degree, and walked away down the corridor, dripping, through the crowd of crooked-eared courtiers, who parted for her without a word.

I didn't move for a long time after she'd gone.

She hadn't said "overloaded" this time. I'd noticed that. I think she'd noticed it too.

Below my window, four hundred elves in bathtub-dyed bedsheets danced in the rain, tipping each other twenty percent.

I'd come to the Silverwood dressed as an elf. Now the elves were dressing as me.

And somewhere in the palace, they were growing a new crystal. One I had never been near.
