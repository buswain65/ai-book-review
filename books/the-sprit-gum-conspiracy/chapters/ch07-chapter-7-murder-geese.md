# Chapter 7: Murder-Geese

In every RPG, your first quest is rats in a cellar, and the first monster is the easy one. Mine was geese. Verathis had not played those games.

The manor of House Velloran stood on the far side of the palace grounds, grown out of three enormous trees that had been coaxed, over a few centuries, into leaning toward each other like old friends sharing a secret. It was very beautiful. It also, as we got closer, honked.

Not the house itself. The sound came from somewhere underneath it, faint and muffled and furious, rising up through the little vents in the stone foundations like a brass band that had been buried alive.

Lord Thandril met us at the cellar door.

He was holding a lantern in one hand. The other hand was wrapped in a bandage the size of a boxing glove. He had the pale, hollow, slightly twitchy look of a man who had been honked at, all night and every night, for a week.

"Highness," he breathed, bowing so low he nearly lost the lantern. "Thank the stars. Thank all the stars. You've come about the..." He stopped. His mouth worked. "The birds."

"The geese?" said Durgan.

Lord Thandril flinched as if he'd been slapped. Behind me, Brakka made a low rumbling sound, which I recognized as the first tremor of an aftershock. Thessa went red to the tips of her tusks.

"Please, Ritualist," Lord Thandril whispered. "There is a lady present."

This was a reference to Thessa, who looked as though she would like very much to be absent.

Since the Royal Greeting, I understood, the word for those particular birds had become unsayable in polite elven company. Which was a problem for Lord Thandril, because his wine cellar was full of them, and he could no longer describe his own emergency out loud.

He did his best. Over the next few minutes, as he explained the situation, I counted him calling them "the birds," then "the creatures," then "the honking ones," then "the feathered unpleasantness," and finally, with a haunted look toward the cellar door, just "*them*."

They had appeared a week ago, he said. Nobody knew how. The cellar had only one door, this one, and it had been locked for a century. "The estate came to my grandfather in the Grants, after the Purge," he added, with a fond little smile. "Lovely old place. Nobody knows who built the cellars." One morning his steward had gone down for a bottle of the four-hundred-year vintage, and come back up without the bottle, without his hat, and without the ability to speak for several hours. Lord Thandril had gone down himself the next day, armed with a broom. He held up the bandaged hand. That was how that had gone.

"And, Highness," he added, lowering his voice even further, "I must ask for your discretion. If it were known at court that House Velloran was overrun by..." He gestured helplessly at the door. "*Them*. In the current climate. Lady Ithrien would dine on it for a century."

Durgan's eyes lit up. He reached into his robe and produced, with a flourish, a form.

"Form 22-D, my lord," he said. "Non-Disclosure of Domestic Fowl Incidents. Standard Guild confidentiality. Sign here, and here, and the matter never leaves this hallway."

Lord Thandril signed it with tremendous relief, in two places, and initialed a third. Durgan blotted it, folded it, tucked it away, and handed him a second, smaller piece of paper. It was a bill.

He held up a heavy velvet purse and shook it, so that it clinked. Two hundred crowns.

I looked at the purse. I looked at the cellar door, which had just shuddered on its hinges as something on the other side hit it hard.

"Your secret's safe with us," I said.

\* \* \*

Lord Thandril unlocked the cellar door, opened it about an inch, and fled.

The four of us stood at the top of a long stone staircase that curved down into the dark. From the bottom came a sound like a hundred bicycle horns arguing. The smell drifting up the stairs was wine, mostly. Wine, and something underneath it that I can only describe as goose.

I turned to face my party.

I've run exactly one group project as a teacher, and I've played a lot of online raids, and I've learned the same lesson from both. Before you go in, everybody needs to know their job. Otherwise you get one person doing everything and three people standing around.

"Okay," I said. "Roles."

They looked at me blankly. I pressed on.

"Brakka. You're the tank."

"What is a tank, Highness?"

"You go in front. You're big, you're armored, and you make them attack you instead of us."

Brakka considered this, then nodded gravely. "I have been doing this my whole life," he said. "I did not know it had a name."

"Thessa. You're the healer."

Thessa's eyes went wide. "Highness, I can't heal."

"Can you clean a wound?"

She thought about it. "...Yes?"

"Close enough. Durgan, you're the mage."

Durgan drew himself up to his full height, which was about level with my belt pouch. "I am a High Ritualist of the Seventh Circle."

"Can you do anything fast?"

"I can perform a Banishing," he said with dignity.

"How long does it take?"

"That depends," said Durgan, "on the moon."

I decided not to ask. "And I'm damage." I drew the foam sword.

There was a pause. All three of them looked at the foam sword. Brakka, in particular, looked at it for a long time, with the expression of a man who has spent thirty-one years around weapons and is trying to be polite.

Then Durgan sighed, swung the enormous pack off his back, and began untying something from its side.

"Highness," he said. "With the greatest respect. Take the pan."

He held it out with both hands: a cast-iron frying pan, black and heavy and seasoned by what smelled like decades of ham. I took it. I felt the weight of it settle into my hand.

And I want you to understand something. In my seventh playthrough of *Elderlight III*, on a bet with Dev, I beat the entire game using nothing but the frying pan you can buy from a traveling merchant in the first village. Every fight. Every dungeon. The final boss, the Wyrm of Ashfall, on hard difficulty. It took me forty-one hours, and I unlocked an achievement called *Breakfast of Champions*, and it remains the proudest moment of my life.

I had never held a real one in battle. But I knew this weapon.

I gave it a test swing. It made a low, satisfying *whoom*.

"All right," I said, and for the first time since I'd arrived in Verathis, I felt completely confident. "Let's go."

\* \* \*

It went wrong immediately.

The cellar of House Velloran was enormous: a long, vaulted room of pale stone lined with wine racks, lit dimly by the lantern Lord Thandril had abandoned on the top step. Half the racks had been knocked over. Barrels lay broken on the floor, and the floor was a shallow lake of four-hundred-year-old wine.

And standing in the wine, and on the barrels, and on top of the racks, were about forty geese.

They were big. They were gray. And they were, I realized as the nearest one swayed slightly and hiccupped, drunk. They'd been living in a wine cellar for a week. They'd been drinking the vintage. Every one of them had the bleary, belligerent look of a man at a wedding who has just decided he can take the groom.

Forty heads turned toward us at once.

"Brakka!" I said. "Tank! Go!"

Brakka did not go.

He was standing on the bottom step with one foot hovering over the wine, and he wasn't moving. I looked up at him. The great scarred face, which I'd seen go through war and laughter and lunch without so much as a flicker, had gone a shade paler, which I wouldn't have thought orc skin could do.

"Highness," he said quietly. "I must tell you about my ear."

"Now?"

"I was seven," said Brakka. "It was a very large goose."

The whole thing fell into place at once: the shaking shoulders in the Hall of Crowns, the aftershocks on the walk home. He hadn't just been laughing at a dirty joke. Somebody had shouted a dirty joke about his oldest enemy in front of the entire Concordance.

I didn't laugh. Every class has a kid with a fear that sounds funny to everybody else, and the worst thing you can do is be the one who laughs.

"New plan," I said. "You're the rear guard. Nothing gets past you from behind."

Brakka's shoulders came down an inch. "Strategy," he said, with deep gratitude, and planted himself on the bottom stair like a door.

The geese charged.

I did what you do in any boss fight: I looked for the weak point. In *Elderlight*, every monster has one. It glows a little, or there's a crack in the armor, or it hesitates after a big attack. I watched the lead goose barrel toward me through the wine, and I looked for its weak point, and I want to tell you clearly what I found.

There wasn't one. It was all beak and spite.

Behind me, Durgan had dropped to his knees and was drawing a circle on the dry top step in chalk, chanting in a low, rumbling voice, the Banishing. Three geese immediately waddled over to attack the chalk. Durgan swatted them off with his staff without breaking rhythm, finished the circle, raised both arms, and bellowed the final word.

There was a flash, and a smell of burnt hair. When the smoke cleared, there was something standing in the middle of the chalk circle.

It was a goat.

It was a small brown goat, with one bent horn and a look of mild surprise. It was chewing something. It looked at us. It kept chewing.

Durgan stared at it for a long moment. "That's my goat," he said faintly.

It was. It was the sacrificial goat from the summoning, the one on his invoice. *Declined to participate; still at large.* Somehow, his Banishing had reached into the world and fetched it home.

"Goat recovery efforts," Durgan whispered, "successful." And he took a stub of pencil from behind his ear and made a note.

Meanwhile, six geese had backed Thessa into a corner. I saw her go rigid. I saw her hands begin to glow.

"*PINEAPPLE!*"

I dove behind a barrel. The light went off like a flashbulb, and the air smelled like clean laundry and lightning.

When I looked up, the dozen geese nearest Thessa had changed. Every feather was spotless, blinding white, glowing faintly in the lantern light. They looked like they had been dipped in starlight. They looked, honestly, beautiful.

They stood still for one moment. Then they all turned toward Thessa again, and they were angrier than before. It was like watching someone come out of a makeover ready to fight.

So I did the only thing left. I raised Skillet, the Iron Moon, as I would later learn to call it, and I waded into the wine.

For eleven glorious seconds, it worked. The first goose came at me, and I swung, and the pan rang like a church bell. The goose went down in the wine with an astonished honk. The second one I caught on the backswing. The third one I fended off with a sort of frying-pan parry that I'd only ever done with a controller. I was the Breakfast of Champions. I was unstoppable.

Then the other thirty-seven noticed.

\* \* \*

Two minutes later, the four of us, plus the goat, were backed up against the far wall of the cellar with our shoulders pressed to the wine racks, and forty geese were advancing on us in a slow, swaying, drunken semicircle.

The bottles behind my head rattled every time one of them honked. Brakka had left his post on the stairs to stand in front of Thessa, which I noticed, and which I knew had cost him. Durgan was holding his staff in front of him like a broom. The goat was eating the hem of my cape.

I was out of video game ideas. There was no weak point. The tank was afraid of the monsters, the healer's spell made them stronger, and the mage's big move had summoned livestock. If this was a raid, it was a wipe.

So I stopped thinking like a gamer and started thinking like a teacher.

It's the same rule that saved me in the summoning chamber: find the one everyone else watches.

I watched the geese.

It took a few seconds. Then I saw it: every time one of them hesitated, it glanced toward the same spot. On top of a broken barrel near the middle of the cellar, apart from the others, stood the biggest goose I had ever seen. He was dark gray, nearly black, with one wing held slightly crooked where it had been torn and healed badly. He wasn't honking. He didn't need to. He was just watching, with small, flat, intelligent eyes.

He looked like a Kevin.

Every class has a Kevin: the kid who never speaks first, never throws the eraser himself, and is somehow behind everything.

"Thessa," I said, without taking my eyes off him. "Do you still have the bread?"

She'd packed lunch. Of course she had. With trembling hands, she pulled a loaf of Master Orrin's dark bread out of her satchel and passed it to me.

Kevin's eyes moved to the bread. All forty heads followed his.

*Feed the spear,* I thought, *and the spear stays sharp.*

That was when I noticed the draft.

It was cold and steady, coming from somewhere behind a collapsed wine rack in the corner to my left. When I leaned over, I could see it: a hole in the cellar's back wall, about three feet high, where the stones had crumbled away into darkness. That was how they'd gotten in. Not through the locked door. Through the wall.

I tore off a chunk of bread and tossed it toward Kevin's barrel. He didn't move. Then I tossed another piece halfway to the hole. Then another, just in front of it. A trail.

Kevin hopped down off the barrel. He walked, unhurried, to the first piece of bread, and ate it. Then the second. The flock began to turn, all of them, and follow him.

It was working. It was actually working. Kevin reached the last piece of bread, right at the mouth of the hole, and ate it, and I felt a surge of the most enormous professional pride.

Then Kevin turned around and looked at me. More specifically, he looked at the rest of the loaf in my hands.

He honked, once.

Forty geese turned with him, and every one of them looked at the bread too. And at me.

I realized, with total clarity, what I'd done. I'd shown the leader where the food was. And I was holding the food.

"Okay," I said. "Everybody. Through the hole."

\* \* \*

We went through the hole in the order of who was closest to it, which meant Durgan first, then the goat, then Thessa, then Brakka, who had to go through on his hands and knees and took a good deal of the wall with him. I went last, holding the bread over my head like a torch, with forty geese an arm's length behind me.

On the other side was a tunnel.

It was low and narrow and completely dark, and it ran away from the cellar at a slight downward slope. We ran down it, bent nearly double, with the sound of the flock filling the darkness behind us, a roar of wings and honking and splashing feet. I couldn't see anything. I could hear Durgan swearing up ahead, and the goat's hooves clattering, and Brakka saying "Strategy" to himself, very quietly, over and over, like a prayer.

Then Thessa, somewhere in front of me, panicked again.

"*Pineapple!* Pineapple, pineapple, *pineapple!*"

I threw myself flat. The tunnel lit up in a white flash, then went dark, then flashed again, and again. She was too scared to stop. It was like running through a thunderstorm, or a dance club, and in those flashes, frozen and perfectly clear, I saw the tunnel.

It wasn't elven.

Everything in the Silverwood palace was grown, not built: tree roots woven through living stone, curves that looked like they'd happened on their own. This tunnel was made of brick. Small, square, red-brown bricks, each one laid by hand, with lines of crumbling mortar between them. The ceiling was a row of low, round arches, the kind I'd seen in pictures of old European wine cellars in a textbook.

Flash. A rusted iron corkscrew, hanging from a nail driven into the wall.

Flash. A wooden shelf, rotted almost to nothing.

Flash. Low down near the floor, at about the height of a child's hand, something scratched into the mortar. Little stick figures. Four of them, a big one and a big one and two small ones, standing in a row, holding hands.

Flash. Each of them had a round little circle on each side of its head. Round ears.

Then the light ahead of us wasn't Thessa's anymore. It was daylight.

We burst out of the tunnel mouth into a narrow, grassy ravine behind House Velloran's estate, blinking and stumbling in the sun. I made it about ten feet and fell flat on my back in the grass, still clutching the bread.

The geese poured out of the tunnel behind us. All forty of them, in a gray and white flood.

And then they stopped. They looked up. For the first time in a week, they could see the sky.

One by one, they spread their wings, and took off. They rose over the ravine in a great ragged V, honking at the treetops, and wheeled away over the forest, and were gone.

Kevin was last. He circled once, low, directly over me. He looked down. He honked once, in a tone I can only describe as personal.

Then he followed his flock.

We lay there in the grass for a while: four people and a goat. Nobody said anything. The goat found Durgan's satchel and began to eat a form.

Lord Thandril's steward found us about an hour later. Lord Thandril himself came down to the ravine to pay us, still holding his lantern in broad daylight, and pressed the velvet purse into my hands, and thanked me over and over, and asked me one last time, very quietly, never to tell a single soul.

I didn't tell him about the tunnel. I'm not sure why. Maybe because I didn't think he knew it was there. Maybe because I didn't think he'd want to.

The goat followed us home. Nobody invited it. Durgan, after some thought, entered it in his ledger as an asset.

The geese were gone, the cellar was clear, and we'd won by running away. Brakka was already composing the ballad. In his version, we didn't run.

Two hundred crowns. Thirty-nine thousand, eight hundred to go.

But that night, I kept thinking about the little round-eared family scratched into the mortar. Somebody had lived under Lord Thandril's wine cellar once. Somebody had built it.

## Interlude: The Siege of the Cellar

*A war-song, composed by Brakka, Sword-Bearer to the Prince, and sung by him to anyone who would sit still*

In the house of Velloran, deep under the stone,\
A demon horde gathered, a thousand of wings,\
With beaks made of iron and hearts made of bone,\
And hunger, and fury, and other bad things.

Then down came the Prince with the Iron Moon high,\
Great Skillet, the ancient, that rings like a bell,\
And the demons fell back with a terrible cry,\
And the mountain stood firm, as mountains do well.

*(The mountain stood still. As a mountain must.)*

The dwarf, who was also there, called to the sky,\
And the sky sent a spirit-beast, horned and wise.\
The maid of the Ashtusk called white fire on high,\
And it made the demons holy. It did not make them nice.

Then the Prince spied the king of the horde on his throne,\
And offered him bread, as a true king would do,\
And he led the whole host through the dark and the stone,\
And out to the daylight, the sky, and the blue.

Some say that he ran. They are liars and fools.\
He moved very quickly. That's different. That's rules.

*And the Prince did not run.*\
*The Prince led.*
