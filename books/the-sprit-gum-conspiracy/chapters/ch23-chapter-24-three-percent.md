# Chapter 24: Three Percent

The first thing the elves did after peace, of course, was start a new trend.

Within a month, half the High Houses of the Silverwood were gluing on round ears.

Lady Ithrien was first. Naturally. She turned up at the Hearthhold meeting hall one morning, about ten days after the treaty was signed, wearing a pair of small, round, slightly lumpy ears glued over the tips of her own, made of beeswax and dyed kidskin. She'd had them made specially. She'd worn them all the way north from the High Houses' camp in a covered litter, so that nobody would see them until she was ready. When she took her hood down in the doorway, the whole hall went silent.

The left one was crooked. On purpose.

"Well?" said Lady Ithrien, turning her head from side to side so we could admire them. "What do you think? Durgan says they're called *Delaneys*."

I didn't know what to say. Ruth Ashgrove, sitting beside me at the long table, made a very small sound into her nettle tea that might have been a cough.

By the end of the week, every lady in the Old Blood faction had a pair. By the end of the month, so did half the young lords of the High Houses, who wore them crooked, because it was more authentic. Durgan couldn't make them fast enough. Old Blood Outfitters had moved its entire operation into a borrowed shed at the edge of Hearthhold, where he sat from dawn to dusk at a long table with a pile of beeswax, a stack of dyed kidskin, and a waiting list that ran to four pages. He glued them on with pine pitch from Hearthhold's forest, harvested properly, one notch at a time. Thessa had personally inspected the trees.

I'd once watched the elves of the Silverwood strip a weepwood grove bare to look more like me, when they thought I was one of them. Now they were gluing on round ears to look more like me, now that they knew I wasn't. I'm not sure what that says about trends. I'm fairly sure it isn't anything good. But I'll take it.

Other things changed too, in that month.

The first elven foresters arrived from the Silverwood: a dozen of them, tall and silver-haired and very solemn, carrying notebooks. They'd come to learn how to plant a sapling beside every stump. Hearthhold's foresters took them up into the valley on their first morning and showed them the notched stakes, and the young trees growing up beside the old cuts, and the elves stood among them for a long time without saying anything. One of them, an old forester with bark-brown hands, knelt beside a stake and touched the notches, the way Thessa had, and stayed there until the others went on without him.

Caelith was waiting for his trial. He'd been entitled to one, under Article Nine, before the assembled Houses, and I'd insisted on it. Not because I felt sorry for him. Because it was the law, and because the whole point of the law is that it applies to everybody, especially the people you'd most like it not to. He was kept in a comfortable tent under guard, and was said to be reading a great deal. Captain Isaura commanded the guard. She had been asked, by the assembled Houses, to command the peacekeepers of the treaty. She'd said yes, plainly, the way she said everything.

Silvaine had started a new ledger. It had one entry in it. I'd seen her, more than once, sitting alone at the end of the long table with the ledger open in front of her, just looking at it.

Venrick had founded the Society for the Preservation of Commas. It had three members. They met weekly.

The goat ate a draft of the treaty, the second-to-last one, from the table in the meeting hall while everyone was at lunch, and was not punished, because it was now officially a royal asset, and because nobody wanted to be the one to tell Durgan.

Pudding lived in the warm shed with the children of Hearthhold and had stopped pretending to recognize me.

And Corvel's painting, *Marcus Delaney, in the Rain*, hung on the wall of the meeting hall, over the hearth, where the people of Hearthhold could see it every day. He'd offered to repaint the parts the rain had run. Ruth had told him to leave them.

\* \* \*

Durgan found me on the wall at the mouth of the ravine, near the end of that month, late in the afternoon.

I'd taken to going up there in the evenings. It was the place Ruth had stood the night before the trial, looking south at the fires. There weren't any fires now. Just the long gray-green valley falling away toward the wastes, and the sky turning gold over it, and the first stars. The snow had started melting off the terraces. You could smell the ground underneath.

Durgan climbed up the steps with his ledger under one arm, puffing, and stood beside me for a while without saying anything, which for Durgan was unusual enough that I turned and looked at him.

He had a form in his hand. He was holding it the way you'd hold something you weren't sure you wanted to give someone.

"Highness," he said. Everyone still called me that. I'd stopped trying to make them stop. "There's something you should know. Professionally." He cleared his throat. "About the summoning."

I waited.

"It can be reversed," said Durgan.

The wind moved on the wall. I didn't say anything.

"Not by just anyone," Durgan went on, quickly, as if he wanted to get it all out at once. "It requires a High Ritualist of the Seventh Circle. With a reinstated license. Which I have." He paused. "Pending." Another pause. "And it can only be done at the new moon. And the summoned party has to consent to it, in writing, of their own free will. It's in the rites. It's always been in the rites. The Guild just doesn't advertise it, because most summoned parties are, ah, heroes, and heroes are expensive to replace."

I looked at the form in his hand. "Form 12-B waived the return window," I said. "I'm non-refundable. You told the whole court."

"Form 12-B waived the *thirty-day* return window," said Durgan, with the dignity of a man who has read every footnote. "For *returns*. For defective goods. This isn't a return." He held the form out to me. "This is Form 12-C. Voluntary Repatriation of a Summoned Party. Entirely different department."

I took it.

It was filled in. All of it. My name, spelled correctly. The date of the summoning. The place of return: *the Sovereign Crystal Grand Ballroom, Radisson, downtown Raleigh, as described by the summoned party.* He must have been listening, all those nights at the fire. Everything was filled in except for one line at the bottom, which was blank, for my signature.

There was also, pinned to the back with a silver ear-clip, an invoice.

"Standard rate," said Durgan, not looking at me. "With a discount. For services rendered."

I stood there on the wall with Form 12-C in my hands for a long time.

I thought about Dev. My roommate, in his cardboard robot suit, who'd shown up at the convention for moral support and was probably still trying to figure out where I'd gone. I thought about Mr. Pickles, the fish, who was almost certainly dead by now, unless Dev had remembered to feed him, which was unlikely. I thought about my rent, which was three months late, and my landlord, who was not a forgiving man. I thought about the five hundred dollars I'd never won.

I thought about Mrs. Okafor, at the back of my classroom with her clipboard, and the notes she'd have for me about the last three months. I thought about Jaylen Price and his eraser lanyard. And I thought about a nine-year-old girl named Lily, in a homemade costume in a convention check-in line, who'd looked up at me and told me I was the best elf there.

Home. It was real. It was still there. And all I had to do was sign.

And I thought about the note in my belt pouch. The one in blue ballpoint, on a page torn out of a spiral notebook, signed *E.M.* The man who had sat at Quillon's desk forty years ago, and asked the same questions I'd asked, and been stopped by the same iron door. Who had found his way home, somehow, and then spent the rest of his life telling the whole story to millions of people in a way that none of them would ever believe.

I didn't know how he'd done it. Whether he'd signed a Form 12-C of his own. Whether he'd had a Durgan. Whether he'd wanted to go, or whether he'd stood on a wall somewhere like this one and looked at a valley and thought about it for a long time first.

Someday, I'd find out. I was sure of that.

Not yet.

\* \* \*

I didn't sign it.

Not because I didn't want to go home. I did. I wanted to go home so badly that, standing on that wall with the form in my hand, I could almost smell the coffee in the faculty lounge and hear the bell. I wanted to see Dev's face. I wanted to apologize to my landlord. I wanted to call my mom.

But I'd looked down from the wall, while I was standing there, at the village below. At the meeting hall with its carved lintel. At the long, low greenhouses, and the lemon tree, and the children of Hearthhold running between the houses in the evening light, with a pack of elven foresters' children chasing them, and a couple of Okko's younger brothers, who'd come north with the orc companies and decided to stay, and a wolf-folk pup who'd attached himself to Davy and followed him everywhere.

They'd been playing Leaf-Lords and Hearthholders, three weeks ago. Now they were just playing.

And not one of them had a school.

There was nobody to teach them. Not the old stories, the honest ones, the way Hearthhold taught its children both halves of the Reckoning. Not the archives, the two drafts, the maps. Not the four questions. Nobody to sit them down at the same table, the elves and the orcs and the humans and the wolf-folk, and teach them to ask *who made this, and when, and why, and what's missing*. Nobody to teach them that history usually has more than one draft, and that the most important thing you can learn is to go and look for the other one.

I'm a teacher. A student teacher. Unpaid. I pay tuition to do it. But a teacher. And I'd never in my life seen a class that needed one more.

So I folded up Form 12-C, carefully, along the creases, and put it in my belt pouch. Next to E.M.'s note, and Wenna's arrow, and the empty travel-size bottle of spirit gum I'd carried through a portal in a hotel ballroom in Raleigh, three months and a whole world ago.

Durgan watched me do it. He didn't say anything. But I saw his shoulders come down about an inch, the way they do when someone has just been told a bill doesn't need to be paid today.

"One school year," I said. "Then we'll see."

"I'll keep the invoice on file," said Durgan gruffly.

That night, I took out my lesson plan. The same sheet of royal parchment I'd started on my first night in the Silverwood, which by now was soft as cloth and covered in crossings-out and three different colors of ink and pencil and a resin stain shaped like Ohio. I turned past *Objective 1: Don't die*, and *Objective 2: Stop the war*, and *Do something*, and *Objective 3: Choose the hour*, and *Still alive. It was Friday.*

I turned to a fresh page.

Under *Objective*, I wrote one word. *Teach.*

Under *Assessment*, I wrote: *We'll see.*

\* \* \*

Ruth Ashgrove gave me the meeting hall for a classroom.

I'd asked her for a corner of it, maybe a couple of benches by the hearth, in the mornings. She'd looked at me over her nettle tea for a long moment, the weather-measuring look, and then said, "You'll need the whole thing," and that was that. In the afternoons it would go back to being a meeting hall. In the mornings, from the first day of spring on, it was a school.

The first day of school was a Monday. They do have Mondays.

I got there before dawn, the way I would have on Earth, and set up the room. I'd made a seating chart the night before, on the back of an old draft of the treaty that the goat had somehow missed. I'd mixed everyone. Every bench had a human kid from Hearthhold, and an elven kid from the foresters' families, and an orc, and wherever I could manage it, a wolf-folk pup. I put Davy in the front row, right in the middle, next to a small, solemn elven girl named Seraphel, whose father was the old forester with the bark-brown hands. They looked at each other with deep suspicion. That was fine. That's how every good seating chart starts.

On every desk, I taped an index card. I'd written them out myself, by lamplight, all thirty-one of them. Each one said the same four things.

> *Who made this?*
>
> *When did they make it?*
>
> *Why did they make it?*
>
> *What's missing?*

And at the front of the room, on the long table, where my desk would have been, I laid out the first lesson. Side by side. The Book of the Reckoning, open to its first page, in the cramped hand of someone who had sat in a cave at the top of this valley three hundred years ago and asked *why*. And next to it, the two drafts of the report on the Burning of the Sister Trees, borrowed from Silvaine, who had made me sign three forms to get them and was standing in the back of the room to make sure I gave them back.

Two stories about the same war. Both true. Both incomplete. That was the first lesson. It's always the first lesson.

They came in at sunrise. Thirty-one children, in a jostling, shoving, chattering crowd, with mittens and snow on their boots and their breakfasts still on their faces, the way every class comes in on the first day of school in every world, I'd guess. They found their seats on the chart, after a lot of arguing. They looked at the index cards. They looked at each other. They looked at me.

And I stood up at the front of the room, in my green tunic with the silver vines, without the cape, without the wig, without the ears, with my own short brown hair, and said what I'd been practicing for four years of college to say.

"Good morning. My name is Mr. Delaney. I'll be your teacher."

Thirty-one faces stared back at me. Then Davy raised his hand.

"Are you still the Prince?" he asked.

I thought about it.

"On weekends," I said.

That seemed to satisfy everyone.

Thessa taught in the afternoons. Forestry: how to read a tree, how much it can spare, how to plant two for every one you take. The children followed her up into the valley in a long, straggling line, and came back at dusk muddy and exhausted and full of opinions about pine trees. She was a much better teacher than I was. I told her so. She told me I was wrong, which, coming from Thessa, was the highest compliment I'd ever received.

And Brakka, after a great deal of serious discussion, accepted the position of Eraser Monitor.

There weren't any erasers. There were slates, and rags to wipe them with. So, technically, he was Rag Monitor. But I told him about Jaylen Price, and the lanyard, and how important the job was, and he took it with a gravity I've never seen anyone bring to anything. He stood at the back of the classroom every morning with his arms folded and the rags arranged in a perfectly straight row on the bench beside him, and when a child needed one, he would walk over and hand it to them, solemnly, like a knight presenting a sword.

Nobody ever misused a rag in that classroom. Not once.

That evening, Hearthhold held a feast in the meeting hall to celebrate the first day of school. There was bread, and butter (Wenna had made sure of it), and roasted root vegetables from the greenhouses, and a whole pig, and a pot of something Master Orrin had sent north in a sealed crock with a note that just said *For the Prince's feast. Weeping.* There were lemons from the lemon tree. There was even a dewdrop, on a single moonlit plate, set at the end of the table for anyone observing the Starlight. Venrick ate it with tremendous dignity, and then, when he thought no one was looking, had some pig.

And after dinner, Brakka stood up on a bench, cleared his throat with a sound like a cart going over a bridge, and announced that he had written a new ballad. His last, he said. About the rain.

It was set, of course, to my dad's old road-trip song. Everyone in the hall knew the tune by then. And when he got to the end of the first verse, and the refrain came around, the whole room sang it with him, orcs and elves and humans and wolf-folk and dwarves, every one of them, even Silvaine, very quietly, at the back.

It was a new refrain. I'd never heard it before. I won't write it all out here; Brakka wrote it down himself, afterward, and it's better in his handwriting. But I'll tell you how it ended.

*And the Prince was not an elf. The Prince led.*

\* \* \*

Late that night, after the feast, when the meeting hall had mostly emptied out and the fire had burned down to embers, I took out my phone.

I hadn't touched it since the night of the luggage search, when Silvaine had held it in her hand and pressed the button for two seconds and let go. It had been wrapped in its scrap of cloth at the bottom of my pouch ever since, through the trial and the rain and the treaty, a flat black slab of glass and metal from another world, perfectly dark.

I unwrapped it and looked at it for a while.

The last time I'd seen the screen on, it had said thirty-eight percent. That had been in the Silverwood, in my first week, in a bedroom with a tapestry of round-eared monsters over the bed. Phones lose charge when they're off, slowly. I'd known that. I'd been trying not to think about it.

I held down the button.

For a long moment, nothing. Then the screen lit up, white, and a little chime played, three bright notes, the most Earth-sounding noise anyone in Verathis had ever heard. Brakka, who'd been dozing on a bench by the hearth, came off it with a roar and his hand on his sword. Thessa nearly dropped her cup. The goat, asleep under the long table, opened one eye. Silvaine, at the end of the table with her ledger, went perfectly rigid and stared at the glowing slab in my hand as if it had confessed.

"The scrying stone," she said slowly. "You said it woke only for royal blood."

"I lied," I said. "Sorry."

She considered this. Then she opened her ledger, and made a small, neat note, and closed it again.

The lock screen came up. It was still the same picture: me and Dev at a minor-league baseball game the summer before, both of us holding up giant foam fingers, sunburned and grinning, with a hot dog in the background. I hadn't seen it in three months. It looked like a picture of somebody else.

In the top corner of the screen, in small white numbers, it said: *3%.*

Three percent. Maybe a minute. Maybe less.

I didn't have to think about what to do with it.

"Everyone," I said. "Come here. Quick."

They came. They didn't know why. Brakka, still holding his sword, very suspicious of the chime. Thessa, with her charms jingling. Durgan, who'd been asleep in his chair with his ledger on his chest and woke up already reaching for a form. The goat, who came because everyone else was coming. And Silvaine, last, and slowest, and only after I asked her twice, standing very straight at the edge of the group with her hands folded and an expression of deep disapproval, because she had never been in a picture in her life, and did not approve of things she didn't understand.

I turned the camera around, the way you do, and held it up at arm's length. On the little glowing screen, all of us appeared at once: a tired, round-eared guy in a green tunic in the middle, and around him, crowding in, an enormous one-eared orc with his war paint on crooked, a young orc woman with braids full of charms, a red-faced dwarf with a beard like a hedge, an elven inquisitor trying very hard not to be in it, and, at the very bottom of the frame, chewing, a goat.

They all stared at the screen. They'd never seen themselves move in a picture before.

"Okay," I said. "Everybody say *pineapple*."

There was a pause. Thessa looked at me. Then she started to laugh, the bell-sound, the one from the day the ceiling got cleaned. And then they all said it at once, in four different accents, loudly and badly and nowhere near together:

"*Pineapple!*"

Thessa didn't cast anything. She didn't even flinch. Not anymore.

The flash went off, white and sudden, and the little shutter sound clicked. Brakka flinched. Silvaine blinked. Durgan said "Ah!" and the goat bleated, musically, in a key I'd never heard before.

And then, before I could turn the phone around to look at it, before I could see what we looked like, the screen went black.

I held down the button. Nothing. Three percent had become zero, and the phone was just a cold slab of glass again, in my hand, in a meeting hall at the top of the world.

They all looked at me.

"Is it broken?" asked Brakka, with deep concern.

"No," I said. "It's just tired."

I wrapped it up again in its scrap of cloth, carefully, and put it back in my belt pouch, next to Form 12-C, and E.M.'s note, and Wenna's arrow, and the empty bottle of spirit gum. All of it, everything I'd carried from one world to the other, and back, and back again.

Somewhere inside that dead black glass, there's a picture of five people and a goat, all saying *pineapple*. Nobody on Earth would ever believe it. Some stories you can only tell if everyone thinks you made them up.

## Epilogue: The Prince of the Rain

**THE PRINCE OF THE RAIN** *A war ballad of Clan Ashtusk, by Brakka, Sword-Bearer and Rag Monitor* *To be sung to the tune of the Prince's song*

> *The floor of the dwarf did open wide,*\
*And out came the Prince with his ears by his side.*\
*He would not eat dew, and he ate all the pie,*\
*And he spoke the great word that made lords want to die.*\
*And the Prince was not an elf.*\
*The Prince led.*
>
> *He shot at the sky and it went in the dwarf.*\
*He hid in a barrel. The barrel had a cough.*\
*He made a small roof for to keep off the rain,*\
*And the elves danced beneath it and got wet again.*\
*And the Prince was not an elf.*\
*The Prince led.*
>
> *He wrote the long letter. He hid the true line*\
*In the middle of grease, where it waited a time.*\
*And the Lord of the Silver said "Bring them to death,"*\
*And the Prince read the axle grease out in one breath.*\
*And the Prince was not an elf.*\
*The Prince led.*
>
> *Then he closed the small roof and he stood in the storm,*\
*And his ears fell away, and he stood in his form.*\
*And the goat ate the ear. It was very well done.*\
*And four thousand stood still, and he was not one.*\
*And the Prince was not an elf.*\
*The Prince led.*
>
> *And I walked to his side, for I chose it at lunch,*\
*For he fed me the bread when he had not so much.*\
*They will ask me for why. I will tell them it's plain:*\
*It was strategy. All of it. Even the rain.*\
*And the Prince was not an elf.*\
*The Prince led.*

---

*Note from the author:* The orcs of Ashtusk now sing this at every feast. It is better with more singers, because the tune is difficult, and also the Prince's song has more notes than a song needs. The Prince says his father could not sing it either. This is a comfort.

*The fifth verse is true. The rest is mostly true. The barrel did cough.*

*Brakka*
