# Chapter 22: Rain

By eleven o'clock, the clouds over Hearthhold were the color of a bruise, and four hundred elves were thrilled about it.

They'd started building at dawn. A thin gray haze, at first, along the northern rim of the valley, the kind of sky that might turn into anything. By nine, it had thickened and darkened and spread, and by ten it had swallowed the sun. The air had gone heavy and still. The weatherglass, when I checked it at first light, had climbed higher than I'd ever seen it, so high it was nearly off the top of my scratches on the reed. I didn't need to check it again after that. I could feel it in my teeth.

And all over the camp of the Concordance, elves were stepping out of their tents and looking up at the sky, and smiling.

Thessa did my ears at dawn, the way she always did. She didn't say anything while she worked, and neither did I. She painted on the resin, thin and even, and set the right ear, and held it, and counted. Then the left, crooked. Then Gwendolyn, braided and pinned, and the glitter. When she was finished, she stepped back and looked at me for a long moment, and then she reached out and straightened the left ear by about a hair. Then put it back.

"There," she said quietly.

On my way out of the tent, I found Durgan, and put two silver coins in his hand.

He looked at them. "What's this?"

"From the Inquisition," I said. "For the tankard. At the Golden Bird."

Durgan looked at the coins for a long moment. Then he looked up at me, and his face did something I'd never seen it do, which was go completely blank with astonishment.

"She *paid*?" he whispered.

He closed his fist around them very carefully, the way you'd hold a bird. I left him standing there, and when I looked back, he was writing something in his ledger with great tenderness.

By a quarter to twelve, everyone was in place.

The host of the Concordance had gathered at the foot of Hearthhold's wall in a great half-ring, rank on rank, filling the floor of the valley from one rock face to the other. The High Houses sat in front, on folding chairs their servants had carried up from the camp, in their silks and their cushions and their crooked ear-clips. Behind them stood the dwarves, with Thane Borrik at their head and a pen behind his ear. Then the orc companies, in their dark iron. Gorza at the front of them, on her huge black horse, still as a statue. Okko beside her. And at the very front of the orc ranks, in full armor, with his war paint on straight for once, Brakka. The wolf-folk ran along the flanks. The owl-folk perched on the rocks above, swiveling their heads.

On the wall above, in a long row, stood every soul in Hearthhold. Ruth Ashgrove in the middle, with the Book of the Reckoning in her arms. Tobiah beside her, in his scarves. Wenna, with her bow. And at the very front, sitting cross-legged on the top of the wall in a neat row with their legs dangling over the edge, the seven members of the committee: Davy, holding the spoon, and the girl with the slate, already writing.

Silvaine stood apart, at the edge of the ring, alone, with the flat leather case at her hip. Captain Isaura sat her dented horse behind Caelith's chair, her scarred face as still as stone. Corvel had set up an actual easel, with a large stretched canvas on it, and a paint box, and three brushes held in his teeth. Lady Ithrien was sitting in the front row with her Prince's Canopy folded across her knees, bouncing very slightly with excitement every time the sky rumbled. Venrick sat beside her in nothing but his silver robes, on principle, already faintly blue. Master Feyhallow held his leaf ready. And on top of the oat wagon at the edge of the field, surveying the entire scene like a judge, sat the goat.

In the middle of it all, at the foot of the wall, on a low platform of planks the servants had laid across the snow, there was a single chair. The Prince's chair.

I walked out to it, through the open ground between the host and the wall, with four thousand people watching me. Thessa walked one step behind me on my left. I had my foam sword at my hip, and Wenna's arrow in my pouch, and in my right hand, furled, I carried my umbrella. The first one. The one that leaned a little to the left.

I sat down. Thessa stood beside the chair.

Above us, very far away, thunder rolled across the top of the world, long and low.

Four hundred elves sighed with pleasure.

\* \* \*

Caelith spoke first. That was his right, under the Charter, as the High Lord who had brought the charge.

He rose from his chair in the front row and walked out onto the open ground in front of the host, in silver from collar to boots, with his long hair loose down his back. He didn't hurry. He stood there for a moment, looking out over the four thousand faces, and up at the wall, and the people on it, and let the silence gather around him like a cloak. Then he began to speak.

And he was magnificent.

I have to be honest about that. I've listened to a lot of speeches in my life. I've heard presidents, on television. I've heard a valedictorian make an entire gymnasium cry. I've never heard anything like Caelith Vaunt prosecuting the last of the Blight.

He didn't shout. He never shouted. He spoke softly, reasonably, sorrowfully, in a voice that carried to the back of the valley, and he told them a story. He told them about the Sister Trees, the oldest grove in the world, the Eldertree's own sisters, and the night they burned. He told them about the rivers that had run black, and the forests cut down to stumps, and the treaties broken one after another. He told them about the long, bitter years before the Concordance rose up, when every people of the forest had suffered under the Blight. He used every word I'd ever read in those forty books in the archive. *Vermin.* *Plague.* *The Cleansing.* He used them gently, regretfully, as if it pained him to say them. And by the time he was finished, I could see it in the faces of the High Houses in the front row: the old fear, coming back. And something else, underneath it. Righteousness. The pleasure of being on the right side of a story.

I recognized every single technique. I'd taught a unit on them. I'd graded essays on them, with a rubric, in red pen.

And my brain, without my permission, graded this one too. *Rhetoric: A-plus. Delivery: A-plus. Sources cited: zero.*

When he'd finished, the High Houses applauded. Not loudly. Solemnly, the way you'd applaud at a funeral. Venrick rose and seconded the charge, briefly and coldly, in about four sentences, and sat back down, bluer than before.

Then it was Hearthhold's turn.

Ruth Ashgrove didn't come down from the wall. She stayed where she was, small and white-haired and straight-backed, among her people, and she opened the Book of the Reckoning in her arms, and spoke from there. Her dry, quiet voice didn't carry the way Caelith's had. People in the back ranks had to lean forward to hear her. They leaned forward.

She didn't deny anything. That was the first thing that struck the crowd; I could feel it ripple through the ranks. She didn't argue with a single word Caelith had said. Instead, she read them her own book.

She read them the forests. The valleys cleared past the treaty lines. The tanneries that fouled the rivers, and the children who'd sickened downstream. The boundary stones moved in the night. The treaties broken, one after another, because human lives are short and human memories are shorter. She read every sin her ancestors had committed, from the pages they'd written themselves, in that cave at the top of the valley, that first long winter. She read them in a plain, steady voice, without excuses.

And then she closed the book, and looked down at the High Houses of the Silverwood, and told them about the rule over the door of her meeting hall. *Take only what you can give back.* She told them that every child in Hearthhold learned it before they learned to read. And she told them that her people had kept it for three hundred years.

"We don't ask you to forgive what our grandparents did," said Ruth Ashgrove. "We've never forgiven it ourselves. We only ask to be allowed to live."

She sat down on the wall, among her people. Nobody applauded. Nobody knew whether they were allowed to.

Then Silvaine stepped forward.

She walked out into the open ground, with her black and silver robes very straight and her ledger in her hand, and the crystal's case at her hip. She bowed, precisely, to the High Houses, and to me in my chair.

"By the authority of the Inquisition of the Concordance," she said, "I have questions." She looked around the ring, slowly, at every face. "Of everyone."

She asked Ruth Ashgrove questions first. Precise ones. How many people lived in Hearthhold. How long they had been there. Whether any of them had ever left the valley, or raided, or stolen, in living memory. Ruth answered each one plainly, and Silvaine wrote each answer down.

Then she turned, unhurriedly, toward Caelith.

"My lord," she said. "A question of history, for the record. In the year of the Burning of the Sister Trees, where did the timber camp of House Vaunt stand?"

The valley was very quiet.

Caelith looked at her. His face didn't change at all. "On the eastern edge of the old Grant lands, Inquisitor," he said smoothly. "As every history records. Some distance from the Sister Trees." He smiled. "Is that relevant?"

"It's a question," said Silvaine, "for the record." And she wrote his answer down, carefully, in her ledger, and underlined something, and closed it.

I saw Captain Isaura, behind Caelith's chair, turn her head very slightly to look at Silvaine. Then turn it back.

And that was when I felt the first drop.

It landed on the back of my hand, on the arm of the Prince's chair: one fat, cold drop of rain. Then another, on my knee. Then a scatter of them, all around the platform, making little dark pocks in the snow.

All around the valley, the elves of the Silverwood turned their faces up to the sky with expressions of pure joy.

In the front row, Lady Ithrien gave a little cry of delight, snapped open her Prince's Canopy, and immediately turned it upside down to catch the rain. And at the end of the row, Master Feyhallow lifted his leaf toward the darkening sky, studied it for a moment with great solemnity, and announced, to absolutely no one:

"The leaf foresaw it."

\* \* \*

It was my turn.

Under the Charter, the trial ended with the judgment of the presiding Prince. The assembled Houses would then vote to accept it or reject it. That was the procedure. I'd read it six times. And every one of those four thousand people turned, as the drops began to fall, and looked at me.

I stood up from the Prince's chair.

The rain was still light, then. Scattered drops, big and cold and slow, the kind that come before the real thing. I felt one land on Gwendolyn. Another on my left ear, on the latex, and run down the back of it toward the edge of the glue. And without thinking, out of three months of pure reflex, I lifted my umbrella and opened it over my head.

It went up with a soft *whump*. Waxed silk, split reeds, ash handle, leaning a little to the left. I stood there under it, at the center of the valley, with the rain pattering on the silk.

And I looked at it.

I thought about the first day I'd carried it across the Court of Fountains, in the long rains, and the courtiers stopping dead to stare. I thought about every courtyard I'd ever dashed across under it, with one hand pressed to my ear. I'd carried it four hundred miles. It had kept me alive. It was the best thing I'd ever built.

It was also the last piece of the lie.

So I closed it.

Slowly. Deliberately. In front of everyone. I lowered it from over my head and folded the silk down around the reeds, and fastened it, and I leaned down and laid it in the snow at my feet, beside the Prince's chair. Then I straightened up, and stood there with nothing between me and the sky.

A murmur went through the High Houses in the front row. I heard Lady Ithrien gasp, in a delighted, tremulous way. I heard someone whisper: *He shares the rain with us.* And all along the front row, I saw elves put their hands over their hearts.

They thought it was a gesture. They thought it was beautiful.

In a way, I guess it was.

Then I began to speak.

I didn't use Aelindor's voice. Not yet. I used the one I use in a classroom, on the first day of a new unit: clear, and steady, and slow enough for the back row.

I told them I had read the archives. I told them about the iron door at the end of the royal library, sealed by order of the High Houses, which no one had opened in three hundred years, and which I had opened under the authority of the Archive Charter. I told them what was inside. The honest reports from the borderlands, about what the humans had really done, which Ruth Ashgrove had just read them from her own book. And the maps. Surveys of the human country, with the names of the High Houses written across every village, dated two years before the Purge began.

In the front row, the High Lords began to look at each other.

And I told them about the Burning of the Sister Trees. About the report that had started the whole war, which had been written twice. I told them about the first draft, in a plain, quick hand, which said that the fire's origin was unknown, and named the place it had started.

I looked at Caelith.

He was sitting very still in his chair in the front row, with his hands folded on his knee. His face hadn't changed. It didn't need to. I'd learned, by then, to read it by what it didn't do.

"The first draft," I said, "says the fire started near the timber camp of—"

And that was when the sky opened.

\* \* \*

It came down at noon. Exactly at noon. My log was right to the minute.

It didn't build up. It didn't start light and get heavier. It went from scattered drops to a solid gray wall of water in about the time it takes to draw a breath, hammering down on the valley so hard that it bounced off the planks of the platform and turned the snow at my feet to slush in seconds. It was the biggest storm I'd seen in Verathis. It was louder than the crowd had ever been. And all around the valley, the elves of the Silverwood lifted their faces to it, laughing.

I kept talking. I had to raise my voice to be heard over the rain, but I kept talking. I told them the name on the first draft. I told them that the second draft had crossed it out, and written *Set by the Blight* in the margin, in a different hand.

And while I was saying it, I felt the left ear go.

It went the way it always did, from the top, where the glue gave first. I felt the rain find the edge of it and run in underneath, cold, against my raw skin. I felt the resin go soft. I felt the latex start to lift, a quarter of an inch, half an inch, the tip of it peeling away from my head and tilting outward, heavy with water.

And for the first time in three months, I didn't reach for it.

I didn't clap my hand over it. I didn't count to thirty. I just stood there with my hands at my sides, in the downpour, and kept telling four thousand people about a crossed-out line on a report, while my left ear slowly peeled itself off my head.

It hung there for a moment, by the very bottom edge. Then it let go.

It slid down past my jaw, and off my shoulder, and fell, turning over once, and landed in the slush at my feet with a soft, wet slap.

The crooked one. Of course it was the crooked one.

The sound that went through the valley wasn't a gasp, exactly. It was too big for that. It was four thousand people drawing a breath at the same moment, and the rain was so loud that you felt it more than heard it, like a change in the air pressure.

I kept talking. My voice cracked a little. I kept going.

The right ear went next. It was quicker. It slid off the side of my head and down my neck, under my collar, and caught in the folds of my bedsheet cape, and hung there, pointing at the ground.

Then Gwendolyn.

She'd been soaking up the rain like a sponge, all that long, too-clean, over-Purified hair, and she'd gotten so heavy that she'd started to slide sideways off the back of my head, dragging the wig cap with her. I could feel her going. So before she could fall, I reached up, and took hold of her, and lifted her off my head myself.

I held her in one hand, dripping, at my side. And I stood there, under the open sky, in front of the assembled Houses of the Silverwood, with my short brown hair plastered flat to my skull by the rain, and my own ears, round and small and red and raw, and the beetle-shell glitter running down my face in long silver streaks.

And beside me, Thessa spoke.

She didn't shout. She didn't panic. She said it calmly and clearly, in a voice that carried over the rain to the front ranks and up the wall behind us, the way a teacher says the one word that the whole class has been waiting for.

"Pineapple."

And from the top of the wall, seven small voices answered her, at the very tops of their lungs, with total joy:

"*PINEAPPLE!*"

I didn't dive behind anything. For the first time ever, I didn't move at all.

Thessa raised both her hands, and called it, on purpose.

It wasn't a flashbulb this time, and it wasn't a firework. It was a slow, wide, steady wave of silver-white light that rose from her hands and rolled out over me like a breaking wave in slow motion, and washed through me, cool and clean, and was gone. And when it had passed, it had taken everything with it. The last smear of resin along my jaw. Every speck of glitter, every streak. The faint gray ghost of the beetle shells. Even the dirt on my boots. Everything that didn't belong.

I stood in the rain, perfectly clean. A twenty-three-year-old from North Carolina, in a green tunic and a bathtub-dyed bedsheet, holding a wet wig.

Nobody moved.

At his easel, Corvel's brush had stopped in midair. The paint was running off it in the rain. He was staring at me with his mouth open.

Off to one side, Silvaine reached down to the case at her hip, and unlatched it, and lifted out the crystal. It blazed in her hand, brighter than I'd ever seen it, brighter than a flare, so bright that the people nearest her turned their faces away, throwing a hard blue light across the rain. She looked at it. She looked at me. And then, without a word, without any change in her face at all, she put it back in its case, and closed the latch, and stood there with her hands folded.

She'd known. Of course she'd known. She'd only been waiting for the right procedure.

I looked out at them. At the High Houses in their front row, frozen in their silks, with the rain running off them. At the dwarves, and the wolf-folk, and the orc companies, every face turned toward me. At Okko, at the front of the orcs, with his mouth open. At Gorza, very still. At Lady Ithrien, with her upside-down umbrella full of rain, and both hands over her mouth. At Venrick, soaked and blue and staring.

I stood there in the rain, holding my own wig, with my own ears, in front of everyone. For the first time in three months, nobody was looking at a Prince. They were all just looking at me.

So I spoke. Not in Aelindor's voice. In mine.

"My name is Marcus Delaney," I said. "I'm human. I'm a teacher. And I'm the Prince you summoned." The rain ran down my face, into my mouth. I didn't wipe it away. "Everything I did for you, I did as one of them."

On top of the wall, the girl with the slate bent her head over it, and very carefully, sounding it out under her breath in the rain, wrote my name down.

\* \* \*

For a long moment, there was nothing but rain.

Four thousand people stood in it, and nobody spoke, and nobody moved. It drummed on the planks of the platform, and on the silks of the High Houses, and on the dark iron of the orc companies, and on the stone of Hearthhold's wall. It ran down my face. It ran down Thessa's braids. It was the loudest silence I have ever stood inside.

And into that silence, the goat climbed down off the oat wagon.

It didn't hurry. It hopped down onto the snow, and shook itself, and ambled out across the open ground between the host and the wall, in no particular direction, the way it went everywhere. Four thousand people watched it come. It wandered up to the edge of the platform, and put its head down, and found my crooked left ear lying in the slush, where it had fallen.

It sniffed it. Then it picked it up, and ate it.

The only sound in the whole valley was the rain, and the goat, chewing.

Nobody reacted. Nobody laughed. I don't think anyone was capable of it. The goat finished the ear, and looked around, and wandered back toward the oat wagon, entirely satisfied, and that was the end of the crooked ear of the Lost Prince of the First Line.

Then Caelith stood up.

He rose out of his chair in the front row, very slowly, with the rain streaming off his silver hair and his silver robes, and for the first time since the summoning chamber, I saw his face without anything on it at all. No smile. No calm. No chess game. Just a pale, still, terrible blankness, like a mask somebody had forgotten to paint.

"Seize the Blight," he said.

It was the first time I'd ever heard him raise his voice. It cracked, very slightly, on the last word.

Nobody moved.

Behind his chair, three hundred soldiers of House Vaunt's household guard stood in their silver ranks in the rain, with their longbows on their shoulders and their hands on their swords. And every single one of them was looking, not at me, and not at Caelith, but at the old, scarred, gray-haired captain on her dented horse at the front of them.

Captain Isaura didn't move.

She didn't draw her sword. She didn't give an order. She didn't even look at Caelith. She sat her horse in the downpour, perfectly still, with her eyes fixed on the place directly in front of her, exactly the way she had the day before at the wall. As if she hadn't heard him at all.

So the guard didn't move either.

"*Seize the Blight*," said Caelith again, louder.

The rain fell. Nobody moved.

And then, at the front of the orc ranks, someone did.

I heard it before I saw it: the creak and clank of heavy iron armor, one set of footsteps, splashing through the slush. And then Brakka stepped out of the ranks, alone, and started walking.

He didn't run. He'd run across this valley the day before, to see if I'd come back. Today he walked. Slowly, steadily, through the pouring rain, across the open ground between the host of the Concordance and the wall of Hearthhold, past the High Houses in their front row, past Caelith, standing, past the easel where Corvel had started, very slowly, to paint again. Four thousand people watched him come. Nobody stopped him. I don't think anybody could have.

He didn't draw his sword. He didn't look at me. He came up onto the platform, onto the planks beside the Prince's chair, and stood there, and the planks creaked under him.

Brakka stopped beside me, and turned, and faced them: the High Houses, and Caelith, and the four thousand soldiers of the Concordance, standing silent in the rain.

I stood there, holding my own wig, with my own ears, in front of everyone. For the first time in three months, nobody was looking at a Prince. They were all just looking at us.

Brakka looked at them for a long time. Then he nodded, once, slowly.

"Strategy," he said.
