# Chapter 12: The Barrel

For four days after the archives, I didn't do anything at all. Caelith, I'd find out later, was counting on that.

I didn't go to council. I didn't go to the archives. I didn't answer Lady Ithrien's notes, which arrived three times a day on scented paper and got progressively more worried. I didn't eat much of what Master Orrin sent up, which I heard later had hurt his feelings badly enough that he'd gone and sat in the pantry for an hour. I didn't open my lesson plan. It sat on my desk where I'd left it, with *Buy time* underlined twice in my own handwriting, and I walked past it every morning without looking.

Mostly, I sat in the window seat and read the two drafts of the fire report.

Over and over. The first one, in its quick, plain hand. Then the second, with its single neat line through *of unknown origin, near the timber camp of House Vaunt*, and the three words in the margin, and the initial. I'd read them until I could have recited them, and then I'd read them again, as if one of these times the words would be different.

On the third day, a note came from Caelith, on heavy silver paper.

> *I hear the Prince is unwell. Rest, Highness. There is nothing that cannot wait.*
>
> *C.*

I read it twice. I should have noticed how much he wanted me to wait. I didn't. I put it on the windowsill with the fire reports and went back to staring at the rain clouds building up, very slowly, over the trees to the west.

My friends noticed, though.

Thessa came and sat with me every day without saying anything, the way she'd sat next to me on the floor of the archive. Durgan came in on the fourth morning with his ledger under his arm, stood in front of me, and announced, in a voice of real alarm, "Highness. You haven't looked at the ledger in four days. *Four days.*" He seemed to think this was the clearest possible sign that I was dying.

And Brakka stood in the doorway, the way he always did, and watched me for four days with his arms folded and a frown on his scarred face that got deeper every time he looked at me.

On the fourth afternoon, he made a decision.

"The Prince," he announced to the room, "needs a song."

I didn't have time to tell him the Prince did not need a song. He'd already started.

It was "The Arrow of the Forest," his ballad of the archery trial, set to my dad's old road-trip song. And this time, Brakka did it properly, which, I learned, meant with a dance. An orc war dance is about what you'd expect: a lot of stomping, a lot of chest-slapping, and a spinning move at the end of every chorus that made the floor shake and the floating lamps sway on their invisible strings. Thessa started clapping, off the beat. The goat got excited and started running in circles. Durgan backed slowly into a corner and held his ledger over his head.

And I'll admit it: about halfway through the second verse, I felt something in my chest come loose, a little.

Then came the last chorus. Brakka spun, enormous and joyful, with his arms flung wide. And his elbow caught the wine-bottle barometer on the balcony rail and swept it neatly off the edge.

There was a pause, about two seconds long, while it fell. Then a crash from the courtyard below.

Nobody moved. Brakka stood frozen in the middle of the room with his arms still out, looking at the empty rail. Then he looked at me. I'd never seen him look so stricken. Not even on the bottom step of the wine cellar.

I got up and went to the rail and looked over. It was in about a hundred pieces on the paving stones, glass and water and the snapped reed, glittering in the gray afternoon light.

"...Strategy?" said Brakka, very quietly, behind me.

"No, Brakka," I said. "That one wasn't."

I heard how it sounded the moment it was out of my mouth. It was the first time I'd ever turned his word down. He took it the way he'd take a blow, standing still, without a sound, and looked at the floor.

I could build another one; the bottle was easy, and Durgan had dozens. But the baseline was the problem. To know when the water was rising, I had to know where it sat on a clear, steady day, and setting that takes a full day of good weather. And the sky over the Silverwood didn't look like it had a full day of good weather left in it.

For the first time since my first week, I couldn't see the weather coming.

Thessa stood up. "Then we're taking you out," she said. It might have been the first time I'd ever heard her say anything without a *Highness* in it. "Today."

Brakka lifted his head. "To make it right," he said.

\* \* \*

They took me down into the lower city.

I'd never been there. In five weeks in the Silverwood, I'd seen the palace, the gardens, the archery green, and the market square under Durgan's awning, and that was all. The lower city was down the hill from all of that, through a gate in the old wall and down a long flight of worn stone steps, and the first thing I noticed was that none of it was grown.

The houses were built. Timber and plaster and slate, with crooked roofs and window boxes and laundry strung between them on lines. The streets were narrow and cobbled and smelled like baking bread, and woodsmoke, and horses, and about forty other things that the palace had never smelled like once. There were orcs here who weren't guards, carrying sacks and arguing over the price of turnips. There were dwarves in leather aprons, and beastfolk of kinds I'd never seen: a family of otter-folk with a pushcart full of fish, an old badger-folk woman mending shoes on a doorstep. And there were elves, working elves, with sunburned necks and patched elbows, who didn't look anything like the moonlight sculptures in the Hall of the Eldertree.

Nobody bowed. A few people stared at the cape. Mostly, they were busy.

The tavern was at the bottom of a crooked lane, under a hanging wooden sign with a gilded bird on it, wings spread. The sign said *THE GOLDEN* in old, faded gold letters, and then, in fresh, much less skillful paint, *BIRD*. Underneath the fresh paint, if you looked closely, you could just make out the ghost of the old word. It had ended in *-OOSE*.

"Changed it after the Royal Greeting," said a voice from the doorway. "Lord fainted on my step."

She was a dwarf, about as tall as my chest and nearly as wide as the door, with a gray beard braided into two thick ropes and forearms like a pair of hams. She looked me up and down, slowly: the cape, the glitter, Gwendolyn, the crooked ear. She did not bow.

"You're the Prince," she said.

"I am."

"Sit down, then," she said. "You're blocking the fire."

Her name was Old Mabb, and she'd run the Golden Bird, formerly the Golden Goose, for a hundred and sixty years, and she did not care about the Old Blood, or the Starlight faction, or the Prince, or anything else that happened up the hill. Inside, the Golden Bird was low and warm and crowded, with a big stone fireplace and smoke-black beams and tables full of people who were there to drink, not to be seen. A table of orcs were playing dice in the corner. Two dwarves were having a loud argument about a horse. And at the bar sat the miller from my consultation line, flour on his knuckles, who saw me and lifted his mug with a grin of recognition.

Nobody bowed in there, either. I can't tell you what a relief that was.

Mabb brought ale in big wooden tankards. When I paid, I added twenty percent, from habit. She looked at the extra coins in her palm, and then at me, with deep disapproval.

"Don't," she said, and gave it back. "Your lot's ruined tipping for everybody. Used to be a copper meant thank you. Now a lord tips me a crown for opening the door, and the baker down the lane wants a crown for a loaf of bread." She shook her head. "Pay what it costs, Prince. That's all I want."

It was the first time since I'd arrived that anybody had told me no.

Brakka, who had been looking for a way to make up for the barometer since the moment it hit the paving stones, found one about an hour in. He stood up on a bench, cleared his throat with a sound like a cart going over a bridge, and began to sing "The Arrow of the Forest."

The Golden Bird didn't know the song. By the second chorus, it did. By the third, the whole room was singing the last line with him, the orcs from the dice table loudest of all, banging their tankards on the wood: *AND THE PRINCE DID NOT MISS! THE PRINCE DECREED!* Mabb shook her head and wiped down the bar, and didn't stop them.

And I laughed. Really laughed, with my head back, for the first time in four days. Maybe for the first time since the archives. Brakka saw it from the top of the bench, in the middle of the chorus, and grinned at me with every tusk he had.

Later, when the singing had worn down and the room had gone warm and drowsy, Mabb pulled up a stool across from me with her own tankard. I don't know why I asked her. Maybe because she was the first person in the Silverwood who'd told me no.

"Mabb. What do people down here think about the Purge?"

She looked at me for a long moment over the rim of the tankard.

"Wars are for lords," she said. "My great-gran fought in that one. Came home with one leg and a medal she sold for soup." She took a long drink. "Nobody down here ever got a Grant, Prince. We just buried people."

I thought about the maps in the iron room, with the names of the Houses written across the farms and villages in neat black ink. *Vaunt. Aldervane. Velloran. Calloway.* Not one of them had been named Mabb.

\* \* \*

The worst place to get caught in the rain, if your ears are glued on, is anywhere. The second worst, I was about to discover, is a bar.

I didn't hear it coming. That was the whole problem. For a month, the wine bottle on my balcony had told me about every storm a day ahead, and I'd gotten used to it, the way you get used to a smoke detector. Without it, I was just a guy in a tavern with a tankard, enjoying himself, not paying attention.

The first thing I noticed was the thunder. It rolled over the lower city long and low, close enough to rattle the tankards on their hooks behind the bar. The second thing was the sound on the roof. It started as a few heavy drops, then many, then a single unbroken roar, like someone had turned a fire hose on the Golden Bird.

The orcs at the dice table cheered. Somebody opened the door to look out, and a gust of wet air blew in and made the fire hiss.

And directly above my head, a drop of water formed on a smoke-black beam, hung there, and fell.

It hit me square on the crown of my head, right through Gwendolyn. I felt it. Then I felt another, and another, cold and steady, a leak in the roof exactly over my seat.

"Been meaning to fix that," Mabb said from across the table, looking up at the beam without much interest. "About a century."

The water ran down through the wig. It found my scalp. It ran down behind my left ear, under the edge of the latex, the way water always finds the one place you don't want it, and I felt the glue go soft.

The ear lifted.

I clapped my hand over it. And that was the exact moment the door of the Golden Bird banged open, letting in a cold blast of wind and rain, and Silvaine of House Thistlevane stepped in out of the storm.

She was soaked, the way she had been on the day of the crystal: water streaming off her black and silver robes, her braid dark and dripping. She stopped just inside the doorway and swept her eyes over the whole room in one slow pass, the way I scan a classroom on the first day.

I'd seen her twice that week. Once across the market square, once at the far end of a palace corridor. I'd thought nothing of it either time. I understood now: she'd been following me since the day of the crystal. Her new one wasn't grown yet. She'd been using the oldest instrument there is instead. Her eyes.

They hadn't found me yet. I was in the back of the room, half in shadow, behind Brakka. But they would. And I was sitting under a leak in the roof with one hand clamped over an ear that was about to come off.

I must have gone very pale. Across the table, Mabb looked at my face, at my hand pressed over my ear, and at the inquisitor in her doorway, and I watched her decide something.

She didn't ask a single question. She just jerked her thumb, once, at the back corner of the room, where a big empty ale barrel stood against the wall, taller than a man's waist.

I went.

Brakka, without being told, shifted his whole enormous body sideways on the bench, so that he blocked the view from the door like a wall. I slid off my stool, crouched low through the shadows behind the tables, hand still pressed to my head, and climbed into the barrel. It smelled like about a hundred years of ale. I folded myself down inside it, knees to my chest, my cape bunched up around me.

Above me, the lid dropped into place, and everything went dark.

Then I heard two sharp, solid knocks, very close to my head, as Mabb drove two nails into the lid with the heel of a pewter tankard.

"There," she said, loud enough for the room to hear. "Sealed for shipment."

\* \* \*

I heard her footsteps cross the room. They were precise, evenly spaced, unhurried, and they stopped right in front of the barrel.

"Proprietor," said Silvaine. "What is in this barrel?"

"Ale," said Mabb.

"I heard hammering."

"Sealing it. For shipment."

"At this hour." It wasn't a question. There was a pause. I could picture her looking at the lid, at the two fresh nails, and I held my breath. "I heard something else before the hammering. Movement."

"Ale moves," said Mabb. "It's a liquid."

Another pause. Then I heard Silvaine do something I didn't expect: she took a step back.

"Under Section Nine of the Inquisitorial Code," she said, in the voice of someone reading aloud from a law she knows by heart, "merchandise sealed for shipment may not be opened by an officer of the Inquisition without a Writ of Inspection." Her voice tightened, just a little. "Which I do not have."

Inside the barrel, in the dark, I nearly laughed out loud. Of course. Of *course*. Silvaine of House Thistlevane would sooner let the Blight walk free than open a barrel without the proper paperwork.

I heard her snap her fingers. Quick footsteps approached, and she gave a low, crisp order, and then the door opened and banged shut, and somebody went running off into the storm toward the Inquisition hall, up the hill. It would take him a while in that rain. Twenty minutes, maybe. Maybe more.

Then I heard the scrape of a stool being dragged across the floor, and set down directly in front of the barrel. Silvaine sat.

"I know someone is in there," she said, to the barrel.

Silence. Outside, the rain roared. I sat very still in the ale-smelling dark with my knees against my chin and my hand pressed to the side of my head.

"Ale does not breathe," said Silvaine.

"Good ale does," said Mabb, from somewhere to my left.

"Barrels do not shift."

"They settle. Old barrel. It's settling."

"I am addressing the barrel, proprietor."

"And I'm answering for it," said Mabb. "It's my barrel."

It went on like that. I don't know how long. It felt like a week. Silvaine asked the barrel who it was, and where it had come from, and whether it was aware that harboring the Blight was the gravest crime in the Concordance, and every time, Mabb answered for it, flatly and without the slightest hurry, like a woman reciting a grocery list. At one point, I heard Durgan's voice, warm and helpful, offering the Inquisitor something to drink, since she'd come in out of the rain, and then the clink of a tankard being set down. She didn't refuse it, I noticed. I heard Durgan make a note in his ledger.

Meanwhile, I worked.

The jar of resin was in my belt pouch, where I kept it now, always, next to E.M.'s note and the empty spirit gum bottle. I got it out by feel, unstoppered it in the pitch dark, and found the edge of my ear with my fingertips. I painted the resin on by touch. I waited for it to go tacky, counting in my head. Then I pressed the ear back into place and held it, and counted to thirty, the way I've counted to thirty for every ear and every twelve-year-old since this whole thing started.

It held.

I let out a very, very slow breath. And because the barrel was full of the fumes of a hundred years of ale, and because I'd been shedding beetle-shell glitter into it for ten minutes, and because the universe has a sense of humor, I breathed in, and my nose began to tickle.

I tried. I really did. I pinched my nose, I pressed my tongue to the roof of my mouth, I thought about the saddest things I could think of.

I sneezed.

It was loud. In that barrel, it sounded like a cannon.

The entire Golden Bird went dead silent. Even the rain seemed to stop and listen.

Then, without missing a single beat, Mabb said, "Barrel's got a cold."

There was a long pause. Then, very faintly, I heard the scratch of a pen on paper. Silvaine was writing it down.

Somewhere to my right, I heard a little jingle of charms, and a sharp, panicked intake of breath. I knew that breath. It was the breath Thessa took right before she said a certain word, and if she said it now, with her hands pointed anywhere near this barrel, it would scrub away the barrel, and the ale smell, and the glue, and my ears, and everything else in here that didn't belong, which was me.

"Pine—" Thessa began.

There was a muffled thump. Then silence. I learned later that Brakka had clapped one enormous hand gently over her mouth and was holding it there, while looking at the ceiling, as though he had no idea how it had gotten there.

In the dark, I shifted my weight, very carefully, to ease the cramp in my legs. And my foot, instead of pressing against the bottom of the barrel, pressed against nothing.

I felt around. There was no bottom. The barrel was just a ring of staves, standing on the floor like a chimney. And under my feet, where the bottom should have been, my fingers found smooth wooden boards, and the cold iron ring of a handle.

It was a trapdoor.

Mabb hadn't hidden me in a barrel. She'd hidden me in her cellar door.

\* \* \*

I pulled the ring. The trapdoor came up an inch with a creak that sounded, in that barrel, about as loud as the sneeze, but out in the room, Silvaine was asking the barrel whether it had any family, and Mabb was telling her it was a barrel, and nobody heard.

Under the trapdoor was a ladder. I went down it in the dark, one rung at a time, lowering the trapdoor back over my head with my fingertips, and stepped off onto a cold stone floor.

It was Mabb's cellar. I could smell it before I could see anything: wood and dust and something sweet and strong and very expensive. When my eyes adjusted to the thin gray light coming down through a grate in the far wall, I saw why. The cellar was stacked from floor to ceiling with small, dark, squat casks, each stamped with a dwarven rune I didn't recognize, and not one of them stamped with the Concordance's tax seal.

Old Mabb, it turned out, had her own reasons for not liking inquisitors.

At the back of the cellar, half hidden behind a stack of casks, there was a gap in the wall where the stone gave way to something older. Brick. Small, square, red-brown bricks, laid by hand, in low round arches. It was the same kind of tunnel I'd run through with forty geese behind me, under Lord Thandril's wine cellar. It ran off into the dark, under the city.

Above me, I heard the tavern door bang open. Running footsteps. Then Silvaine's voice, crisp and satisfied: "Thank you. Proprietor, I have here a Writ of Inspection, duly issued and sealed."

I went into the tunnel. I didn't wait to hear any more.

But I heard it anyway, later. Brakka told me the whole thing. Silvaine had read the writ aloud, every word. Then she'd pried the nails out of the lid with a little silver tool she carried for the purpose, and lifted it off, and looked down into the barrel.

It was empty. And at the bottom, where the bottom should have been, was an open trapdoor, and below it, a ladder, and below that, a cellar full of untaxed dwarven brandy.

Silvaine stood there for a long moment, according to Brakka, looking down into the hole. Then she straightened up and reasoned it through, out loud, step by step, the way she had with the crystal in the Hall of Crowns.

Someone had been in the barrel. That was certain; she had heard them, and the barrel had sneezed. That someone was now gone. And throughout the entire interrogation, there had been only one person in the Golden Bird who had stood beside the barrel, and answered on its behalf, and claimed it as her own.

She turned to Mabb. She looked, for a long and thoughtful moment, at Mabb's ears, which were dwarven ears, and which were, as Silvaine had noted in her ledger, insufficiently pointed.

And she arrested her. And the brandy.

According to Brakka, Mabb went quietly, with her arms folded, and on her way out the door, she told the orcs at the dice table to mind the bar.

I didn't know any of that yet. I was underground.

The tunnel ran on for what felt like miles, under the lower city, in the dark. Here and there, iron grates in the ceiling let in a little gray light and a lot of falling water, and I could hear the storm hammering on the streets above. But the tunnel itself was dry. Whoever had built it had built it well. It sloped up, gently, toward the palace, and branched, and branched again, and once I passed a little alcove with a stone shelf in it and a rusted iron hook on the wall, like a place where somebody had once hung a lantern every night.

I came up, eventually, through a grate in the corner of the palace kitchen garden, behind the herb beds. It was the middle of the night. I was covered in dust, smelled powerfully of brandy, and was perfectly, completely dry. My ears were exactly where I'd left them.

I had walked home under the Silverwood through tunnels humans had built, and nobody had seen me at all.

\* \* \*

Mabb was released at dawn. Silvaine always releases them; she's very fair about it, once she's made her mistake.

I went down to the Golden Bird that morning, in the rain, with a hood up over Gwendolyn and Brakka holding a very large oilcloth over both of us, and found Mabb behind her own bar again, wiping it down as if nothing had happened. I paid her fines for the brandy. They were considerable. She took the coins without comment, and counted them, and didn't give any of them back this time.

Then I told her about the tunnel.

She didn't even look up from the bar. She just shrugged.

"Everyone down here knows what's under the city, Prince," she said. "Half the cellars in the lower city open onto that brick. My grandfather found a little shoe down there once, when he was a boy. A child's shoe. Old as anything." She wiped a ring off the bar with her rag. "We just don't talk about it."

"Why not?"

"Because lords never ask," said Mabb.

She didn't ask why I'd been in her barrel. She didn't ask what I'd been so afraid of. She just poured me an ale I hadn't ordered, and set it in front of me, and looked at me with her small, shrewd, unimpressed eyes.

"Nobody gets arrested in my bar," she said. "Except me. That's the rule."

Outside, the rain hadn't stopped. It hadn't stopped all night. It was still coming down in long, steady, gray curtains, the kind of rain that settles in. On the way back up the hill, Brakka looked up at the sky from under the oilcloth for a long moment, and then said it plainly.

"The long rains," he said. "Early."

Back in my chambers, I sat down at my desk for the first time in five days, and pulled my lesson plan toward me, and picked up the silver pen.

Under *Assessment*, in my own handwriting from my first night in the Silverwood, it still said: *Still alive by Friday.*

I crossed it out. And underneath it, I wrote a new line.

*Objective 2: Stop the war.*

Silvaine had arrested forty-four people, and been wrong forty-four times. But the last time, she'd been standing right next to the truth. And the truth had been holding its ears.

I'd walked home under the city through tunnels that humans had built. The elves had been walking on top of them for three hundred years without ever looking down. I was starting to think that described the whole Silverwood.

## Interlude: The Ledger

**From the Ledger of Inquisitor Silvaine of House Thistlevane**

**ENTRY 44**

**Suspect:** Mabb, proprietor of the Golden Bird (formerly the Golden G—), lower city.

**Evidence:**

1. Ears insufficiently pointed.
2. Was near a barrel.
3. Answered questions on the barrel's behalf, repeatedly, and without being asked.
4. Claimed ownership of the barrel.
5. Barrel sneezed. Suspect stated that the barrel "had a cold." *(Note: barrels do not catch colds. Inquiry ongoing.)*
6. Harbored untaxed dwarven brandy, which is not evidence of being the Blight, but is certainly evidence of something.

**Procedure:** Writ of Inspection obtained and executed in full accordance with Section Nine of the Inquisitorial Code. Lid removed with the proper instrument. All forms correctly filed. *(Procedure was flawless.)*

**Persons present at the scene:** Assorted orcs (dice). Two dwarves (horse). A miller. His Highness the Prince's sword-bearer, attendant, and ritualist, who were very still. The Prince's goat. His Highness the Prince himself, who, according to his sword-bearer, had "stepped out to look at the rain" shortly before my arrival, and was therefore not near the barrel.

**Outcome:** Suspect released at dawn. Brandy confiscated as evidence. Evidence subsequently found to be significantly reduced. Inquiry into evidence ongoing.

**Expenses:** One tankard of ale, provided at the scene by the ritualist Durgan Pell. *(He has since sent an invoice. It is for two crowns. I have not paid it.)*

**Investigations to date:** 44.
