# Chapter 1: Best in Show

The thing nobody tells you about elf ears is that they're held on by faith and a two-dollar bottle of glue.

I was learning this in the second-floor men's room of the Radisson in downtown Raleigh, which had been taken over for the weekend by FaeCon, the Triangle's third-largest fantasy convention. I was leaning so close to the mirror that my breath fogged a little circle where my face should have been. Spirit gum, for the uninitiated, is the adhesive of choice for serious cosplayers, theater kids, and anyone who has ever needed to stick something fake to their own head. It smells like a pine tree that's been drinking. You paint it on, wait for it to go tacky, press the prosthetic into place, and hold it there while you think about your choices.

I owned a big bottle of the professional stuff. It was on the bathroom counter of my apartment off Hillsborough Street, next to my phone charger, about a mile and a half away. What I had with me was the travel size, which holds about as much as a thimble and costs as much as a sandwich.

"Come on," I told the left ear. "We talked about this."

The left ear peeled up at the tip. I pressed it back down and counted to thirty in my head, which is a thing I do with seventh graders when somebody needs a minute before they say something they'll regret. It works on ears about as well as it works on twelve-year-olds, which is to say, eventually.

My supervising teacher, Mrs. Okafor, says I have "a gift for redirecting energy." She said it the week I got Jaylen Price to stop throwing erasers by appointing him Official Eraser Monitor, with a lanyard. I think about it more than I should. Nobody had ever told me I had a gift for anything before.

Inventory.

Ears: latex, party store, $8.99, reshaped with a heat gun and a level of patience I could never find for grading. Wig: platinum blonde, waist length, named Gwendolyn by my roommate for reasons he refused to explain. Tunic: forest green, with silver vines I'd embroidered on the couch every night since June while Netflix asked if I was still watching. Cape: a king-size bedsheet I'd dyed midnight blue in our bathtub, which was now also midnight blue. Sword: foam, carved and painted to look like Glaivenmoor, the Moonblade of Aelindor, Last Prince of the Silverwood, from *Elderlight Chronicles III*, the greatest video game ever made. I will not be taking questions.

And glitter. I want to be clear that I had been told, by several people who loved me, that I was using too much glitter. I want to be equally clear that those people were wrong. The game's codex describes Aelindor as *radiant*. You can't do radiant halfway.

I stepped back and took in the whole picture.

Here's what I saw: a 23-year-old guy, six feet tall and built like the offensive lineman I was never coordinated enough to become, wearing an elven prince he'd spent four months building instead of writing lesson plans. And I want to say this plainly, because it matters for everything that happened after.

I looked great.

I raised the foam sword. I dropped my voice into Aelindor's register, the one the voice actor uses right before the final boss, and said, "*Thal'vorin aneshka!* The Silverwood stands!"

At the next sink, a man dressed as a very tired wizard had stopped washing his hands. He stared at me in the mirror. The water kept running over his fingers.

"Big contest today," I told him.

He turned off the tap and left without drying his hands.

I'd gotten the same reaction from my second-period class. I took it as a good sign.

\* \* \*

Dev was waiting outside the bathroom, which was impressive, because Dev was dressed as a robot made entirely of cardboard boxes and had to rotate his whole body to look at anything.

"Your cape's tucked into your tunic," he said. His voice came out of a dryer-vent hose where his mouth should be.

"It is not."

"It's a little bit tucked into your tunic." He reached over with a robot hand, which was a dish glove stapled to a shoebox, and tugged. "There. Now you're a prince."

Dev and I have been roommates since sophomore year, when the housing office matched us based on a survey where we both described ourselves as "neat" and "early risers," which turned out to be two lies each. He's a computer science major. He wired the LED vines into my tunic, which stopped working on Tuesday, and he's the kind of friend who'll spend a Saturday inside a box because you asked him to walk the convention floor with you.

I didn't know yet how much I was going to miss him. You never do.

The Radisson's lobby and ballroom level held a few thousand people in various states of armor, wings, and sleep deprivation. It smelled like popcorn, foam rubber, and the quiet desperation of people who'd been hot-gluing things at four in the morning. I loved it. I'd loved it since I was fourteen and came to my first convention as a very unconvincing wizard in my mom's bathrobe.

I left a trail. Every step, a fine shimmer drifted off me and settled on the carpet, the people around me, and Dev.

"You're shedding," Dev said.

"I'm sharing."

We stopped at the coffee cart, where a medium cold brew cost $6.75. I tipped twenty percent. The guy running the cart had the eyes of someone who'd been asked about oat milk four hundred times since dawn, my mother raised me right, and I'm bad at math under pressure. That left $3.12 in my checking account until the fifteenth. Student teaching is a full-time job that pays nothing. In fact, I pay tuition to do it. I had a box of off-brand cereal at the apartment called Frosted Wheat Rectangles. I'd be fine.

I pulled out my phone to check the contest schedule. Battery: 41%.

"Did you bring a charger?"

"I'm a robot, Marcus," Dev said. "I *am* the charger."

He was not the charger. I switched to low power mode, as if that has ever saved anyone.

Near the *Elderlight* booth, a guy in a merch hoodie squinted at me the way people squint at a typo. "Aelindor's sword is silver," he said. "Not blue."

"In the base game," I said. "After the Moonwell questline in the expansion, the blade takes on the color of the moon of your birth month. I'm September. Harvest moon. It's blue."

He opened his mouth. He closed it. He took out his phone and looked it up, and I watched him read.

"Huh," he said.

I'm bad at a lot of things: parallel parking, cooking anything that isn't cereal, saying no to my supervising teacher. But I have played *Elderlight III* eleven times, including once using only a frying pan, and I know that world better than I know the fire exits in my own apartment.

Behind the hoodie guy, the *Elderlight* booth had hung a banner the length of a minivan, printed with the only thing Elliot Marsh has ever said in public. Marsh wrote all three games, and he's famous for being impossible to find. He gave exactly one interview, in 2009, to a gaming magazine that doesn't exist anymore, and when they asked him where the world of *Elderlight* came from, he said: SOME STORIES YOU CAN ONLY TELL IF EVERYONE THINKS YOU MADE THEM UP.

The booth was also selling it on tote bags, for twenty-five dollars. Next to the tote bags sat a stack of Collector's Edition Codexes, leather-bound replicas of the books from the game's royal library, for three hundred. Fans have argued about what Marsh meant for seventeen years. The studio had clearly settled the question. He meant merchandise.

I had my own theory. My theory was that he was being pretentious.

I was wrong about that, as it turned out. I was wrong about a lot of things that day.

\* \* \*

The costume contest check-in was a folding table outside the Sovereign Crystal Grand Ballroom, which was neither crystal nor sovereign and had carpet the color of a hospital waiting room, staffed by a volunteer who handed me a sticker without looking up. HELLO MY NAME IS. I wrote AELINDOR in my best whiteboard capitals and pressed it over my heart.

First prize was five hundred dollars and a year's supply of Mana Surge, the energy drink sponsoring the contest. I didn't care about the Mana Surge. Five hundred dollars was rent.

The line wrapped down the hallway, and I did what I always do in lines, which is grade everyone. I can't help it. It's a sickness. A Viking in real chainmail: A. Two college girls in a two-person dragon: A-minus, points off because the back half kept walking into the front half. A guy in a hoodie with cat ears: incomplete, see me after class.

Then I saw Trent.

I didn't know his name was Trent yet, but he looked like a Trent. He was six-foot-three of willowy elegance, airbrushed to a pale, poreless glow. His ears were silicone, sculpted and blended so perfectly into his skin that they looked grown. His robes were real silk. He had his own photographer, a girl with two cameras who kept saying "chin" at him.

I graded him on sight: A-plus, craftsmanship. That costume cost more than my car, and it was worth it.

He noticed me noticing. He looked me up and down, slowly, the way you'd read a menu in a language you didn't speak.

"Aelindor?" he asked.

"Aelindor."

"Huh." He smiled with half his face. "Bold choice."

"Thank you."

"I just mean," he said, with a little wave that took in all of me, "elves don't really look like that."

There it was. I'd heard some version of it my whole life, in gym class, at the beach, in the comment section under every cosplay photo I'd ever posted. You learn to let it slide off. You also learn, if you're me, to bring receipts.

"Modern elves, sure," I said. "But have you read the Codex of the Old Kings? It's in the royal library in the Silverwood, second floor. Aelindor's descended from the First Line, and the codex says the Old Kings were 'mighty of stature and great of appetite.' They were huge. The skinny elves came later. Aelindor's a throwback."

Trent blinked. "That's a deep cut."

"I teach history. Primary sources are kind of my thing."

I could have stopped there. I'd won. But here's the thing I've learned from a semester of seventh graders: you don't make an enemy of the kid who's acting out. You find the thing he's proud of and ask him about it.

"Your ear blending is incredible, though," I said. "Seriously. How'd you hide the seam?"

Trent's whole face changed. For the next two minutes, he walked me through silicone thickening agents, edge feathering, and an alcohol-activated paint I could never afford. He was a nice guy under there. Most people are, if you hand them a lanyard.

I revised his grade: A-plus, craftsmanship. B, attitude. Showing improvement.

That was when I felt a tug on my cape.

She was about nine, missing a front tooth, wearing a homemade dragon hoodie with felt spikes down the back. Her dad stood behind her, holding a paper bag of kettle corn and looking apologetic.

"Are you the elf prince?" she asked.

I drew myself up. "I am Aelindor, Last Prince of the Silverwood."

She studied me with total seriousness. Then she said, loud enough for the whole line to hear, "You're the best elf here."

Next to me, Trent inhaled sharply.

I knelt, which in a bedsheet cape is a process, and looked her in the eye. "That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me all year. And I've had a year."

I opened the pouch on my belt, took out the little vial of backup glitter, and tapped a pinch into her palm. "Royal glitter," I said. "It's radiant."

She stared at it like I'd handed her a diamond. Her dad mouthed *thank you* over her head. Her name, he told me, was Lily.

That's the part of teaching nobody puts in the brochure. It isn't the curriculum. It's that face.

I was still kneeling when a man in an inflatable dinosaur suit turned around too fast. His tail swept a water bottle out of someone's hand, and it hit me square on the side of the head.

Cold water ran down my neck and under the wig. And I felt it: the left ear, lifting. Just a millimeter at the edge. Then two.

I clamped my palm over it and smiled at the dinosaur, who was apologizing through a mesh window in his own neck. Spirit gum hates water. Every cosplayer knows that. I pressed, and I counted to thirty, and I prayed to whatever gods watch over latex.

The ear held. Barely.

I made a mental note to avoid fountains, rain, and sad movies for the rest of the day.

\* \* \*

The Sovereign Crystal Grand Ballroom held about six hundred chairs, and every one was full. The stage was a plywood runway with a black curtain at the back, two spotlights, and a judges' table off to the side. The host was a tired man in a polo shirt who had been mispronouncing names since noon and had stopped apologizing around two.

Trent went right before me.

He was perfect. I mean that. He glided down the runway like the floor owed him money, his robes moving in slow, expensive ripples, and he struck three poses at the end that looked like oil paintings. His photographer's flash went off like a strobe. The judges nodded. The audience clapped politely, the kind of applause you give a very good cello recital.

"Up next," the host read off his card, "we have Ay-LIN-door?"

"Aelindor," I said from the wings.

"*Ay*-lin-door," he said into the mic, with complete confidence, and waved me on.

Here's what a semester of student teaching taught me: the first thirty seconds decide whether the room is yours. Twenty-eight seventh graders can smell fear the way sharks smell blood, and the only defense is to walk in like you were expected.

So I walked onto that stage like I was expected.

The cape caught on the mic stand three steps in. I made it part of the move. I spun, swept it free in a wide arc, and threw a cloud of glitter into the spotlight. It hung in the light for one perfect second. Somebody in the third row said, "*Whoa*."

I drew the foam sword. I walked the runway slowly, chin up, scanning the crowd like they were my subjects and I was disappointed in some of them. At the end, I knelt to an invisible throne, rose, and swept the sword up toward the lights.

Then I gave them everything I had, from the diaphragm, the way my high school choir teacher taught me.

"*THAL'VORIN ANESHKA!* THE SILVERWOOD *STANDS!*"

The room exploded.

In the back, a cluster of *Elderlight* fans started chanting it back to me. Lily was on her dad's shoulders, shrieking and throwing her pinch of royal glitter into the air. Dev's cardboard arms were flailing so hard that one fell off. One of the judges, a woman in a professional armor build, had her head thrown back laughing, the good kind of laughing. In the front row, the tired wizard from the bathroom was clapping with damp hands. Even Trent was clapping. Only a little. But he was.

At the judges' table, three pens were moving across three clipboards. For the first time in my life, I was the one being graded, and I didn't mind.

I've been a lot of things in my life. The kid picked last. The guy who's "so funny." The student teacher whose supervisor has to remind him, gently, about his lesson plans.

For one minute, under those lights, I was the best elf in the world.

I wiped my forehead with the back of my wrist, very carefully, so I wouldn't touch the ears. They were holding. Gwendolyn was holding. The cape was holding. My life, briefly, was holding.

I started my bow.

I was halfway down when the floor started to glow.

Not stage lighting. Stage lighting comes from above. This came from underneath me, a ring of silver light spreading out across the plywood with me at the center, threaded with lines and symbols that looked almost like the runes in *Elderlight* and not quite like anything at all.

My first thought, and I'm not proud of it, was: *They finally sprang for pyro.*

My second thought was that the floor was warm. My third was that the floor was soft, like a trampoline, like the plywood had decided to stop being plywood.

"Uh," said the host, into the mic. "Is this part of the—"

The light got brighter. The audience made the noise crowds make when they can't tell if something is a special effect or an emergency, which is a sort of rising *oooh* with a question mark at the end.

I heard Dev yell my name. His robot head fell off and bounced down the aisle. Trent screamed, high and clear, like a teakettle. The judges were standing up. And Lily, on her dad's shoulders, was waving at me with both hands, absolutely delighted, because as far as she was concerned, this was the best part of the show.

I waved back. It seemed rude not to.

Then the floor let go.

I didn't fall down, exactly. I fell *through*. The ballroom, the lights, and six hundred people tore away like paper, and then there was wind, and the wind smelled like rain and pine needles and something sharp and electric I'd never smelled before.

The glitter came with me. It whirled around me in the silver light, a cloud of tiny stars, and for a second, falling through nothing, I was genuinely radiant. My name tag flapped against my chest. HELLO MY NAME IS: AELINDOR.

I had a lot of thoughts on the way down. Most of them were not useful. I wondered whether this counted as an absence, and who was going to cover my third period on Monday, and whether Mrs. Okafor would write it up as "failed to provide lesson plans." I wondered if Dev would remember to feed my fish. I wondered, briefly and with real grief, whether I'd won.

The wind roared. The light swallowed everything.

I held onto my ears. It seemed like the right priority.
