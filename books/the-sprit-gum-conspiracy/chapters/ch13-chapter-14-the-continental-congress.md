# Chapter 14: The Continental Congress

The most powerful weapon in American history isn't the musket. It's the committee.

I explained this to Thessa by the fire, on the night after we'd finally gotten the barometer working again.

That had taken some doing. Durgan had produced a new bottle, which wasn't the hard part. The hard part was the baseline. To know when the water in the neck was rising, I needed to know where it sat on a clear, steady day, and there hadn't been one since the barrel. Then, out of nowhere, on the eighteenth morning of the long rains, the clouds broke. For about six hours, the sky over the Silverwood was blue and still and perfect. The elves came out and stood in the courtyards blinking at it in mild disappointment, like people whose favorite show had been interrupted by an emergency broadcast. I sat on my balcony the whole time and watched the water, and when it had stayed put for six hours straight, I scratched a new line into the reed with my knife.

The clouds came back that evening. But I could see the weather again.

Now I sat by the fire with my lesson plan on my knee, and Thessa sat across from me on the rug with the little pot of calendula salve, and on the page in front of me was the line I'd written after the barrel: *Objective 2: Stop the war.*

Underneath it, I'd written a list of the ways I could do it. I'd crossed every single one out.

I couldn't fight the war. I had an army of one orc, one apprentice shaman, one dwarf, and a goat. I couldn't refuse to lead it; if I stepped down, command went to Venrick, and Venrick would march north the next morning, happily. And I couldn't tell the truth. The second I told the truth, I'd be the first casualty of the war I was trying to stop.

"So what's left?" Thessa asked.

"Time," I said. "I need to buy time. As much as I can get."

"How?"

So I told her about the thirteen colonies.

I told her about thirteen little places on the edge of a big ocean, who couldn't stand each other: who had different religions, and different money, and different accents, and argued about everything. And when they needed to act together, against a king across the sea, they didn't start by acting. They started by sending people to a room in Philadelphia to write something together. And then those people argued about every word of it. For months. They argued about who should have to pay for things, and how to count people, and what to call themselves, and whether a sentence should end in one place or another. Some of those arguments went on for years.

"Nothing in the world," I told her, "slows a group of powerful people down like asking them to agree on wording."

Thessa considered this with her serious, tree-talking face.

"So you're going to make them write a letter," she said slowly.

"A very, very long letter."

"And they'll argue about it."

"For as long as I can possibly make them."

She thought about it a while longer. Then she smiled, for the first time all evening, a slow, small, wicked smile I hadn't known she had in her.

"Highness," she said, "that is the most boring plan I have ever heard."

"Thank you," I said. "That's the whole idea."

\* \* \*

Two days later, at council, Caelith asked me again.

It was the fourth time. He did it the way he always did, at the very end of the session, pleasantly, as the lords were gathering up their papers. "Highness. The Houses grow impatient. When will the rains end?"

This time, I was ready.

I didn't answer right away. I closed my eyes, and lifted my hand slowly, and laid it over my left ear. I'd learned that the Listening worked best if you let it run long, so I let it run long. I counted to seven. I could hear the room go quiet around me, and somewhere down the table, I heard at least three lords quietly lift their own hands to their ears.

Then I opened my eyes.

"The rains will end," I said, "at the second new moon."

A murmur went around the table, and I watched the High Lords of the Silverwood do arithmetic on their fingers. The moon had been new four days ago. The second new moon from now was nearly two months off. Seven weeks, give or take.

I'd picked it on purpose. It was the latest date I thought I could get away with. The long rains were supposed to last about two months, Brakka had said, and they'd started early, so the second new moon was plausible. It was just at the far, generous end of plausible. And I'd checked the moon myself. I'd taught the phases of the moon to my seventh graders, with a flashlight and a Styrofoam ball on a pencil. A new moon comes every twenty-nine and a half days, like clockwork. That part I could predict. The rain was another matter.

I had no way of knowing if the rains would actually end then. A barometer can't see two months ahead. If they stopped early, I'd look like a fraud. If they went on late, I'd look like a fraud. But either way, the army wouldn't march for seven weeks, and seven weeks was a lot of time.

Venrick was frowning. "Seven weeks," he said, with disgust.

Caelith only nodded, slowly. "Then we have seven weeks to make ready," he said.

"About that," I said.

Every head turned back to me.

I'd practiced this part in the mirror, the way I'd practiced the battle cry. I made my voice slow and grave and reasonable, Aelindor's voice at the council table in the third game.

"When the host of the Silverwood marches north," I said, "it'll march under one banner. All the Houses together. And I wonder if, before it does, the Houses shouldn't agree. In writing. On what, exactly, the host is marching for."

Silence.

"A charter," I said. "A Charter of the March. Setting out its purpose. Its command. Its provisions." I paused, very slightly. "And the division of the northern lands, once the Blight is gone."

If you've ever opened a can of tuna in a house with cats, you'll know exactly what happened next. Every lord at that table leaned forward at once. Lord Thandril of House Velloran, who'd come back from the country for this session, actually half rose out of his chair. Even Venrick stopped frowning.

The division of the northern lands. In writing. With their names on it.

The only person who didn't lean forward was Caelith. He sat back in his chair and looked at me for a long moment with that chess-game expression, and I had the uncomfortable feeling that he could see every gear turning in my head.

Then he smiled.

"An excellent idea, Highness," he said. "Let it be written."

I found out later why he'd agreed so easily. At the time, I was just relieved.

They appointed me to chair the drafting committee, since it had been my idea. They appointed Lord Merrow, the Treasurer, as its clerk, since he had the neatest handwriting in the Silverwood, which he accepted with the expression of a man being handed a shovel and pointed at a very large hole. And the Houses agreed to meet the next morning, in the Hall of Silver Boughs, which was the only room large enough.

It belonged to House Vaunt, of course. Caelith charged us rent.

\* \* \*

I've chaired exactly one meeting in my life before that one. It was the eighth-grade student council, where I was elected treasurer, unopposed, because nobody else wanted to count the money from the bake sale. But I'd watched our advisor, Mr. Pruitt, run those meetings for a whole year, and he'd run them by the book. And the book, it turned out, was the best weapon I had.

On the first morning, before anybody could propose anything, I taught the High Houses of the Silverwood the rules of order.

I taught them motions, and seconds. I taught them that you had to be recognized by the chair before you could speak, and that the chair would say *the chair recognizes Lord So-and-so*. I taught them amendments, and amendments to amendments, and how to call the question, and what a point of order was. I wrote it all on a big sheet of parchment and had Brakka pin it to the wall.

I'd expected them to resent it. They loved it.

Elves, it turns out, are crazy about procedure. Within an hour, Lady Ithrien was raising her hand to be recognized with the delight of a child with a new toy. By lunch, "Point of order" had become the most fashionable phrase in the hall, and I heard two young lords from House Calloway using it on each other in the corridor afterward, in an argument about a horse. Within a week it had spread to the whole court. I'd once seen a lady at dinner raise a point of order about the soup.

And then the drafting began.

It took three days to agree on a title.

House Aldervane wanted *The Charter of the March*, which was what I'd called it. House Velloran wanted *The Articles of Restoration*, since the war was, after all, restoring the north to its rightful condition. And House Calloway made an impassioned case for *The Proclamation of the Righteous Host Against the Blight*, which was long, which I liked, because long things take longer to argue about. There were speeches. There were amendments. Lord Thandril of Velloran gave a forty-minute address on the noble meaning of the word *restoration*. On the third afternoon, the vote went to *The Charter of the March*, by one, and House Velloran refused to speak to House Aldervane for a week.

Then I proposed the preamble.

I'd borrowed it, of course. I'd taken the opening of my favorite document in the world, which every seventh grader in my class had to memorize the first part of. I translated it, as best I could, into something that sounded like the Silverwood. And I'd stood up, in my best Aelindor voice, and begun to read: *We hold these truths to be self-evident...*

Venrick's hand went up before I'd finished the sentence.

"The chair recognizes Lord Venrick," I said.

He rose. He looked genuinely puzzled, which was a new look for him. "If these truths are self-evident, Highness," he said, "then why must we write them down?"

It was, I have to admit, a pretty good question. It took two days to answer, and we never really did.

After that came the provisions. The Starlight faction wanted the Charter to promise that the army would be *sustained* on the march. The Old Blood faction wanted it to say *fed*. The Starlights felt that *fed* was vulgar and implied pie; the Old Bloods felt that *sustained* implied dew, and that you could not march an army on dew. Lady Ithrien and Venrick, at the heads of their factions, argued about it for four days through Lord Merrow, since they still weren't speaking to each other directly. Lord Merrow, who had to repeat every insult word for word for the record, aged about a century.

And then there was the comma.

It was in the article on command, in a list of the Houses that would provide the host's generals: *the Houses of Vaunt, Aldervane and Velloran.* House Velloran noticed, on the second reading, that there was no comma after *Aldervane*. And Lord Thandril rose, trembling with outrage, to point out that without it, the sentence could be read as though Aldervane and Velloran were a single entity, sharing a single general between them.

House Aldervane said that was ridiculous. House Velloran demanded a comma. House Aldervane said that adding a comma would imply that Velloran thought itself equal to Aldervane, which was even more ridiculous. It went on for a week. There were three formal motions, two duels of petition, and one afternoon when Lord Thandril and Lord Venrick both rose to be recognized at exactly the same moment and stood there, glaring, refusing to sit down, for nineteen minutes. I know, because I timed it.

I've never in my life been so happy to watch a punctuation argument.

The committee had its dangerous moments. Twice, a vote came so close to passing that the whole Charter might have been finished in a single afternoon. And both times, I'd look across the hall at Thessa, who sat at a small table beside Lord Merrow, taking notes. And she'd pass a folded slip of paper down the row toward Durgan, and on it would be one word: *Now.*

And Durgan would rise, and clear his throat, and say: "Point of information."

Then he would begin to read the bylaws of the Guild of Arcane Conveyance aloud, in full, from the beginning. There were two hundred and twelve sections. He read every one of them in a slow, sonorous, deeply sincere voice, pausing only to sip from his flask, and he read the footnotes too. The first time, it took four hours. Nobody could stop him, because I'd taught them the rules, and the rules said a point of information could not be interrupted.

Brakka, meanwhile, had been appointed sergeant-at-arms, which he took with tremendous seriousness. He stood by the door of the Hall of Silver Boughs every day in full armor, and on one occasion, when Lord Aestrin of House Calloway was caught slipping two crowns into Lord Merrow's pocket during a close vote, Brakka picked him up under one arm and carried him out of the chamber.

"Bribery," Brakka explained to me afterward, gravely. "Tipping the clerk during a vote."

And outside the tall windows of the Hall of Silver Boughs, every day, the rain kept falling.

\* \* \*

But the title, and the preamble, and the provisions, and even the comma were nothing. They were the warm-up. The real fight started in the fourth week, when we reached the article on the division of the north.

That was the morning the maps came out.

Lord Merrow had them brought up from the treasury: big, new surveys of the northern wastes, drawn by the army's scouts, showing every valley and frozen lake and stretch of forest between the Silverwood and the edge of the world. They were unrolled across the long table in the Hall of Silver Boughs and weighted down at the corners with inkwells. And the lords of the High Houses crowded around them, and picked up their pens.

And they started to write their names on the north.

I stood at the head of the table and watched it happen, and I felt something cold go through me, from the top of my head right down to the soles of my feet.

I'd seen this before.

Not something like it. This. Exactly this. The same neat black ink. The same careful handwriting across valleys and rivers and stretches of forest. *Vaunt. Aldervane. Velloran. Calloway.* I'd sat on the cold stone floor of the iron room three weeks earlier and looked at maps exactly like these, drawn up two years before the Purge, with the names of these same Houses written across the farms and villages of people who were still living in them.

It was happening again. In front of me. In a room I'd rented, at a table I was chairing, because I'd asked them to do it.

I did one thing, that first morning, before I let it go on. I rapped on the table for order, and when the room had quieted, I asked the question that nobody else had asked.

"Shouldn't someone," I said, "speak for the people who live in the north?"

There was a silence. A few of the lords looked at each other, puzzled, as if I'd asked whether someone should speak for the weather.

Then Venrick, without looking up from the map: "There is no one in the north, Highness."

"Except the one the patrol saw," Lady Ithrien added helpfully.

A few lords laughed. Then they picked up their pens again.

Forty books about a people, I thought, and not one word from the people. And now a map of their country, and not one of them at the table.

And here's the part I'm not proud of: it was the best stall of all. It worked better than the title, better than the self-evident truths, better than the comma. Because the comma had only been about pride. The maps were about land, and land was what the Houses really wanted, and there wasn't one of them who'd give up a single valley without a fight. House Calloway and House Velloran fought for a week over a frozen lake called Kell Tarn. House Aldervane claimed a river, and then claimed both banks of it, and then claimed the fish. Every lord who came to the table wanted more than the lord beside him, and every claim led to a counter-claim, and every counter-claim led to a motion, and every motion led to a vote that ended in a tie.

I let it. I did more than let it. As chair, I recognized the angriest lords first. I called for a fresh survey whenever two claims overlapped. I suggested, very reasonably, that perhaps each House should submit its claims in writing, with justifications, for the committee's review. It bought me another two weeks.

I was buying time with the same greed that had started the Purge. I knew it. I went to bed every night feeling a little sick about it, and got up every morning and did it again.

Late one night, near the end of the sixth week, I stayed behind in the Hall of Silver Boughs after everyone else had gone, sitting alone at the long table, looking down at the maps. The lamps had burned low. The northern wastes were covered in so many names now, in so many colors of ink, that in places you couldn't see the land underneath at all.

I didn't hear him come in. I never did.

"You've kept them arguing for six weeks, Highness," said Caelith.

He was standing at the far end of the table, in the shadow just past the lamplight, with his hands clasped behind his back. I don't know how long he'd been there.

I thought about pretending I didn't know what he meant. Then I decided I was too tired.

"Democracy's slow," I said.

It came out in English. There wasn't a word for it in the tongue-binding, I think. Caelith tilted his head slightly at the strange sound of it, but he didn't ask.

"So it is," he said. He walked slowly along the table, looking down at the maps, and stopped beside me. He reached out one long, elegant finger and laid it on the northernmost edge of the parchment, where the scouts' drawings ran out into blank paper.

"But it does end," he said. "And when it ends, Highness, every House in the Silverwood will have signed its name to the north. In writing. Before witnesses. Under the seal of the Lost Prince of the First Line." He looked at me, and smiled his lovely, empty smile. "Legally. Forever."

I understood then.

He'd known what I was doing all along. From the very first day. He'd let me do it, because a signed charter was worth more to him than seven weeks. The last time the Houses had carved up a country, they'd had to do it in a sealed room, with a crossed-out line and a forged report. This time, they'd be doing it in the open, with a Charter, with my seal on it, all perfectly legal. I hadn't been stalling Caelith's war.

I'd been writing the deed.

"You're letting me stall," I said.

"I'm letting you *help*, Highness," said Caelith gently.

Then he inclined his head, the precise bow of a High Lord to a Prince, and walked away into the dark, and left me there alone with the maps.

\* \* \*

I didn't sleep that night. I sat at my desk with a lamp and the final draft of the Charter of the March, which Lord Merrow had copied out fair in his beautiful handwriting, all fourteen articles of it, ready for the signing in two days.

The north was going to be divided. I couldn't stop that anymore. Caelith had seen to it. But there was one thing I'd learned in eighth grade, and again in the iron room, and again at the archery trial, and it was this: nobody reads the whole document. Everybody reads the part with their own name on it.

So I turned to Article Nine.

Article Nine was called *On the Provisioning and Conveyance of the Host*, and it was the most boring thing I'd ever read, and I had read every word of the Guild bylaws, because Durgan had read them to me, twice. It was eleven pages long. It was about wagons. It specified the number of wagons per company, and the number of horses per wagon, and the correct grade of axle grease, and the oat ration for each horse, and the proper method of replacing a cracked wheel on the march. Nobody had argued about Article Nine. Nobody had even read it aloud. It had passed in four minutes on a Tuesday afternoon, while Lord Thandril and Lord Venrick were still glaring at each other about the comma.

On the seventh page, in the middle of a long paragraph about axle grease, I picked up the silver pen and added one sentence, in Lord Merrow's handwriting, as carefully as I could copy it.

It took me forty minutes and six practice sheets. About halfway through, it occurred to me that I was doing exactly what someone had done to the fire report three hundred years ago: changing an official document, in someone else's hand, by lamplight, where no one would see. The difference, I told myself, was what my sentence said. I mostly believed it. Then I burned the practice sheets in the fire.

Two days later, the Charter of the March was signed.

It was a grand ceremony, in the Hall of Silver Boughs, with every High Lord of the Silverwood in his finest robes and the rain drumming on the tall windows. Lord Merrow read out the title, and the preamble about self-evident truths, which everyone had finally agreed to leave in on the grounds that it sounded nice. There were nine days left until the second new moon. Then each House came forward in turn to sign and seal it. And I watched every one of them turn straight to the article with their own name in it, and read it closely, lips moving, checking every valley and lake and river, and then flip past everything else to the end, and sign.

Not one of them read Article Nine. Not one.

Caelith signed last. He took the pen from Lord Merrow, and turned to the final page, and signed the name of his House with a long, graceful stroke: *Vaunt.* The V at the beginning had a little flourish on its tail.

I'd seen that flourish before, in the margin of a report, on the cold floor of the iron room.

Under the table, Thessa reached over and squeezed my hand, once, hard. She'd seen it too.

And so the Charter of the March was signed and sealed by every High House of the Silverwood, and by me. And somewhere in Article Nine, between the axle grease and the oat rations, on the seventh page, there was now a sentence that read:

> *No people of the forest shall be put to the sword without trial before the assembled Houses.*

Every House had signed it. Not one of them had read it.

I thought about an eighth-grade writing test, and an essay about a sandwich. Nobody ever reads the rubric.

It took the thirteen colonies years to agree on how to govern themselves. I'd gotten the High Houses to spend a week on a comma. Mrs. Okafor would have given me an A.
