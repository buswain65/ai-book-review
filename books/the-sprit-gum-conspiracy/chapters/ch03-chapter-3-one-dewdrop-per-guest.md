# Chapter 3: One Dewdrop Per Guest

The first crisis of my reign was a bath.

I woke up to morning light coming through the balcony curtains and six elves standing around my bed in a neat semicircle, holding towels. They stood very still, like a choir waiting for its cue. I don't know how long they'd been there. I don't want to know.

"Good morning, Highness," said the one in the middle. "Your bath is drawn."

Across the room, the crystal tub was steaming like a hot spring. Someone had scattered rose petals on the water. It was beautiful. It was a death trap.

My hand went to my left ear before I'd fully sat up. Spirit gum plus hot water plus six witnesses equals a prince with no ears, and in this place, I was pretty sure, a prince with no ears was a prince with no head.

Here's something I learned student teaching: kids will argue with any rule you make up on the spot, but they'll follow any rule that sounds like it's always existed. You don't say, "Nobody leaves without a pass, starting today." You say, "You know the pass policy." They don't know the pass policy. But they'll nod like they do.

So I raised one hand, the way Aelindor does before he speaks to his council, and said, "Hold. You forget the Vow."

Six elves exchanged six quick, nervous looks.

"The Vow of the Moon," I said. "No eyes but the moon's may look upon the Prince unrobed. It has been so since the First Line."

The tongue-binding did something wonderful to that sentence. It came out low and rolling, half hymn and half legal contract, the kind of thing that ought to be carved over a doorway. One of the attendants actually dropped her towel. The one in the middle went pale, which on an elf is impressive.

"Forgive us, Highness," he said, bowing so low his hair touched the floor. "The records of the First Line are... incomplete."

"They always are," I said, with great sadness.

They began backing toward the door, bowing as they went. At the threshold, the youngest one paused.

"And during the day, Highness?" she asked, timidly. "When the moon is not in the sky?"

"Then I bathe at night," I said.

She nodded, as if this were deep wisdom, and closed the door.

The tailors came next. There were three of them, with measuring cords and armfuls of silver silk, and they lasted about four minutes. The head tailor got his cord around my chest, ran out of cord, and stared at the end of it in his hand as if it had personally betrayed him. The three of them stepped into a corner and conferred in urgent whispers. I caught the phrases "twice the silk" and "the looms" and, from the youngest, "I don't think the looms go that wide."

I saved them the trouble. I smoothed my green tunic, straightened my bedsheet cape, and said, "These are the garments of my journey. I will wear nothing else until my purpose here is fulfilled."

They bowed with visible relief and fled.

Alone again, I sat on the edge of the bed with the travel bottle of spirit gum and re-glued my left ear, measuring out the drop like a pharmacist with a controlled substance. When I held the bottle up to the light afterward, there was barely enough left for two more tries.

I was going to need a plan for that. I put it on the list, right under "figure out where I am" and "don't die."

\* \* \*

The banquet was held in the Hall of the Eldertree, which was exactly what it sounds like: a hall built around a single living tree so enormous that its branches formed the ceiling. Floating lights drifted among the leaves like slow fireflies. Long tables of white wood ran the length of the room, and every seat was filled.

There must have been four hundred elves there, and every one of them looked like they'd been carved out of moonlight by someone with a lot of time on their hands. Silver hair. Silk in shades of pearl and frost. Cheekbones you could open mail with. Walking in wearing a bathtub-dyed bedsheet, I felt like a craft-fair project that had wandered into the Louvre.

I walked in like I was expected anyway. The hall rose to its feet as I passed.

They seated me at the high table, at the right hand of the silver-haired elf from the summoning chamber. By then I knew his name: High Lord Caelith Vaunt, head of the most powerful of the High Houses, which as far as I could tell made him the person actually running things around here.

"Highness," he said warmly, as I sat. "I trust you slept well."

"Very well, thank you."

"And I'm told," he added, in the same pleasant voice, "that the Vow of the Moon has returned to court."

It had been about eight hours. He already knew. I filed that away too.

"Some traditions are worth keeping," I said.

"Indeed," Caelith said, and smiled his lovely smile. "Some."

Across from me sat the tuning-fork lady from the summoning, whose name turned out to be Lady Ithrien. She gave me a gracious little nod. Venrick sat two seats down, pretending I didn't exist. And at the very far end of the hall, as close to the wine as a person could physically get, sat Durgan Pell, who raised his goblet to me with both hands.

It was then that I noticed the orcs.

They stood along the walls, in the shadows between the tree's great roots, about one every twenty feet. They wore heavy armor of dark iron, and they were the first orcs I'd ever seen that weren't polygons: gray-green skin, heavy brows, real tusks curving up from their lower jaws. Each of them was big enough to carry me under one arm.

Nobody looked at them. Not once. The elves moved around them like they were part of the furniture.

One stood by the kitchen doors. He was the biggest of them, with a face that was mostly scar and only one ear. And he was staring at those kitchen doors the way a dog stares at an oven.

I knew that look. It's the look of third period, ten minutes before lunch.

\* \* \*

Servers in gray glided out with crystal plates under silver domes and set one in front of each guest. On a signal from Caelith, four hundred domes lifted at once.

On my plate was a single dewdrop.

It sat dead center, perfectly round, trembling slightly. Beside it lay one violet petal.

Around the hall, elves murmured with appreciation. Lady Ithrien sighed as if she'd been handed a sunset.

I waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it.

"The first course of the Moon Feast," Lady Ithrien explained, seeing my face. "You'll forgive us, Highness. The court has kept the starlight fast for four hundred years. We of the High Houses take only what the sky provides: dew, petals, and moonlight." She let her eyes drift toward the orcs along the wall. "Grossness of appetite is for the lesser peoples."

"Of course," she added quickly, "the Old Kings were exempt. Everything was different in the heroic age."

I ate the dewdrop. It tasted like a dewdrop.

The second course was an empty plate, which a server held up beneath a gap in the leaves for about a minute so it could fill with moonlight before setting it reverently in front of me. The elves around me lowered their faces over their plates and breathed in deeply, eyes closed.

I hadn't eaten anything but a cold brew since yesterday morning, in another universe. My stomach made a noise like a garbage truck backing up. Lady Ithrien politely pretended not to hear it. Two seats down, Venrick smirked.

I've watched a lot of kids decide whether to ask a question in class. Most of them don't, because they're afraid of looking stupid. And then they go home not knowing the answer.

"Forgive me," I said to the table. "Is there a main course?"

The silence started at the high table and spread outward, table by table, like a dropped tray in a cafeteria. Lady Ithrien's hand went to her throat. Somewhere far away, Durgan froze with his goblet halfway to his mouth.

I was about to make something up, maybe "in the time of the First Line, we feasted," when Caelith laughed. It was a soft, charming laugh, and the whole room relaxed the instant they heard it.

"Of course, Highness," he said. "Forgive us. We've grown rather... refined in your absence." He lifted one hand without looking away from me. "Master Orrin."

The kitchen doors opened, and a frail old elf in a stained apron shuffled out, wringing his hands.

"A feast worthy of the Old Kings," Caelith told him.

Master Orrin stared at him. "A feast, my lord?" he whispered. "With... *food*?"

"With food."

The old cook clutched the doorframe. His eyes filled with tears. "Four hundred years," he said, to no one in particular, and then he turned and ran back into the kitchen faster than I would have thought he could move, shouting for boars.

It took about an hour. Someone was sent to wake up Quillon Ferreth for recipes. While we waited, I made small talk with Lady Ithrien about the weather, which as far as I could tell was always perfect. Then the doors banged open, and the food came out.

A whole roast boar on a silver platter, with an apple in its mouth. Loaves of dark bread as big as footballs. Pies with golden crusts. Roasted roots glazed in honey. A gravy boat the size of an actual boat.

The court recoiled. Lady Ithrien covered her mouth with her napkin. One elf at the next table actually fainted.

I didn't wait. I took a knife to that boar, tore a loaf of bread in half with my hands, and ate like a man who'd crossed between worlds on a cold brew. The hall watched the Prince eat the way you'd watch a meteor shower: with awe, and a little fear.

Then Lady Ithrien lowered her napkin, dipped the very tip of one finger in the gravy, and touched it to her tongue.

Her eyes rolled back in her head.

After that, it was over. Within ten minutes, four hundred of the most refined beings I had ever seen were tearing into bread and passing pies down the tables. Venrick sat with his arms folded, refusing to touch anything, while his stomach growled loud enough to be heard over the harps. Down at the far end, Durgan was weeping openly into a pork pie.

I noticed only one other person who didn't eat. Caelith sat beside me with an empty plate, sipping his wine and watching me enjoy myself, the way you'd watch an experiment run.

\* \* \*

When the boar was mostly bones, the servers refilled every goblet with a pale wine that smelled like honeysuckle, and Caelith rose to his feet. The hall fell quiet at once.

"To the Prince," he said, lifting his glass, "who returns to us in our hour of need."

Four hundred elves roared it back. I stood up and raised my glass with them and smiled, and the back of my brain quietly asked, *Hour of need?*

Then came more toasts. They always do. Lady Ithrien, glowing and slightly gravy-stained, stood up and toasted "the Old Kings, and their great appetite," and the hall laughed and cheered. An elf lord I didn't know toasted the Eldertree. Someone else toasted Master Orrin, who wept again.

Then Venrick stood.

He didn't smile. He lifted his untouched goblet and spoke in a cold, carrying voice.

"To the Purge," he said. "To the heroes who cleansed the forests of the Blight. And to the day we finish what they started."

The roar that answered him was louder than any before it. Elves pounded the tables. Along the walls, the orc guards didn't move, but I saw the one-eared orc by the kitchen door set his jaw.

I raised my glass, because everyone else was. Then I leaned across the table toward Lady Ithrien.

"Forgive me," I said quietly. "My memory is clouded by the Veil. So much of what I knew has faded." I gestured vaguely with my goblet. "What is the Blight, now?"

I'll never forget how happy she looked. There is nothing in any world a gossip loves more than being asked to explain something.

"Oh, Highness," she said. "Humans."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped stone.

She kept going, warming to her subject. Humans were short-lived, she said, barely eighty years, and they bred like rabbits to make up for it. They had round little ears and grasping little hands. They cut down the Eldertree's sisters for firewood and burned whole forests to plant their crops. They poisoned the rivers. They broke every treaty they ever signed. "Vermin," she said, in the tone you'd use for mice in a pantry. "Three hundred years ago the Concordance rose up and cleansed the land of them. Elves, orcs, dwarves, beastfolk, all of us together. Every last one."

She smiled and sipped her wine.

"Well," she added. "Every last one until this spring."

I thought about the tapestry over my bed. The ragged horde. The burning forest. The stubby, round ears.

Here's the thing about teaching history. Eventually, you teach the bad chapters. And when you do, you learn to recognize certain words. *Cleansed.* *Vermin.* *Every last one.* You find them in primary sources from the worst moments humans have ever had, and they're always spoken in the same voice: proud, and certain, by people who think they're the heroes of the story. I had a unit on it. I had a rubric.

I'd just never heard those words said about me.

I didn't say anything. I'm not sure I could have. I touched my left ear, the way you'd touch a pocket to make sure your wallet is still there. It was latex. It was holding. It was all that was standing between me and the end of Lady Ithrien's story.

Caelith rose again, and the hall quieted.

"This spring," he said, "one of our patrols in the northern wastes saw something that should not exist. A human. Alive."

Gasps. A goblet fell somewhere and shattered.

"For three hundred years, we believed the Blight was ended," Caelith went on, his voice rising. "We were wrong. Where there is one, there may be more. But in our hour of need, the prophecy is fulfilled. The Lost Prince has returned to us, and he will lead our armies north and end the Blight forever."

He turned to me. Four hundred faces turned with him.

"Highness?"

There's no good way to describe what it's like to be handed a moment like that, like a full glass you can't put down. I did the only thing I could do.

I raised my glass to my own extinction, and four hundred elves cheered.

And as they cheered, I glanced to my left, and found Caelith wasn't looking at the crowd at all. He was looking at me. He was watching me the way my supervising teacher watched my first observed lesson: politely, completely, and taking notes.

\* \* \*

About twenty minutes later, the court began to fall over.

It started with the elf lord who'd toasted the Eldertree. He set down his fork, said, "Oh," very softly, and slid sideways off his bench. Then two ladies at the next table went glassy-eyed and slumped against each other. Then a whole row. Within half an hour, the most elegant beings I'd ever seen were draped across the tables of the Hall of the Eldertree like laundry. One lord was asleep face-down in a pie.

Four hundred years of dewdrops, and then a boar. Their bodies simply didn't know what to do.

The royal physicians were summoned. They arrived, looked around, and pronounced it a spiritual awakening.

"The Old Blood stirs!" someone cried from beneath a table.

"The Old Blood stirs," the court murmured back, drowsily, as if it were a prayer.

Lady Ithrien, on her third slice of pie, gazed at me with wide, unfocused eyes. "Highness," she whispered. "I can see colors." She held out her empty plate. "Is there more?"

Durgan was asleep under the far table with his arms wrapped around a ham.

I excused myself, which nobody noticed, and walked out of the hall.

My route to the door took me past the kitchen, where servers were already clearing the leftovers onto carts, half a boar, three loaves, a whole untouched pie, to be thrown away. The one-eared orc was still at his post beside the door. He didn't turn his head as the carts went by, but his eyes followed every one of them, and as the boar passed, I heard his stomach growl. It sounded like distant thunder.

Four hundred elves had just eaten their first real meal in four centuries, and not one of them had thought to offer a plate to the people who'd been standing guard all night.

I didn't do anything about it, not that night. But I noticed. I'm a teacher. Noticing which kid didn't get lunch is half the job.

\* \* \*

Back in my chambers, I didn't light the floating lamps. I didn't need to. The moon was bright through the balcony curtains, bright enough to see the tapestry on the wall across from my bed.

The silver-armored elves. The burning forest. The ragged horde fleeing the trees with axes and torches in their hands.

I'd looked at it the night before and seen monsters. Now I looked closer, the way I'd teach a class to look at a primary source: who made this, and why, and what are they leaving out?

The horde didn't look like it was attacking. It looked like it was running. Some of the figures were carrying others. Near the bottom corner, half hidden by a stitched flame, one small figure was holding a bigger one's hand.

Every one of them had round ears.

I reached up and checked mine. Both still there. I checked them again.

Somewhere in the north, there was someone like me. And I'd just been put in charge of killing them.
