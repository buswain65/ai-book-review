# Chapter 19: Round Ears

There is no dignified way to take off your own ears in front of two hundred people.

I'd had all night to think about it. I hadn't slept much. I'd lain on the cold stone floor of the meeting hall with my tied hands tucked under my chin and listened to the wind, and to Thessa breathing on the bench beside me, and to the two guards by the door shifting their weight and muttering to each other, and I'd thought about how to do it. Whether to make a speech first. Whether to do it fast, or slow. Whether I should ask for a mirror.

In the end, I didn't plan anything. Some things, you can't lesson-plan.

They came for us at first light. Not to take us anywhere. The village came to us. By the time the sky outside the narrow windows had gone from black to gray, the meeting hall was full: every bench, every stretch of wall, the space around the hearth, the doorway, people standing on the long table at the back to see over the heads in front. I counted roughly two hundred. I think it was everyone in Hearthhold old enough to walk, and some who weren't, carried on hips and shoulders.

Ruth Ashgrove sat at the head of the long table, on a plain wooden chair, with four other elders on stools around her: two old men and two old women, gray and weathered and silent. Old Tobiah was there, wrapped in his four scarves, with the weatherglass in his lap. And Wenna stood just inside the door with her bow in her hands and an arrow on the string, pointed at the floor, but not very far from me.

They'd sat Thessa and me on a bench in front of the elders, facing them. With the whole of Hearthhold behind us.

Ruth looked at me for a long time. Then she said, "We've talked, the elders and I. Through the night." Her dry, quiet voice carried to every corner of the hall. "We've decided what to—"

"Can I show you something?" I said. "First?"

It came out louder than I meant. Somewhere in the crowd, somebody gasped at the interruption.

Ruth stopped. She looked at me with those steady, weather-measuring eyes.

"I'll need my hands," I said. "Just for a minute. You can tie them again after."

There was a long silence. I could hear two hundred people breathing.

Then Ruth nodded, once, very slightly. One of the guards stepped forward with a knife, and cut the cord around my wrists, and stepped back quickly. And at the door, I saw Wenna's arrow come up off the floor, and steady, and point straight at my chest.

I rubbed my wrists. I stood up. I turned around to face Hearthhold.

\* \* \*

I started with the left one. The crooked one.

I reached up and found the edge of it with my fingernails, at the top, where the resin always gave first. And I peeled.

It hurt, a little. Two months of glue and raw skin don't come apart quietly. There was a small, wet, unpleasant sound, like a bandage coming off, which in that perfectly silent hall sounded about as loud as a door slamming. The latex came away from my skin slowly, a quarter of an inch at a time, and then all at once, and then I was standing in front of two hundred people holding a long, pointed, slightly crooked elf ear in my hand.

Nobody made a sound.

I held it up so they could see it. Then I set it down on the long table beside Ruth, very carefully, the way you'd set down something fragile at a museum.

Then I did the right one.

Then I reached up and pulled off Gwendolyn: all of her, all that long, silver-blond, over-Purified, too-clean hair in one piece. And then I pulled off the wig cap underneath it, the tight beige mesh thing that had been flattening my real hair against my skull for three months. And I stood there, under the timber beams of the meeting hall of Hearthhold, with my own short, brown, badly flattened hair sticking up in about six directions, and my own round, small, ordinary, very red and raw ears.

Last, I lifted my sleeve and wiped the glitter off my face. It took a few tries. I'd been very generous with it.

Then I just stood there and let them look.

The silence went on for what felt like a full minute. I could hear the peat fire hissing at the far end of the hall. I could hear the wind. I could hear one baby, somewhere near the back, making a small curious sound. And two hundred people, every one of whom had been raised since birth on stories of tall, cruel, pointed-eared Leaf-Lords, stared at a twenty-three-year-old guy from North Carolina with bad hair and a rash.

It was a little girl who broke it. She was about four, sitting on her father's shoulders near the front, and she pointed at me with one mittened hand and said, at the absolute top of her lungs, with tremendous delight:

"*HIS HAIR CAME OFF!*"

The whole hall made a noise. Not a laugh, exactly. More like two hundred people letting out a breath they'd been holding for three hundred years.

And from somewhere in the middle of the crowd, a small, horrified voice, which I recognized instantly. It was the boy who'd kicked me in the shin.

"Mama," he whispered, very loudly. "Did I kick a *human*?"

"Yes," said his mother grimly, gripping his collar. "And you're going to apologize."

At the door, Wenna lowered her bow. She didn't put the arrow away. But she lowered it, slowly, until it pointed at the floor again, and she stared at me with her mouth slightly open, as if she was trying very hard to fit what she was seeing into anything she'd ever been told.

Someone reached out and picked up the left ear from the table. It was one of the young men from the forest, the one with the axe. He turned it over in his hands, and squeezed it, and looked at the inside of it, where the latex was stamped, very faintly, with a manufacturer's number. Then he handed it to the woman beside him. And she handed it on.

Within about five minutes, both of my ears were traveling around the meeting hall of Hearthhold from hand to hand, being examined, squeezed, sniffed, and held up to the light. And within about five more, they'd reached the children. And the children of Hearthhold, who had grown up with stories of the Leaf-Lords the way kids in Raleigh grow up with stories of the boogeyman, discovered that if you held a pointed ear up to the side of your head, you looked exactly like one.

They were delighted. A boy of about eight held them both up to his head and growled. A little girl wore one sideways. They took turns. They chased each other around the long table, shrieking, with my ears held up to their heads, playing Leaf-Lords and Hearthholders, the way kids back home play cops and robbers. Nobody stopped them.

Only one person in that hall didn't move at all, through any of it. Ruth Ashgrove sat in her plain wooden chair at the head of the table, with her rough hands folded in her lap, and watched me. Her face hadn't changed.

"A man who can take off his ears," she said quietly, "can put on anything."

The noise in the hall died away.

She was right. I knew she was right. I'd just proven, in front of everyone, that I was a very good liar. Why should she believe this was the truth, and not just a cleverer lie underneath the first one?

I didn't have an answer.

It was old Tobiah who had one.

He stood up slowly, creaking, from his stool beside the elders, holding the weatherglass. He walked over to me, and held it out in both hands, the wine bottle and the bowl and the reed with my scratches on it.

"Tell me how this works," he said. "Tell me what it does."

I looked at him. Then I looked at the barometer.

And I understood what he was doing. Anybody could steal a weatherglass. But you couldn't steal knowing why it worked. Only somebody who'd been taught it could explain it. And the only people left in this world who'd been taught it were standing in this hall.

So I taught it.

I did it the way I'd do it for my seventh graders, on a Tuesday afternoon in October, standing at the whiteboard. I explained that air has weight, even though you can't feel it. That it presses down on everything, all the time, on the lake and the fields and the people in this room. That sometimes it presses harder, and sometimes softer, and that when it presses softer, a storm is usually coming. I showed them how the water in the bowl gets pushed by the air outside, and how the water in the neck of the bottle climbs when the push gets weaker. I used my hands. I drew it in the air. At one point, without thinking about it, I asked if anybody had any questions, and three children raised their hands.

When I was finished, Tobiah stood very still for a long moment.

Then he said, quietly, so that only the people near the front could hear:

"My grandfather explained it just like that."

Ruth Ashgrove looked at Tobiah for a long time. Then she looked at me. Then she turned to the guards by the door.

"Leave their hands untied," she said.

That was all. It wasn't trust. Not yet. But it was something.

About an hour later, after the hall had mostly emptied out and the children had been chased outside, still wearing my ears, Wenna walked up to the bench where I was sitting. She didn't say anything. She just set down a small clay crock in front of me, and walked away again without looking back.

I took off the lid.

It was butter.

Not long after that, the boy who'd kicked me marched up to my bench, with his mother hovering a few steps behind him like a storm cloud. He stood up very straight, with his hands behind his back, and delivered an apology so stiff and formal that he'd obviously rehearsed it about forty times. He was sorry he had kicked me. He had not known I was a human. He would not do it again.

I accepted it with great seriousness. He nodded, satisfied. Then he looked up at me with tremendous hope.

"Can I kick a real one, then?" he asked. "When they come?"

His mother hauled him away by the collar.

\* \* \*

That afternoon, Ruth Ashgrove sent for me.

She was sitting by the hearth at the far end of the meeting hall, with the four other elders and old Tobiah in a half-circle on stools around the peat fire, and she gestured for Thessa and me to sit with them. Somebody had brought tea. It was nettle tea, and it was terrible, and it was hot, and I'd never been so grateful for anything.

"Now," said Ruth. "Tell us. All of it."

So I did.

I told them about Raleigh, and the convention, and the costume contest, and the floor opening under my feet. I told them about the summoning chamber, and Durgan shouting "Behold!", and the Codex, and the Lost Prince. I told them about the banquet, and Lady Ithrien explaining the Blight to me over pie. I told them about the iron door, and the two drafts of the fire report, and the maps with the names of the Houses written across their great-great-grandparents' villages. I told them about the Charter of the March, and the Council of the Host, and the study at the top of the tallest tree, where Caelith had told me, very gently, that he intended to leave no witnesses.

They listened to all of it without interrupting. They were good listeners. Better than the High Houses, by a long way. They sat in stunned silence through the summoning chamber and the banquet. But when I got to starlight fasting, and explained that the most powerful people in the world had lived on a single dewdrop each for four hundred years, one of the old women snorted into her nettle tea. And when I got to the Charter of the March, and the week-long argument about a comma, Tobiah stared at me and said, "A *comma*?" and the whole hearth broke. The elders laughed until they had to wipe their eyes. Even the corner of Ruth's mouth twitched.

You'd think three hundred years of being afraid of someone would prepare you for finding out they once spent a week arguing about punctuation. It doesn't.

When I'd finished, one of the elders, an old woman with a face like a dried apple and very bright eyes, leaned forward on her stool.

"This place," she said. "Where you come from. This Raleigh." She said it carefully, like a foreign word, which I suppose it was. "Are there others there? Like us?"

"In Raleigh? About half a million," I said. "In the whole world? About eight billion."

I don't think I've ever heard a room go so quiet.

The peat fire hissed. Outside, a dog barked once, and stopped. The old woman stared at me. Then she said, very slowly, in a voice that shook a little:

"Eight... billion."

"Give or take."

"And they're all..." She stopped, and swallowed, and tried again. "They're all still alive?"

I understood then. For three hundred years, the people of Hearthhold had believed they might be the last humans left anywhere. A few hundred people at the top of the world, waiting to be found. And I'd just told them there were eight billion more, somewhere, going to work and doing homework and ordering pizza and complaining about traffic.

"Yes," I said. "All of them. Mostly they're fine."

The old woman put both hands over her face. Nobody said anything for a while.

Then it was Ruth's turn to tell me their story.

She told it simply, the way you'd tell something you'd told many times, to many children. After the Purge, she said, about two hundred people had made it this far north. Out of a whole people. They'd come up through the wastes in winter with whatever they could carry, and found this valley, with its spring and its trees and its south-facing slope, and half of them had died before the snow melted.

And the ones who lived, she said, had sat in a cave at the top of the valley, all that first long winter, and asked each other one question.

*Why?*

Why had every other people in the world turned on them at once? Why had elves and orcs and dwarves and beastfolk, who agreed on nothing, agreed on this?

"And they answered it," Ruth said. "Honestly. That was the hard part."

They'd written it all down, she said. Everything their people had done before the Purge. The forests cut past the treaty lines. The rivers fouled. The boundary stones moved in the night. The treaties broken, one after another, because a human life is short and a human memory is shorter, and nobody alive remembered signing them. They'd written down what had been done to them, too: the burning, and the killing, and the long, terrible march north. They wrote it all in a single book, and they called it the Book of the Reckoning, and it was kept here, in this hall, in a chest under the long table.

"Every child in Hearthhold learns both," Ruth said. "What was done to us. And what we did. They learn it before they learn to read." She looked at the carved lintel over the door, though from where we sat we could only see the edge of it. "That's where the rule came from. *Take only what you can give back.* We wrote it down so we'd never be the Blight again."

I sat there by the fire with a cup of terrible nettle tea in my hands, and I couldn't say anything for a long while.

I'm a history teacher. For three months, I'd been living among the oldest, most refined, most powerful people in the world, people with a library of grown trees and forty books about the Purge, and not one of those books had a single word of truth about why it happened. They'd taken the first draft, the honest one, and crossed out the part that was inconvenient, and written in the part that made them heroes, and sealed it behind an iron door, and forgotten it was there.

And up here, at the top of the world, the people they'd called vermin had done the opposite. They'd written down the worst things about themselves. On purpose. And taught it to their children, so that they'd never forget.

"You kept both drafts," I said, finally. My voice didn't sound quite right.

Ruth didn't know what I meant, exactly. But I think she understood.

Then Thessa spoke for the first time. Quietly, the way she always did, with her hands folded around her cup.

She told them about the iron room. About the muster rolls. About Clan Ashtusk, and three hundred spears, and the note in the margin: *Compliance secured by grain embargo. House Vaunt.* About her great-great-grandfather Ulgrin, who'd marched north because his children were hungry, and about the songs that said he'd done it for the forest.

Ruth Ashgrove listened to every word, without moving.

"We were always told," she said slowly, when Thessa had finished, "that the Spears came gladly. That they wanted it. That they cheered."

Thessa looked at her across the fire.

"We were starved," she said.

Ruth was quiet for a long moment. Then she nodded, slowly, and something in her old face shifted, just slightly, the way ice shifts on a lake in spring.

"So were we," she said. "After."

\* \* \*

Late that afternoon, Wenna showed us the wall.

She didn't offer. She just turned up at the door of the meeting hall with her bow slung across her back, and jerked her chin at Thessa and me, and walked off up the valley without checking whether we'd follow. We followed.

She took us up through the village, past the terraces, past the long low greenhouses with their misted glass. Through one of the panes, as we passed, I saw rows and rows of green: cabbages, and onions, and something leafy I didn't recognize, and at the far end, a little potted lemon tree, of all things, with three small yellow lemons on it, glowing under the glass in the middle of a frozen wasteland. I stopped and stared at it. Wenna stopped too, and followed my eyes, and for the first time, something almost like pride crossed her sharp, freckled face.

"Took my great-grandmother forty years to get that to fruit," she said. Then she walked on.

At the top of the valley, where the ravine we'd come down narrowed to its tightest point, the people of Hearthhold had built a wall.

It wasn't very tall. About the height of my chest. But it ran right across the mouth of the ravine, from one rock face to the other, built of the same fitted gray stone as the terraces, each block shaped to lock into the ones around it, no mortar at all. It was beautiful work. It had been built, I realized, over generations, a few stones at a time, the way you'd build anything you expected to need someday. Behind it, there were piles of smooth river stones for slings, stacked in neat pyramids. There were pitchforks with their tines filed sharp. There were a few old swords, rust-spotted, wrapped in oiled cloth. And there were people working on it, even now: hauling stones, testing the slings, sharpening anything that could be sharpened.

Further up the valley, I could see a line of figures climbing a steep path toward a set of dark openings high in the cliffs. Women and old men, carrying bundles. Children, carrying smaller bundles. Somebody was leading a goat. It wasn't my goat, but it made my chest hurt anyway.

"The high caves," Wenna said, following my look. "For the little ones. And whoever can't hold a sling."

I looked at the wall. I looked at the pitchforks. I thought about the household guard of House Vaunt, centuries-old soldiers in shining silver armor, with longbows that could split an arrow at two hundred paces. I'd seen Venrick do it.

"How long can you hold it?" I asked.

Wenna didn't answer.

Ruth Ashgrove did. She'd come up behind us, quietly, the way she seemed to do everything, and stood beside the wall with one hand resting on the cold stone.

"Not long," she said. "An hour. Maybe two, if they're careless." She looked down the ravine, south, toward where the army would come. "We've known this day was coming for three hundred years. We've always known how it ends." Her voice didn't waver at all. "We'd rather it ended with us standing up."

I looked at her: small and old and white-haired, with her hand on a wall her great-grandparents had started building, calmly planning to die behind it.

"There's another way," I said. "Maybe."

She looked at me.

So I told her about Article Nine.

I told her about the Charter of the March, and the six weeks of arguments, and the comma, and the long, boring article about wagons. And then I told her about the sentence I'd written into the middle of it, by lamplight, in another man's handwriting, on page seven, between the axle grease and the oats. *No people of the forest shall be put to the sword without trial before the assembled Houses.* And how every High House of the Silverwood had signed it, and sealed it, and not one of them had read it.

And I told her about the Archive Charter, the oldest law in the Silverwood, older than the High Houses themselves, which said that the royal archive belonged to all the peoples of the forest.

"All the peoples," I said. "Not the elves. Not the High Houses. *All.* Were you one, once? Before the Purge?"

Ruth was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The Book of the Reckoning says we sat in the councils of the forest, once. Long ago. Before we broke the treaties. Before they stopped inviting us."

"Then you're a people of the forest," I said. "Under the oldest law they have. And under Article Nine, they can't put a single one of you to the sword without a trial. In front of the assembled Houses. With witnesses."

Ruth looked at me for a long moment. The wind blew down the ravine, cold and steady.

"Elves," she said at last, "do not keep laws for us."

She said it without bitterness. Just as a fact, the way you'd say that water runs downhill. And she was probably right. Three hundred years of history said she was right.

"No," I agreed. "Probably not. Not on their own." I looked south, down the ravine. "But they might keep one in front of witnesses. If somebody makes them."

"And who," said Ruth Ashgrove, "is going to make them?"

I didn't answer. I didn't have to. She looked at me, a twenty-three-year-old in a bedsheet cape, with bad hair and red, raw, round ears, standing on her wall. And for the first time since I'd met her, I saw her almost smile.

\* \* \*

That night, I went back up to the wall.

I couldn't sleep, again. I don't think anyone in Hearthhold could. The village below was full of quiet sounds: doors opening and closing, low voices, the clink of sling stones being sorted into baskets, a baby crying and being hushed. Every window had a light in it.

Ruth Ashgrove was already there when I climbed up. She was standing at the wall in her long gray coat, alone, with her hands resting on the cold stones, looking south down the ravine and out over the dark wastes beyond it. I came and stood beside her. She didn't say anything. Neither did I, for a while.

The fires were much closer now.

Three nights ago, from the ridge, Wenna and Tobiah had seen them as pinpricks at the edge of the world. Now they were a bright, scattered line of light across the plain, maybe a day's march away. Not the whole host. I could tell from the shape of them. The main army of the Concordance was still far back, a dim glow on the southern horizon, a day or more behind them. This was the vanguard. The household guard of House Vaunt, pushed out ahead on Caelith's orders, to get here first. To do the work before anyone else could see it.

"My grandmother," Ruth said, after a long time, "always told me that someday someone would come. From outside. From the south."

I looked at her.

"She said it the way you'd talk about the weather," Ruth said. "Someday, someone will come. She thought it would be an army. She said it would come with fire, the way it came before. She made me promise I'd be ready."

She looked at the line of fires on the plain.

"She was right about the army," she said. Then she turned her head and looked at me, standing beside her on her wall, in a borrowed felted coat over my bedsheet cape, with my hair sticking up and the glitter still not quite all off my face. "She never said anything about you."

Her grandmother had expected an army. She'd gotten a seventh-grade history teacher in a bedsheet. I wasn't sure yet which of us was the bigger disappointment.

There was a crunch of snow on the path behind us. Old Tobiah was climbing up the steps to the wall, slowly, wrapped in all four of his scarves, carrying something in both arms. When he reached us, he held it out to me.

It was the weatherglass. The wine bottle and the bowl and the reed, packed carefully in a fresh wool blanket, with the water in it still level, still sitting just where I'd last scratched it.

"You'll be wanting this," Tobiah said.

"And about your ears," he added. "The children held a vote. They're giving back the right one."

"And the left one?"

"They're keeping the left one."

"The *crooked* one?"

"*Especially* the crooked one," said Tobiah.

I took the weatherglass. I didn't know what to say to any of it, so I just nodded. He nodded back, and patted the blanket once, the way you'd pat a dog, and went back down the steps into the dark.

I stood there on the wall until the fires below had burned low, holding the weatherglass against my chest, watching the fires.

The children of Hearthhold were still passing my ears around when I finally went to sleep. Somebody's little sister had them on. She looked great.

A day south of us, Caelith's guard was coming. And on page seven of the Charter of the March, between the axle grease and the oats, was the only weapon Hearthhold had.
