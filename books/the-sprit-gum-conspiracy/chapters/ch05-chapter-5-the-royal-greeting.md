# Chapter 5: The Royal Greeting

My high school choir teacher had one rule for performances: if you don't know the words, sing louder.

I want to be clear that this was terrible advice, and that I followed it anyway.

The morning of my formal presentation to the Concordance, Thessa sat me down in front of a mirror the size of a garage door and braided Gwendolyn. She did it carefully, the way you'd handle something that might explode, her charms jingling softly as she worked. Halfway through she paused and ran a strand through her fingers.

"Your hair is very soft, Highness," she said. "It's not like any hair I've ever touched."

"Royal conditioner," I said.

She nodded seriously and kept braiding. When she was finished, Gwendolyn had become a crown of silver-blond plaits, pinned with tiny silver leaves, cascading down my back. Gwendolyn had never looked better. I made a mental note to tell Dev, if I ever saw Dev again.

Then I sent Thessa out of the room, took the travel bottle of spirit gum out of my belt pouch, and squeezed.

One drop came out. One small, tired drop. I used it on the right ear, which had started to lift at the top during the night, and pressed and counted to thirty. Then I squeezed again.

Nothing.

I held the bottle up to the light, shook it, and squeezed it with both hands like it owed me money. It wheezed at me. That was all.

I put the empty bottle back in my pouch anyway. Some things you can't throw away. On my lesson plan, I moved item one from "Known problems" to a new heading at the very top of the page, which I titled "EMERGENCIES." It was, for the moment, the only one. That was going to change.

Lady Ithrien arrived at nine to coach me on protocol. She had volunteered. I think she'd decided that I was her personal project, the way some aunts decide about nephews.

She spent two hours teaching me to bow. There were apparently eleven kinds of bow, one for each degree of rank, plus a special half-bow for the beastfolk, plus a slow nod reserved for the orc chieftains that she demonstrated with obvious distaste. She taught me where to stand, and when to sit, and never to look the owl-folk elder directly in the eyes, for reasons she didn't explain.

Then, at the very end, almost as an afterthought, she said, "And when the Herald speaks the Salutation of Crowns, you'll answer with the words of your House."

I waited for her to tell me what they were. She didn't.

"The words of my House," I said.

"Of course, Highness."

"My memory," I said carefully, "is still clouded by the Veil."

She waved a hand. "They'll come to you, Highness. They're in your blood."

They were not in my blood. My blood is mostly Frosted Wheat Rectangles.

But I knew what the words of the Silverwood were. Everybody who's played *Elderlight III* knows. They're printed across the loading screen of the Silverwood chapter, in silver letters, over a painting of Aelindor on a white horse. They're the first thing you hear when the music swells. They're the words I'd shouted on the stage of the Sovereign Crystal Grand Ballroom, right before the floor opened.

RPG logic: when you don't know what to do, use what the game gave you. And so far, the game had been right. The codex line had been right, word for word.

Why wouldn't this be?

\* \* \*

The Hall of Crowns was round, like a coliseum someone had civilized. Tiers of seats rose up on every side under a ceiling of woven branches, and around the top of the walls hung the banners of every people in the Concordance: the silver trees of the elven Houses, iron-gray orc clan totems, a dwarven hammer on a field of red, and a dozen beastfolk sigils I didn't recognize, claws and feathers and a stylized wolf's head.

And the people under the banners were there too. All of them.

I'd only seen elves and orc guards so far. Now, for the first time, I saw the rest of the world.

The High Houses filled the lowest tiers in a shimmer of silver and pearl. Above them sat the orc chieftains in black iron and furs, each with a clan totem planted beside their seat. The dwarf envoy sat alone in a box of his own: short, square, with a beard bound into one severe braid and a gold chain across his chest. When he saw Durgan wave to him from the far side of the hall, he turned his entire chair around to face the other way.

The beastfolk delegation took my breath away. There were wolf-folk, tall and gray-furred, with jeweled collars and pale yellow eyes. And there were owl-folk, feathered and silent, wrapped in cloaks of their own molted plumage. Their elder sat at the center: tiny, ancient, with enormous amber eyes. As I walked in, her head turned to follow me. It kept turning. It turned a lot farther than a head should.

I remembered Lady Ithrien's advice and looked somewhere else very quickly.

Thessa walked a step behind me on my left, as my attendant. She leaned in and whispered, "The old one in the third row, Highness, with the bear-skull totem. That's Warchief Gorza. She's the chief of my clan." The old orc woman was broad as a door, with gray braids and a face like a weathered cliff. She was watching me with frank, unimpressed curiosity.

Brakka took up his post beside the great doors, arms folded, his fresh war paint slightly crooked.

I did what I do on the first day of school: I scanned the room. You learn to do it fast. Who are the loud ones? Who are the quiet ones? And who's the one who's watching you, instead of the room?

That was when I saw the inquisitors.

They stood together on the left side of the hall, a dozen elves in robes of black and silver, very still, like a row of crows on a wire. In front of them stood a young elf woman with her silver hair pulled back so tightly it looked painful. She had the kind of face that has never once found anything funny. A crystal hung from a silver chain around her neck.

As I walked into the Hall of Crowns, the crystal began to glow.

It was a soft, pale blue at first, like the glow from a phone screen under the covers. She looked down at it. She frowned. She tapped it with one finger. She shook it, the way you'd shake an old thermometer.

It kept glowing.

My stomach dropped all the way to my shoes. My feet kept walking, because the whole Concordance was watching, and I had already learned the most important rule in this world. You never let them see you sweat.

Caelith met me at the center of the hall. He was magnificent, as usual, in silver and white, and he turned to face the tiers with one hand raised, and the whole Concordance fell silent.

"Peoples of the Concordance," he said. "The Lost Prince of the First Line. Returned."

The Herald stepped forward. He was a narrow elf with a ceremonial staff and a voice like a trumpet, and he'd clearly been waiting his entire career for this moment. He struck the staff on the floor three times. The sound rang around the hall.

"*Who comes to the Hall of Crowns,*" he cried, "*and by what words is his House known?*"

Five hundred people waited.

In the front row, Lady Ithrien was nodding at me encouragingly, the way you'd nod at a toddler about to try a step.

I thought about my choir teacher. I thought about the loading screen and the white horse. I drew the foam sword, which Thessa had straightened as best she could, and raised it high, and I filled my lungs from the diaphragm, and I did what I'd been taught.

I sang louder.

"*THAL'VORIN ANESHKA!* THE SILVERWOOD *STANDS!*"

I felt the tongue-binding take the second half of it. *The Silverwood stands* went out into that hall transformed: ringing, ancient, a line that sounded like it had been shouted on a hundred battlefields.

The first half didn't change at all.

I felt it leave my mouth exactly the way I'd practiced it in a convention bathroom mirror. The same sounds, the same syllables. The tongue-binding didn't touch it, because it wasn't a language I knew. It was just noises from a video game.

Except, as it turned out, it wasn't.

There was a silence. Then there was a sound I can only describe as five hundred people inhaling at the same time. It was so loud the banners moved.

A lady in pearl silk in the second row put the back of her hand to her forehead and fainted, gracefully, into the arms of a very surprised wolf-folk envoy. Venrick went red, then purple, then a color I didn't know elves came in. On the far side of the hall, Durgan had been taking a drink at the wrong moment, and he sprayed it in a fine red mist across the back of the dwarf envoy, who did not turn around but whose shoulders rose slowly toward his ears.

The Herald's face had frozen in an expression of pure horror. The owl-folk elder's amber eyes had gotten so big they took up most of her head. In the third row, Warchief Gorza let out one loud bark of laughter before she clapped a hand over her mouth.

And from beside the great doors came a sound I had never heard before.

It started low, like boulders shifting somewhere deep inside a mountain. Then it built. Then it rolled out across the Hall of Crowns like a rockslide. Brakka was bent nearly double, one hand braced against the doorframe, his shoulders shaking. The other guards were staring at him in astonishment.

Brakka was laughing.

I didn't move.

There's a rule for this, too. When a kid says something truly terrible in the middle of a lesson, the worst thing you can possibly do is look shocked. The moment you look shocked, they own the room. So you don't. You keep your face exactly where it was, and you let the moment pass through you, and you act as though everything is going precisely according to plan.

So I held the sword high and my chin higher, as if I'd meant every word. I didn't know what every word meant.

It was Caelith who moved first.

He was looking at me, and for the first time since I'd arrived, his face showed something I'd never seen on it: genuine surprise. It sat on him strangely, like a coat that belonged to someone else. It lasted half a second.

Then he smiled and spread his hands toward the tiers.

"The words of the First Line," he said, smooth as cream. "We had forgotten how... *vigorous* they were."

A ripple of nervous laughter went around the hall. I watched it happen: five hundred people deciding, all at once, that what they'd just heard was ancient and therefore all right. The fainted lady was revived. The Herald, still pale, struck his staff on the floor and moved on to the next part of the ceremony as if nothing had happened.

I lowered my sword. My hands were perfectly steady. I was shaking everywhere else.

Caelith had just saved me. I didn't know why. But I knew one thing about people from twenty-three years of watching them: nobody bothers to save something they don't need.

\* \* \*

The receiving line took three hours.

I did all eleven kinds of bow. I did the half-bow for the beastfolk, and the wolf-folk envoy, who was still holding the lady who'd fainted, bowed back with great dignity. Warchief Gorza gripped my forearm in a grip like a vise, grinned, showing every tusk she had, and said one word: "*Vigorous*." The dwarf envoy, still damp, gave me a stiff nod and a look that said he held me personally responsible for Durgan.

Then the inquisitor came.

Up close, she was even more severe than she'd been from across the hall. She was tall, even for an elf, and her black and silver robes were so perfectly pressed they looked starched. Her eyes were the gray of a hawk's. The crystal around her neck had stopped being a soft glow some time ago. It was blazing now, bright as a flashlight, throwing hard blue shadows across her face.

She gave me the bow for a Prince, precise to the degree. "Silvaine of House Thistlevane," she said. "Inquisitor of the Concordance."

Behind me, Lady Ithrien whispered, thrilled, "The youngest inquisitor in history, Highness. She's only three hundred and twelve."

"Highness." Silvaine lifted the crystal on its chain and held it up between us. "Forgive me. This is a Crystal of Discernment. The Inquisition uses them to find the Blight."

The light was so bright I had to squint.

She looked at the crystal. Then she looked at me. Then she looked at the crystal again.

I felt every drop of the spirit gum holding my ears to my head, and I thought: this is how it ends. In a receiving line, after three hours of bowing. Not with a sword. With a flashlight.

But I've been in this situation before. Not exactly this one, but close.

During my first month of student teaching, a vice principal caught me in the copy room with a stack of worksheets I hadn't technically gotten approved, and asked me, in a very level voice, what they were for. And I learned something that day. When someone suspects you, the worst thing you can do is defend yourself. Defending yourself means there's something to defend. Instead, you get interested in *them*.

"Remarkable," I said, leaning in to look at the crystal. "How does it work?"

Silvaine blinked. I don't think anyone had ever asked her that.

"It responds to the essence of the short-lived," she said slowly. Then, as I kept nodding, with more confidence: "It's attuned to the Blight's nature. Their brevity. The crystals were grown in the years after the Purge, specifically to find any who might have hidden among us. I have used this one in forty-three investigations."

"The Concordance is lucky," I said, "to have someone so thorough."

Something shifted in her face. It wasn't a smile. I don't think her face did smiles. But it relaxed by about a millimeter.

She looked back down at the blazing crystal, and frowned, and I watched her think. It was like watching someone work a math problem on a board.

"It has never been in the presence of the Old Blood," she said. "There are no records of it ever being tested on royalty." She tilted the crystal. "The Prince is of the First Line. The Blight is the very opposite of the First Line. Therefore, the crystal is reacting to an *excess*. An overwhelming presence of essence. It's overloaded." She nodded once, satisfied. "I'll have it sent for repair."

"Very wise," I said.

"My record in these matters is impeccable, Highness."

From the doorway, without moving, Brakka said: "Forty-three."

Silvaine turned her head toward him very slowly.

"Forty-three *investigations*," she said.

"Forty-three wrong," said Brakka.

Behind me, Lady Ithrien whispered, with deep satisfaction, "One of them was her *mother*."

Silvaine's jaw tightened. "The Inquisition," she said, in a voice like a closing door, "does not make mistakes. It makes inquiries." She turned back to me and bowed again, precisely. "I will be watching everyone in this palace, Highness. Everyone."

Then she walked away. The crystal blazed all the way across the Hall of Crowns, and faded only when she was through the far doors.

Not everyone, I thought. Everyone except me.

And for now, that one blind spot was the only thing keeping me alive.

\* \* \*

Brakka kept laughing all the way back to my chambers.

Not continuously. He'd be fine for a while, walking behind me with his usual expression, which was the expression of a cliff. Then his shoulders would start to shake, and a sound like distant thunder would roll up out of his chest, and he'd have to stop and put a hand on the wall. Then it would pass, and he'd straighten up, and we'd walk on. Then it would happen again. It was like walking home with an earthquake that had aftershocks.

Thessa walked beside me with her face so red it was almost purple, staring at the floor, her braids jingling with every step.

Once the doors of my chambers were shut behind us, I turned around.

"Okay," I said. "What does it mean?"

Thessa made a small noise and covered her face with both hands. "I can't, Highness."

"Thessa."

"I *can't*."

I looked at Brakka. Brakka wiped his eyes with one thick knuckle, cleared his throat, and visibly tried to compose himself into something dignified.

"It is," he said carefully, "an offer."

"An offer of what?"

He thought about it. "An enthusiastic offer."

"Brakka."

"Involving," Brakka said, "a goose."

There was a pause.

"A goose," I said.

Brakka nodded solemnly. "A specific goose."

Behind her hands, Thessa whimpered.

He went on for a little while after that. I'm not going to repeat it. I'll just say that by the time he finished, I understood exactly why a lady had fainted in the second row, and I knew I would never be able to look at a goose the same way again.

I sat down on the edge of the bed. Hard.

In *Elderlight III*, *Thal'vorin aneshka* is just flavor. Fantasy gibberish. If you look it up on the fan wiki, it says *Ancient Elvish: "By leaf and by blade."* Somebody at the studio made it up. That's what I'd always thought. That's what everybody thought. There are hundreds of thousands of people on Earth who have shouted those words at their TVs.

But it wasn't gibberish. It was real elvish, and it meant something, and that something was so filthy it had nearly ended a state ceremony.

The codex line had been one coincidence. I could almost believe in one coincidence.

This was two.

I teach history. Two coincidences isn't a pattern yet. But it's how every pattern I've ever seen got started.

Somebody who worked on that game had known this language. The real one. Well enough to make a joke in it. And then they'd put that joke where it would get shouted by every player who reached the Silverwood chapter, in a language nobody on Earth was supposed to understand.

An old interview drifted up out of the back of my mind: *Some stories you can only tell if everyone thinks you made them up.*

I sat there for a long time while Brakka had one more aftershock in the corner.

\* \* \*

The next morning, I went out on the balcony to check my barometer.

The water hadn't moved. The sky over the Silverwood was blue and clear and endless. The rains were still weeks away, according to Brakka, and the wine bottle agreed with him.

I was standing there with my coffee, which in the Silverwood was a kind of hot bark tea that tasted like a pencil, when I heard voices in the garden below.

Two court ladies in gowns the color of morning were coming toward each other along the gravel path. One of them was Lady Ithrien. As they met, she lifted one elegant hand.

"*Thal'vorin aneshka*, darling!"

"Oh!" The other lady pressed a hand to her chest. "And *aneshka* to *you*!"

Then both of them collapsed into helpless giggles, clutching each other's arms, hiding their faces behind their sleeves. A moment later, the High Dewkeeper came gliding past them on his way to some morning rite. He laid one hand gravely over his ear, inclined his head, and murmured it too, with complete and holy sincerity.

Below my balcony, two of the most refined ladies in the Silverwood greeted each other with a goose joke and giggled like seventh graders.

Somebody on Earth had known this language. And they'd taught a whole generation of gamers to shout that joke at elves.
