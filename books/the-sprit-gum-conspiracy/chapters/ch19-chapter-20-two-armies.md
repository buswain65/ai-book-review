# Chapter 20: Two Armies

Before I could face an army, I had to get my ear back from a nine-year-old.

The right ear had been returned the night before, as promised, by a solemn little delegation who delivered it to Thessa on a folded cloth like a crown on a cushion. The left ear was another matter. The left ear, I was informed at breakfast, was being held by the committee.

"The committee," I said.

"That's what they're calling it," said Wenna, who was eating porridge across from me and clearly enjoying herself. "They've been in session since dawn."

The committee was meeting in the far corner of the meeting hall, around a low stool they were using as a table. There were seven of them, ranging in age from about five to about ten, sitting cross-legged on the floor in a circle, with the gravity of a supreme court. At the head of the circle, chairing, sat the boy who had kicked me in the shin. His name, I'd learned, was Davy. He was holding a wooden spoon.

It took me a moment to realize what the spoon was. Then I understood, and felt something between pride and horror. They'd watched me the day before, at the hearth. They'd watched me run that conversation with the elders, the way I'd run the Council of the Host, the way I'd run every meeting since my first week in the Silverwood. And they'd learned. The spoon was a talking stick.

The left ear sat on the stool in the middle of the circle, on a folded handkerchief.

Thessa went over. I stayed back by the hearth, because I was fairly sure this was not a negotiation I'd improve by being in it.

"The committee recognizes the Spear shaman," said Davy gravely, and handed her the spoon.

Thessa took it. She sat down cross-legged on the floor with them, in the circle, as solemn as they were. "I would like," she said, "to request the return of the Prince's ear."

The committee conferred, in whispers. Then a girl of about eight, who appeared to be keeping minutes on a slate, raised her hand. Davy took back the spoon and passed it to her.

"The committee," she said, "has terms."

They had two. First: every member of the committee would get a ride on Pudding. Thessa agreed to that immediately; Pudding, as far as I could tell, would have agreed too. Second, and this was the one they leaned forward for, all seven of them at once: Thessa had to teach them a secret word. A Leaf-Lord word. One that no one else in Hearthhold knew.

Thessa held the spoon and thought about it for a long moment. Then I saw her mouth twitch at the corner, and I had a terrible premonition.

"Pineapple," she said.

The committee breathed it back to her, in a reverent whisper. *Pineapple.*

"What does it mean?" asked the girl with the slate.

Thessa considered. "It means," she said, "get behind something."

They loved it. I could see it on their faces. It was the best word they'd ever heard. They had no idea what a pineapple was, and they didn't care. Davy called for a vote, and seven hands went up, and the girl with the slate wrote something down, and Davy picked up my left ear off the handkerchief and handed it to Thessa with great ceremony.

Someone had drawn a little face on it in charcoal. Two dots for eyes, and a big curved smile, right on the inside of the point.

Thessa looked at it. Then she held one fingertip over it, and called up the smallest Purify I'd ever seen her cast: a single pinprick of silver light, no bigger than a firefly, that touched the charcoal face and wiped it away so cleanly that the latex underneath shone.

The committee gasped. Then it applauded.

I'd like to tell you that was the end of it. It wasn't. For the rest of that morning, every time a member of the committee saw me in the lane, they'd shout "*PINEAPPLE!*" at the top of their lungs, out of pure joy. And every single time, from three months of reflex, I dove behind the nearest thing. A rain barrel. A woodpile. Once, a very surprised goat. By noon, the whole committee had worked out exactly what was happening, and were following me around the village in a giggling pack, waiting for me to turn my back.

Thessa did not apologize. She said it was the best negotiation of her life.

\* \* \*

Thessa put me back together in the meeting hall, at noon.

She sat me on the bench by the hearth, and set out her things on the long table the way a surgeon lays out instruments: my two ears, the jar of resin, her little brush, Gwendolyn on a borrowed hat stand, the pouch of beetle-shell glitter, the comb, the silver pins. And with half of Hearthhold watching from the benches along the walls, very quietly, she turned me back into the Lost Prince of the First Line.

She started with my skin. She dabbed her calendula salve along the raw red edges where the ears had been, and let it sink in, while the people of Hearthhold watched in total silence. Then she painted on the resin, thin and even, and waited, testing it with her fingertip, and set the right ear in place, and held it, and counted in the old orcish way, and let go. Then the left. She set it at exactly the wrong angle without my having to ask. She'd been doing it for a month now. She could have done it in the dark.

Then the wig cap, and Gwendolyn on top of it, too clean and too fluffy still from the night of the reflex Purify, braided back into a crown of plaits and pinned with the little silver leaves. Then the glitter, brushed over my face and neck and the backs of my hands until I caught the firelight. Then the green tunic, and the bedsheet cape, and the foam sword.

When she stepped back, the hall was very quiet. I stood up.

I'd been human in Hearthhold for a day and a half. It had been the best day and a half I'd had in Verathis. And now I was a Leaf-Lord again, in front of everyone who'd just learned I wasn't one. I could see it on their faces: the little flicker of the old fear coming back, just for a second, at the sight of the long, pointed ears and the glitter and the fall of silver-blond hair. Even though they knew. Even though they'd watched it go on, one piece at a time.

That's how strong a costume is, I thought. Even when you've seen it built, it still works.

Ruth Ashgrove had watched the whole thing from her chair at the head of the long table, without a word, with her rough hands folded in her lap. When Thessa finished, she looked me up and down, slowly, from the crooked left ear to the bedsheet cape.

"So," she said dryly. "That's how a Leaf-Lord is made."

"Eight dollars and ninety-nine cents," I said. "And a lot of glue."

She didn't know what a dollar was. But the corner of her mouth moved again, just slightly, the way it had at the wall.

We'd made the plan the night before, the elders and Thessa and I, around the hearth. It wasn't complicated. Hearthhold would stay behind its wall, with its slings and its pitchforks, and wouldn't strike first, no matter what happened. Ruth would wait on the wall, with the Book of the Reckoning, until I needed it. And I would ride out alone, as the Prince, to meet whatever came up the ravine.

Wenna had wanted to come with me. She'd argued for it, hard, for most of an hour. I'd said no, the same way I'd said no to Brakka. Some people you can't bring with you, not because they can't help, but because of what it might cost them if they're standing next to you when the truth comes out.

She hadn't liked it. She'd given me her spare arrow anyway, wordlessly, for luck. It was in my belt pouch now, next to E.M.'s note and the empty spirit gum bottle and my copy of the Charter of the March, folded open to page seven.

\* \* \*

They came up the ravine in the middle of the afternoon.

Wenna saw them first, from the wall, and didn't shout. She just lifted one hand, and the whole wall went still. Then I heard it, from where I stood at the wooden gate in the middle of the wall: a sound like a stream running over stones, coming closer. It was the sound of three hundred people in armor, walking in step.

Then they came around the last bend of the ravine, and I saw them.

I'd seen the household guard of House Vaunt before, at a distance, at the back of the host on the march. I'd never seen them like this. They came up the narrow ravine in a column four abreast, and they were beautiful, the way a blade is beautiful. Silver armor, polished until it threw the gray afternoon light back at the sky. Tall, silver-haired, perfectly matched, like a set of very expensive cutlery, every one of them with a longbow over their shoulder and a sword at their hip. Above them, on long poles, flew the banners of House Vaunt: a silver tree on a field of deep blue, with a letter woven into its roots.

A V. With a little flourish on its tail.

At their head, on a tall white horse, rode Caelith.

He was in silver too, from collar to boots, with a thin circlet on his brow and his long hair loose down his back, and he rode up that ravine as if he were riding into his own garden. On his right, on a smaller horse, rode Corvel, with his sketchbook open on his saddle, already drawing. And on his left rode an old elf woman I'd never seen up close before: lean and gray, her silver hair cropped short as a soldier's, a long pale scar running from her temple to her jaw, in armor that was plainer than the others' and much more dented. She sat her horse like someone who had been sitting horses for longer than the United States has existed. Her eyes moved over the wall, and the people on it, and the slings, and the pitchforks, and didn't change at all.

I'd learn her name within the hour. Captain Isaura. She'd commanded House Vaunt's household guard under Caelith's grandmother, and his mother, and now him.

The column stopped about fifty yards below the wall, where the ravine was widest, and spread out across it in a long, gleaming line. Three hundred longbows, unslung. I heard the people of Hearthhold on the wall above me go very quiet. Somewhere behind me, someone was praying under their breath.

And I opened the gate, and rode out.

Pudding didn't hesitate. I'll always love her for that. She walked straight out through the gap in the wall and down the slope of the ravine toward three hundred elven soldiers as calmly as if she were walking to her oat bag, with her mane still braided with bits of red and yellow and blue yarn from the children of Hearthhold. I sat on her back in my bedsheet cape, with the glitter catching the light and my foam sword at my hip and my crooked left ear and Gwendolyn's braids falling down my back. The Lost Prince of the First Line, coming out of the human village. From the human side of the wall.

I watched it land on them. I watched three hundred faces in silver helmets do the arithmetic, and fail. A ripple went down the line, a murmur, a creak of armor as soldiers shifted.

On his horse, Corvel had stopped drawing, pencil frozen in midair, and was staring at me with his mouth open. Then, after a moment, he started drawing again, much faster.

I rode Pudding down the slope, and across the open ground, and stopped her about ten yards in front of Caelith's white horse.

He looked at me for a long, long moment. At the yarn in Pudding's mane. At the wall behind me, and the people on it. At my face. I couldn't read his expression at all.

Then he smiled, as pleasantly as if we'd met in a palace corridor.

"Highness," said Caelith. "You've seen the field."

"I have," I said.

\* \* \*

"Then you'll have seen," said Caelith, still pleasantly, "that it's a very small field." He lifted his eyes to the wall, and the people on it, as if he were counting them. "Stand aside, Highness. Make camp with us tonight. At dawn, the guard will cleanse it."

He said it the way you'd say the guard will tidy it. Above me, on the wall, I heard someone's breath catch.

I didn't stand aside.

Instead, I reached into my belt pouch, past Wenna's arrow and E.M.'s note, and took out my copy of the Charter of the March. Lord Merrow's beautiful handwriting. Fourteen articles. The seals of every High House of the Silverwood, pressed in colored wax across the bottom of the last page, with House Vaunt's silver tree at the very end. I unfolded it slowly, so that everyone could see what it was. And I turned to page seven.

Then I dropped my voice into Aelindor's register, the one I'd practiced in the mirror of the Radisson a lifetime ago. I let the tongue-binding take it, and make it ring off the walls of the ravine, so that every one of those three hundred soldiers could hear it. And I read.

"The Charter of the March," I said. "Article Nine. *On the Provisioning and Conveyance of the Host.*"

I read them the part about the wagons. I wanted them to hear the wagons. I read them the number of horses per wagon, and the grade of axle grease, and the oat ration, and the proper method of replacing a cracked wheel, every dull word of it, and I watched the soldiers in the front rank begin to frown in confusion. One of them, a gray-haired quartermaster near the end of the line, listened to the oat ration per horse, nodded slowly, and murmured, before he could stop himself, "That's actually very sensible." The soldier beside him elbowed him in the ribs. And then I got to the middle of the paragraph about axle grease, on page seven, and I slowed down.

"*No people of the forest,*" I read, "*shall be put to the sword without trial before the assembled Houses.*"

I lowered the Charter. The ravine was dead silent.

I looked at Caelith.

And for the second time since I'd known him, I saw it happen: something I'd only ever seen once before, on the night I shouted a goose joke at the Hall of Crowns. His face changed. Just for a second. The pleasant, unruffled calm slipped, and underneath it, for one moment, there was real surprise.

It didn't last. He held out one long hand, without a word. I rode Pudding forward and put the Charter in it. He looked down at page seven. He read it once, his eyes moving along the line. He found his own seal at the bottom of the last page. Then he read page seven again.

"Between the axle grease," he said softly, "and the oats."

"Nobody reads the whole document," I said. "Everybody reads the part with their own name on it."

He looked up at me. And for a moment, I thought I saw something almost like respect in his eyes. It was gone as fast as the surprise.

"A clever sentence, Highness," he said. "But it doesn't apply." He handed the Charter back to me, with a little flourish, as if returning a borrowed handkerchief. "These are the Blight. Not a people of the forest."

"The Archive Charter says otherwise," I said. "The oldest law in the Silverwood. Older than the High Houses. *This house of memory belongs to all the peoples of the forest.* All of them." I turned in my saddle, and looked up at the wall. "And they were one. Once. Before the Purge."

On the wall, Ruth Ashgrove had been waiting for exactly this moment. She leaned out over the stones, with a basket on a rope, and lowered it down the face of the wall, hand over hand, to the snow at its foot. In the basket was the Book of the Reckoning: a thick, battered, leather-bound volume, three hundred years old, its pages soft with handling. It had been open, I knew, to a particular page, though by the time I'd ridden back and leaned down from Pudding's saddle to lift it out, the wind had riffled through it. Wedged in beside it, at the bottom of the basket, was a small clay crock. I looked up at the wall. Wenna was very carefully not looking at me. It was butter. I didn't ask. I found the page again. I held it up.

"The humans sat in the councils of the forest," I said. "It's written here. Before they broke the treaties. Before they were thrown out."

Caelith didn't even look at the book. "A book," he said gently, "written by vermin, Highness, proves nothing."

"It's a primary source," I said.

He didn't know what that meant. But somewhere in the front rank of the guard, I heard a soldier make a sound that might have been a laugh, quickly swallowed.

Caelith's eyes moved to the soldier, and the sound stopped.

"Very well," he said, turning back to me. "Let us suppose, for the sake of argument, that you're right. That this Charter applies to these... people." He gestured at the wall, at the slings and the pitchforks and the faces. "Then they'll have their trial. Before the assembled Houses. As the Charter says." He smiled again. "But the assembled Houses, Highness, are a full day behind us. There can be no trial without them."

"Then we wait for them," I said.

He went very still on his white horse.

And in that stillness, I saw him think about it. Really think about it. I watched him look at the wall, and the three hundred longbows behind him, and the narrow ravine, and the long empty plain to the south, and I could see him working out how long it would take. An hour. Maybe two. Before the assembled Houses ever arrived. Before anyone else ever saw. He could say the humans had attacked first. He could say anything. Corvel would draw it however he was told.

And there was nothing at all I could do to stop him. I'd read my sentence. I'd held up my book. And none of it would matter, not even a little, if the person who'd sealed the Charter decided not to keep it.

Caelith opened his mouth.

And beside him, on her plain, dented horse, Captain Isaura spoke for the first time.

She didn't raise her voice. She didn't look at me. She didn't even look at the wall. She looked straight ahead, at nothing, and said, quietly, in a voice like an old blade being drawn:

"My lord. The Charter bears your seal. And the seals of every House."

Caelith turned his head toward her, very slowly.

"If the guard of House Vaunt breaks it," Captain Isaura went on, still not looking at him, "before the Houses arrive, then the Houses will ask why we were in such a hurry." She paused. "And what we had to hide."

The ravine was absolutely silent.

Caelith looked at her for a long time. She'd served his grandmother, and his mother, and him. I don't know what passed between them in that look. I don't think I'll ever know. But it went on for what felt like a full minute, and Captain Isaura never once met his eyes, and never once looked away from the place in front of her where she was looking.

Then Caelith smiled.

It was the same lovely, empty smile as always. It just took him a little longer to put it on.

"Very well," he said, and turned back to me. "Captain Isaura is quite right. The law is the law. Send riders south. The Houses will assemble here, and there will be a trial, in full and proper form." He gathered up his reins. "And then, Highness," he said softly, "there will be a valley."

He turned his white horse and rode back through the lines of his guard. Captain Isaura followed him, without a glance at me.

Only Corvel stayed behind a moment longer, on his little horse, sketching so fast his pencil was a blur. I looked over at his page as he finished it: the Lost Prince of the First Line, on a tall, wild-eyed white stallion, holding a scroll aloft before a line of shining soldiers, the very picture of noble defiance.

At the bottom, he'd already written the caption.

*The Prince Invokes the Law.*

He hadn't asked why the Prince had come out from the human side of the wall. He'd just drawn it.

\* \* \*

The riders left before sunset.

I watched them go from the open ground below the wall: four of them, House Vaunt's fastest, galloping south down the ravine and out onto the plain, getting smaller and smaller against the gray until they were just specks, and then nothing. By tomorrow afternoon, they'd reach the host. By tomorrow night, or the morning after, the whole army of the Concordance would be standing in this valley. Every High House. The orc companies, and the dwarves with their payment schedules, and the wolf-folk, and the owl-folk overhead. Gorza and Okko. Venrick and Lady Ithrien. Durgan, probably still writing invoices. Silvaine, with her crystal. Brakka.

I'd wanted witnesses. I was about to get all of them.

Caelith sent an officer, not long after, with an invitation. It was written on a card of heavy silver paper, in a beautiful hand, and it requested the pleasure of the Prince's company at dinner in the guard's camp that evening. It listed the menu: *Roast boar in the Old Blood style. Dew, for those observing the Starlight.* And at the bottom, in smaller letters, it asked the Prince to kindly reply. The man was planning a massacre for the morning, and he'd sent an RSVP card. I wrote my regrets on the back, and sent the officer back with my thanks. I told him that the Prince would keep vigil on the field tonight, as the Old Kings had always done, and that he hoped the High Lord would understand.

I had no idea whether the Old Kings had ever done any such thing. It sounded right. Nobody argued.

So Thessa and I made our camp in the empty space between the two sides: a strip of trampled snow at the bottom of the slope, about halfway between Hearthhold's wall and the first of the guard's fires. We had a little lamp, and two blankets, and Pudding, who'd been given an extremely generous helping of oats by somebody on the wall and lowered down to us in a bucket. Behind us, on the wall, the people of Hearthhold kept watch by torchlight. Below us, the long, glittering line of the guard's campfires stretched across the ravine from side to side. And there we sat, in the dark, exactly in the middle.

It was very cold, and very quiet. We didn't talk much.

At one point, sometime after midnight, I heard a small, hoarse voice from the top of the wall above us, whispering as loudly as it could.

"*Pineapple!*"

I threw myself face-first into the snow. Above me, I heard a burst of muffled giggling, and a mother's voice hissing something sharp, and then quiet again.

Thessa, who hadn't moved, looked down at me lying in the snow and said, "You're going to have to get over that."

"I know," I said, into the snow.

When I'd brushed myself off, I couldn't sleep, so I unwrapped the weatherglass.

I hadn't checked it since the night on the wall, when Tobiah had handed it back to me. I set it on a flat stone beside the lamp, and turned up the wick, and leaned down close to look at the water in the neck of the bottle, against the scratches on the reed.

I'd bought a day with a sentence about axle grease. Mrs. Okafor would have called that classroom management.

In the lamplight beside me, the water in the weatherglass had started to climb.
