# Chapter 23: A Correct Arrest

It turns out the hardest part of a revolution is the next five minutes.

After Brakka said "Strategy," nobody in the valley moved. So Thessa did what she'd done for three months. She stood beside me.

She'd been one step behind me on my left the whole time, through the trial, through the rain, through the Purify. Now she stepped up, onto the planks of the platform, and stood at my left shoulder, the way Brakka was standing at my right. She didn't say anything. She didn't look at anyone. She just folded her hands in front of her, with the rain running off her braids and her charms, and stayed there.

The three of us stood in the rain, in front of the whole Concordance. An orc, an orc shaman's apprentice, and a soaked human holding a wig.

And then, from the front of the orc ranks, somebody broke and ran.

It was Okko. He came pelting across the open ground through the slush, all elbows and short tusks and armor too big for him, so fast he nearly slipped twice, and skidded to a halt on the planks beside Brakka. He was out of breath. He looked at me, wide-eyed, with rain streaming down his face, and then at Brakka, and then out at the host, as if he'd only just realized what he'd done.

He didn't leave. He just turned around, the way Brakka had, and stood there, very straight, trying to look like a soldier.

I think that was the moment the valley changed. Not Brakka. Brakka was Brakka; everyone knew he'd follow me into a volcano. But Okko was nobody. A kid from Ashtusk who'd fed apples to my horse. And if Okko could walk out of the ranks, then anyone could.

Warchief Gorza moved next.

She urged her huge black horse forward, out of the front of the orc companies, at a walk. The horse's hooves sank deep into the slush with every step. She rode across the open ground slowly, deliberately, the way she did everything, until she was in the middle of the field, between her people and the High Houses, and she stopped there and turned her horse to face the elves in their front row.

She was soaked to the skin. Her gray braids hung heavy with water. She didn't seem to notice.

"Last time," said Gorza, in a voice like stones rolling downhill, "House Vaunt starved us into it." She looked, for a long moment, straight at Caelith, standing frozen beside his chair. "This time, we choose." She lifted one huge arm and pointed it at the platform. "Ashtusk stands with the Listening Prince."

And behind her, the orc companies began to move.

Not all at once. One rank, and then another. They came across the field in their dark iron, in no particular order, not marching, just walking, the way people walk when they've made up their minds. They came and stood around the platform, and around Gorza's horse, a great dark crowd of them, in the rain. Some of them were humming. It took me a moment to recognize the tune. It was the Prince's song. My dad's song. Badly.

Then the wolf-folk.

Elder Ashka walked out from the flank of the host, alone at first, gray-muzzled, with her jeweled collar dark with rain. She stopped at the edge of the orcs, and looked up at the wall of Hearthhold for a long moment, at the people on it. Then she looked at me. "Whose land does the north become?" she said quietly. It was the question she'd asked at the Council of the Host, weeks ago. She answered it herself. "Theirs. It was always theirs." And she sat down on her haunches in the slush, at the edge of the orc crowd, and behind her, one by one, the wolf-folk came and did the same.

And then Thane Borrik, of the dwarves, cleared his throat.

He did it very loudly, the way you'd clear your throat before reading a document into the record. Everyone turned to look at him. He was standing at the head of the dwarf companies with his pen behind his ear and a roll of parchment in his hand, and he unrolled it with a snap.

"The payment schedule for the dwarven companies of the host," he announced, "was signed and sealed by the Prince, at the Council of the Host. In the presence of witnesses." He held it up. The rain spattered on it. "I have read it," said Thane Borrik. "Three times. And nowhere in it does it say Prince *of what*."

He rolled it back up, tucked it under his arm, and marched across the field toward the platform. The dwarves followed him, in perfect order, by company.

And then there was nobody left in the middle of the valley but the elves.

\* \* \*

They sat in their front row in the rain, in their soaked silks and their sodden cushions and their crooked, dripping ear-clips, and looked at each other.

For three hundred years, they had been the Concordance. The orcs and the dwarves and the beastfolk had followed where the High Houses led. And now, for the first time anybody could remember, the High Houses of the Silverwood were the ones sitting alone in the middle of a field, wondering what everyone else was going to do.

They looked at Caelith. Caelith didn't look back at them. He was staring at Captain Isaura.

They looked at each other again.

And then, in the middle of the front row, Lady Ithrien stood up.

She was holding her Prince's Canopy upside down, full of rainwater, like an enormous silk bowl. She didn't seem to know what to do with it, so she held it carefully in front of her with both hands. Her face had a look on it I'd seen many times before, at banquets and Blessings and council meetings: the expression of a woman who has been thinking very, very hard.

"The Codex of the First Line," said Lady Ithrien, to the whole valley, in a voice that wobbled only slightly, "describes the Old Kings as mighty of stature, great of appetite, and radiant."

The High Houses turned to look at her.

"I have read the passage," said Lady Ithrien. "Many times. I had it copied out and framed for my sitting room." She took a breath. "And last night I read it again. Twice. Very carefully." She looked around at the other High Lords, as if daring them to contradict her. "It doesn't say they were elves. Anywhere. It never once says *elves*."

A strange silence fell over the front row.

"He is mighty of stature," said Lady Ithrien, gaining confidence. She looked at me. "He is certainly great of appetite. *Everyone* knows that." A few of the High Lords, despite themselves, nodded. "And he *was* radiant." She paused, and added fairly: "Until the rain."

I stood there on the platform, dripping, holding my wig, and felt something extremely strange happen in my chest.

They were going to keep me.

The High Houses of the Silverwood, who had spent three centuries teaching their children that humans were monsters, were going to keep a human as their Prince. On a technicality. Because the Codex said *mighty of stature, great of appetite, and radiant*, and nobody, in four hundred years, had ever read it closely enough to notice that it didn't say anything else.

Nobody reads the rubric. I'd been saying it since my first month in the Silverwood. I'd just never expected it to save my life twice.

And then Lady Ithrien did something I will remember for the rest of my life. She reached up, slowly, thoughtfully, with one hand, and touched the tip of her own long, pointed ear, the one with the little silver clip pinning it out at a crooked angle. She held it for a moment, considering it, the way you'd consider a hat you'd been wearing all day.

Then she leaned toward the platform and said, to me, in a very low, urgent, confidential whisper, the kind you'd use to ask about a tailor:

"Highness. Where does one *get* round ones?"

Behind her, in the second row, I saw Durgan already pulling his ledger out from inside his soaked robe. He licked his pencil. And he wrote, in large capitals, so that I could read it from where I stood: *OLD BLOOD OUTFITTERS. NEW LINE. ROUND EARS. PRE-ORDERS OPEN.*

After that, it went quickly.

The High Dewkeeper, who had been weeping since the first drop of rain, stood up and laid his hand over his ear and announced that he was listening to the forest, and that the forest was entirely satisfied. Master Feyhallow consulted his leaf, and the leaf agreed. Lord Thandril of House Velloran, who had once spent an entire afternoon unable to say *goose*, stood and said that he had always found the Prince very good company. And one by one, the lords and ladies of the High Houses rose from their wet chairs, and stood, and turned toward the platform.

All except one.

Venrick sat alone at the end of the front row, in his soaked silver robes, with no cloak, blue to the lips, shaking with cold, staring straight ahead.

I watched him. Everybody did. If there was anyone in the Silverwood who would never, ever accept a human Prince, it was Lord Venrick of House Aldervane. He'd tried to unmake me on my first day. He'd invoked an ancient law to take my command away. He'd posted thirty-one questions on the Petition Wall to prove I was a fraud. And he'd been right. He'd been right the whole time.

He rose, slowly, stiffly, with his teeth chattering.

"I have no love for the Prince," said Venrick, in a voice like cracking ice. "I never have. I wish to state that for the record."

"Noted," said Lord Merrow faintly, from somewhere in the second row.

"But the Charter of the March bears the seal of House Aldervane," said Venrick, "and the seal of every House in this valley. And Article Nine is the law." He drew himself up, shivering, with the rain running off his nose. "I have spent my life defending the old laws of the Silverwood. I did not do so in order to throw one away the moment it becomes inconvenient." He turned, very deliberately, and looked straight at Caelith. "Lord Venrick of House Aldervane stands with the law."

I'd never liked Venrick. I'd never once, in three months, wanted to hug him.

I wanted to hug him.

\* \* \*

And that left Caelith.

He was standing alone beside his chair, in the middle of the front row, with every other elf in the valley on their feet and turned away from him. The rain ran down his silver hair. He didn't move. He didn't speak. He was looking at the platform: at me, at Brakka and Thessa and Okko, at the crowd of orcs and wolf-folk and dwarves around us, at the High Houses standing in their wet silks. I think he was doing the arithmetic, the way his guard had done it the day before, when I rode out of the human village. I think, for the first time in his life, it didn't come out right.

And then Silvaine stepped forward.

She'd been standing at the edge of the ring through all of it, perfectly still, with her ledger under one arm and her crystal's case at her hip. Now she walked out into the open ground, into the rain, precise and unhurried, and stopped in the exact center of the valley, between Caelith and the platform. She bowed to the assembled Houses. She bowed to the platform. She did not bow to Caelith.

"By the authority of the Inquisition of the Concordance," she said, "and before the assembled Houses, as the Charter of the March requires, I present evidence."

And she did. All of it. Properly.

She took the papers I'd given her out of a flat oilcloth case, one at a time, and held each one up so that the front rows could see it, and described it in a clear, carrying voice for the record. The first draft of the report on the Burning of the Sister Trees, bearing the seal of the royal archive, which named the place the fire had begun: *near the timber camp of House Vaunt.* The second draft, bearing the same seal, with that line struck out, and *Set by the Blight* written in the margin in a different hand. The land surveys, sealed and dated two years before the Purge, with the names of the High Houses written across every human village.

Then she opened her ledger, and read aloud, word for word, the question she had asked Caelith an hour earlier, in front of everyone: *Where did the timber camp of House Vaunt stand, in the year of the Burning?* And his answer, which she had written down exactly as he'd given it: *Some distance from the Sister Trees.*

She held up the first draft beside it.

She didn't say anything else. She didn't have to. Four thousand people were perfectly capable of reading.

"The fire was three hundred years ago," said Caelith. His voice was very quiet. "Whatever House Vaunt did then, it was not done by me."

"No," Silvaine agreed. "It was not."

She closed her ledger.

"The Inquisition calls Captain Isaura of the household guard of House Vaunt," she said.

The valley went very quiet again.

Captain Isaura dismounted. She did it slowly, stiffly, like someone whose knees have been riding horses for six hundred years. She walked out into the open ground and stood beside Silvaine, in her plain, dented armor, with the long pale scar down her face, and the rain running off it. She didn't look at Caelith. She looked straight ahead, at the platform, at nothing.

And she testified.

It wasn't a speech. It was a report. She told the assembled Houses, in a flat, steady, soldier's voice, that on the night of the Council of the Host, in the Silverwood, the High Lord of House Vaunt had summoned her and two other officers of the household guard to his study. That he had told them of a human settlement in the north, which the scouts had found three weeks earlier, and which he had not reported to the Council. That he had ordered the Concordance troops to make camp a day's march back, for reasons of supply, so that the guard of House Vaunt alone would reach the settlement first. That he had ordered them to take the valley, and leave no witnesses. And that he had ordered them to keep the Prince in front, in full view, at every moment that mattered, for the paintings.

"And if the Prince refused," said Captain Isaura, "we were told he would become a Prince who fell bravely in battle."

She stopped. She'd said everything. She didn't add anything about conscience, or regret, or how she felt. I don't think it would have occurred to her. She'd been asked what happened, and she'd told them.

And then Silvaine reached into the oilcloth case one more time and took out a last sheet of paper. Heavy. Folded. Sealed in black wax with the sigil of the Inquisition.

"A Writ of Arrest," she said. "Issued last night, and duly sealed, upon receipt of the evidence. Section Four of the Inquisitorial Code." She broke the seal, and unfolded it, and read it out in a voice that didn't tremble at all.

"Caelith of House Vaunt, High Lord of the Silverwood. I arrest you for concealing the settlement of Hearthhold from the assembled Houses of the Concordance, and for ordering its destruction in violation of Article Nine of the sealed Charter of the March."

Two soldiers of the household guard of House Vaunt, his own guard, came forward at a nod from Captain Isaura and stood on either side of him.

Caelith didn't resist. I'll give him that. He didn't shout, or plead, or reach for a sword. He looked at the two soldiers on either side of him, and at Captain Isaura, and at the High Houses, who wouldn't meet his eyes. Then he straightened his soaked silver robes, very slightly, with a small, elegant movement of his hands, and walked.

His path took him past the platform. And as he came level with me, he stopped.

He looked up at me for a long moment. At the wet wig in my hand, and the round ears, and the bedsheet cape. His face was calm again. He'd found the mask somewhere, and put it back on. But there was something underneath it now that hadn't been there before. I think it might have been curiosity.

"Sad men," said Caelith softly, "make the best kings, Highness."

I thought about it. I thought about four days in a window seat with the drafts in my lap. I thought about the Golden Bird, and the barrel, and a lesson plan with *Do something* written on it.

"I wasn't sad," I said. "I was taking notes."

He looked at me a moment longer. Then something happened at the corner of his mouth that I'd never seen before: not a smile, exactly. Something smaller and more real than a smile. Then the guards moved, and he walked on, through the rain, and didn't look back.

And Silvaine of House Thistlevane, Inquisitor of the Concordance, youngest in its history, who had made forty-five arrests in three hundred and twelve years and been wrong every single time, stood in the middle of the valley and looked down at the writ in her hands. Her forty-sixth.

I watched it hit her.

It started in her face. The composure didn't crack, exactly. It just slowly drained away, until she looked very young, and very pale, and very surprised, like someone who had just been told that a lifelong illness was cured. She looked at the writ. She looked at Caelith's back, walking away between the guards. She looked at her ledger. Her lips moved, very slightly, as if she were counting.

Then her knees gave out, and she sat down, abruptly, on the first thing behind her.

It was my umbrella. The one I'd laid in the snow beside the Prince's chair. There was a loud, sharp crunch of splitting reeds.

She didn't notice. She just sat there on the flattened remains of the first umbrella in the history of Verathis, in the rain, staring at nothing, holding her writ.

Beside me, Brakka cleared his throat.

"Forty-six," he said helpfully. "One right."

\* \* \*

The rain began to ease a little after that. Not to stop. Just to soften, from a solid wall to a steady gray curtain, the kind you could see through.

I still had a judgment to give. Under the Charter, the trial wasn't over until the presiding Prince had spoken and the assembled Houses had voted. So, standing on the platform in the rain, in my bedsheet cape, with Brakka on one side and Thessa on the other and a few hundred orcs behind me, I gave it.

I didn't use Aelindor's voice. I didn't make a speech. I'd made enough speeches. I just said it plainly, the way you'd read out the answer to a test.

That the people of Hearthhold were a people of the forest, under the Archive Charter, the oldest law in the Silverwood, and the Book of the Reckoning, which was a primary source. That under Article Nine of the Charter of the March, they could not be put to the sword. That the charge against them was dismissed. And that every Grant drawn on the Charter's maps across the northern lands, every valley and river and frozen lake with a House's name written across it in neat black ink, was void.

Then I sat down in the Prince's chair, because my legs had stopped working.

Lord Merrow, the Treasurer, rose from the second row to call the vote. He was soaked to the bone and looked as though he'd aged another century in the last hour, but he did it properly, with rules of order, the way I'd taught the Charter committee. He read out the judgment. He called for a second. Thane Borrik seconded it, instantly, without even being recognized, and nobody objected. Then Lord Merrow called the roll of the assembled Houses, one by one, and every House answered.

It passed. Not unanimously. House Vaunt didn't vote, because there was nobody left to vote for it. Lord Thandril of House Velloran voted aye in a very small voice, because the Charter's maps had promised him an entire lake. But it passed, overwhelmingly. Venrick voted aye, through chattering teeth, for the law. Lady Ithrien voted aye, and then burst into tears, and then voted aye again, in case the first one hadn't counted.

And on top of the wall, where every soul in Hearthhold had been watching in total silence, the children's committee held a vote of their own.

I saw it happen. Davy stood up on the wall, holding the wooden spoon, and called for a show of hands, and seven small hands shot up into the rain. The girl with the slate wrote something down. Then Davy turned toward the valley and announced, at the top of his lungs, that the committee had also passed it, unanimously.

It didn't count. Nobody told him that. Somebody in the orc crowd started to cheer, and then everybody did: orcs and dwarves and wolf-folk, and then, slowly, the people of Hearthhold on their wall, who had been standing so still for so long that some of them didn't seem to remember how to move. And then, after a moment of uncertainty, a good number of the High Houses too.

I sat in the Prince's chair in the rain and let it wash over me.

It was some time after that, when the crowd had started to break up and drift and mingle, orcs wandering up toward the wall, children leaning over the top of it to stare at the dwarves, that Corvel came over to the platform.

He was carrying his canvas. He held it out in front of him with both hands, carefully, like something that might break, because it was still wet. The rain had gotten into it in places and made the colors run. He stood at the foot of the platform and turned it around so I could see.

It was the moment in the rain.

Not the way he'd have painted it a week ago. There was no white stallion. No silver sword. No chin of inevitable heroism. It was a young man in a soaked green tunic and a bedsheet cape, standing on a platform of rough planks, holding a wet blond wig in one hand at his side. His hair was brown and plastered flat. His ears were round, and red. His face was streaked with something silver that was running down it with the rain. And beside him, enormous and gray and still, stood an orc with one ear, facing out.

It was the best thing I'd ever seen him paint. It was the only true thing I'd ever seen him paint.

"It's not finished," Corvel said. "The caption." He looked up at me, and his face was doing something I'd never seen it do before. He was hesitating. "I wanted to get it right." He swallowed. "Forgive me, Highness. How do you spell *Delaney*?"

I spelled it for him. He wrote it down, very carefully, letter by letter, in the corner of the canvas.

It was the first time, as far as I know, that Corvel had ever checked a fact.

\* \* \*

We wrote the treaty in the meeting hall of Hearthhold, over the next three days, around the long table worn smooth by three hundred years of elbows.

I say *we*. In practice, I held the pen, because somebody had to, and because everyone in the room had decided, without asking me, that I was the one who knew how these things were done. Which was funny, because I didn't. I'm a seventh-grade history teacher, and a student one at that. I'd never written a treaty in my life. What I had, instead, was the United States Constitution, or at least the parts of it I could remember from school. So I used that.

I remembered the preamble perfectly. Every word. I'd learned it in fourth grade from a cartoon, the way everybody in my generation did, and I could still hear the tune in my head. So the treaty of Hearthhold begins *We the peoples of the forest, in order to form a more perfect union*, and goes on from there, with a few adjustments for local conditions.

After that, it got patchier.

I remembered freedom of speech. Everybody liked that one. I remembered the right to a fair trial, in front of witnesses, which nobody in that room needed explained. I remembered something about not being searched without a warrant, which Silvaine, who had been invited as a witness for the Inquisition and was sitting very upright at the end of the table, insisted on writing herself, in precise language, with three subsections.

And, to my own surprise, I remembered the Third Amendment, word for word, more or less. The one nobody ever talks about. *No soldier shall, in time of peace, be quartered in any house, without the consent of the Owner.*

I was going to leave it out. It seemed a little specific. But when I read it aloud, the elders of Hearthhold looked at each other, and then out the narrow window toward the ravine, where three hundred soldiers of House Vaunt's household guard had been camped for the better part of a week, and they voted, unanimously, to keep it.

The rest, I mostly made up. I'm not proud of that. But I'm not ashamed of it, either. Every constitution starts with somebody making something up.

And everybody added something.

Ruth Ashgrove went first. Before I'd written a word of the articles, she put her rough hand flat on the parchment, over the space at the top, and said quietly, "This goes first. Above everything else." Then she told me what to write, and I wrote it, in the largest letters I could make with a silver pen.

*Article One. Take only what you can give back.*

Nobody argued.

Warchief Gorza added a clause guaranteeing grain to the families of any people of the forest, in any season, in any year, so that no one would ever be starved into anything again. Thane Borrik added payment schedules, in writing, for everything, which took up the better part of two pages and which he read through four times before he would sign. Elder Ashka of the wolf-folk added a seat, for the beastfolk, in every council of the forest, from that day on. The elders of Hearthhold added the forests: every tree cut, a tree planted, with a notched stake beside it to mark the year. And they added an invitation. Any forester of the Silverwood who wished to come north and learn how it was done would be welcome in Hearthhold, and fed.

Lady Ithrien added one amendment of her own. She proposed it on the second afternoon, rising to be recognized with tremendous dignity, and read it aloud from a slip of paper she'd clearly been working on all night.

*The right of the people to a main course shall not be infringed.*

It passed at once. Nobody even called for a vote. Master Orrin, back in the Silverwood, would have wept.

And on the third day, as we were reading the final draft aloud, article by article, Venrick and Lord Thandril of House Velloran rose to be recognized at exactly the same moment. They'd both noticed it at once: a comma in the list of the peoples of the forest, in the second article. Or rather, the absence of one.

They stood there, glaring at each other across the table, both drawing breath to begin.

Ruth Ashgrove didn't even look up from her copy.

"One week," she said quietly.

They both sat down.

Durgan, meanwhile, spent all three days explaining to anyone who would stand still long enough that the summoning had been intended to bring peace from the very beginning. That this outcome had been specifically anticipated in the Guild's rites, under Section Fourteen, Subsection C, *Unforeseen Benefits*. That he had, in fact, foreseen all of it, and simply hadn't mentioned it at the time, out of professional modesty. Nobody believed him. He didn't seem to need them to.

The treaty of Hearthhold was signed on the evening of the third day, at the long table, by Ruth Ashgrove for the people of Hearthhold, and Gorza for the orcs, and Thane Borrik for the dwarves, and Elder Ashka for the beastfolk, and the assembled Houses of the Silverwood, one by one. And then they all looked at me, and handed me the pen, and I signed it too.

I signed my own name. It was the first time in Verathis I'd ever written it down.

Durgan filed for his second bonus that night. Under *Services rendered*, he'd written one word: *Peace.*

I took out my lesson plan. Under *Assessment*, below the line from my first night that I'd crossed out weeks ago, *Still alive by Friday*, I wrote: *Still alive. It was Friday.* And then, after a moment: *Note: they do have Fridays.*
