# Chapter 15: The War Council

I tried, very hard, to run a bad meeting. It turns out I don't know how.

Caelith called the Council of the Host on the morning after the Charter was signed. Not the High Houses this time. Everyone. Every war leader of the Concordance: the elven generals of the High Houses, the orc chieftains, the dwarf envoy, the beastfolk delegates. Everyone who would be sending soldiers north in eight days' time, gathered in the Hall of Crowns to plan the march. And as the Lost Prince of the First Line, commander of the host by right of the Trial, I would preside.

Thessa and I had planned it the night before, by the fire.

"Same as the Charter," I'd said. "Let them talk. Let them argue. Recognize the angriest people first. Call for more information whenever anything gets close. Make it go long."

"How long?"

"Eight days, ideally."

She'd nodded. She'd been taking notes.

It was a good plan. I really believed in it. I believed in it right up until I walked through the doors of the Hall of Crowns and saw the room.

The High Houses sat in the front tiers, in a solid block of silver, Old Bloods on the left and Starlights on the right, glaring across the aisle. The orc chieftains had taken the back corner, where they always sat, as far from the elves as the room allowed, arms folded. The beastfolk delegates stood along the windows, in a nervous cluster, because nobody had offered them chairs. And the dwarf envoy sat by himself in the exact middle of the room, alone, in a little island of empty seats, because nobody would sit near him.

I looked at it, and something in my brain that I don't control took over.

I've walked into a lot of classrooms that looked exactly like that. On the first day of school, kids sort themselves. The popular ones take the back. The quiet ones take the corners. The new kid sits alone in the middle. And if you let them stay that way, the class never becomes a class. It just stays a room full of little countries, at war.

So before anybody could stop me, and honestly, before I could stop myself, I picked up a sheet of parchment and a pen from Lord Merrow's clerk's table and said, "Okay, everyone, before we start. Seating chart."

The Hall of Crowns went silent.

And then I moved them. I put Starlights next to Old Bloods, alternating. I put the orc chieftains in the front row, where they could be heard, next to elven generals who had never in their lives sat beside an orc. I brought chairs for the beastfolk and put them at the center of the room. I sat the dwarf envoy between the wolf-folk elder and Lady Ithrien, who was so startled she forgot to be offended. And I sat Lord Venrick directly beside Warchief Gorza of Ashtusk, who was about twice his width and grinned at him with every tusk she had.

Nobody argued. That's the thing about seating charts. Nobody ever argues, because they're too surprised.

From across the room, Thessa caught my eye with a look that said, very clearly, *What are you doing?*

I didn't know. That was the honest answer. I was already in too deep to stop.

She sighed, and came forward, and handed me the talking stick.

We'd talked about this the night before, too. It was a branch of weepwood, about as long as my forearm, silver-barked, from the grove. It hadn't been cut. It had fallen in a storm, a week after the grove was stripped, and Thessa had picked it up and carried it home and kept it in a pot of water in her room, and tended it every day. The bark was healing. There were little green buds on it now.

"Only the person holding this branch may speak," I told the Council of the Host. "Everyone else listens."

I'd meant it as a way to slow things down. It didn't.

\* \* \*

The trouble was that I only know how to run one kind of difficult meeting. They train you for it in your education courses. It's the parent-teacher conference.

And a parent-teacher conference has rules. You always start with something positive. You ask everyone to speak about their own concerns, in "I" statements, not to attack anybody else's. You let every person in the room be heard, completely, before anybody responds. You write the concerns down where everybody can see them. And then you look for an answer that works for everyone at the table, because if one person walks out of that room feeling unheard, you'll be having the same meeting again in a month.

I didn't mean to do any of it. My mouth just started doing it on its own.

"Let's start," I heard myself say, "with something that's going well."

There was a baffled pause. Then Lady Ithrien raised her hand to be recognized, the way she'd learned in the Charter committee, and I handed her the branch. She held it in both hands, as if it might bite.

"The pie," she said, sincerely.

The whole room laughed. Even some of the Starlights. And just like that, the temperature in the Hall of Crowns dropped about ten degrees.

Venrick went next, since he couldn't bear to let Lady Ithrien have the last word. "The trouble with the orcs," he began, "is that they—"

"'I' statements," I said gently. "About your own concerns."

Venrick stared at me. Then, with the air of a man swallowing a lemon, he started again. "*I* feel," he said, "that the orcs are—"

"About you, Lord Venrick."

A long pause. "I feel," Venrick said, at last, through his teeth, "annoyed."

"Thank you for sharing that," I said, and I meant it, a little.

He tried to keep talking after that, and when Gorza reached for the branch, he kept right on holding it. And Brakka, still my sergeant-at-arms, crossed the room in four enormous strides and took it out of his hand, as gently as you'd take a toy from a toddler, and handed it to Gorza.

The warchief of Ashtusk stood up. She was old and gray and built like a stone bridge, and when she held the little weepwood branch in her enormous fist, it looked like a twig.

"I feel," she said, slowly, trying out the strange new grammar, "that my people will need grain. For the families of the fighters, while the fighters march." She looked across the room, at Caelith, sitting very still at the end of the front row, and something passed over her weathered face. "Last time," she said, "grain was a problem."

That was all. She sat down.

I wrote GRAIN on a big sheet of parchment and pinned it to the wall.

The dwarf envoy took the branch next. His name was Thane Borrik, and he was the one who'd turned his chair around to avoid Durgan at my presentation, and he spoke in short, sharp, practical sentences. He felt that the dwarven companies would need their pay agreed in advance. In writing. With a schedule. Since, he said, looking at no one in particular, promises made before a war had a way of looking different after one.

Before I could answer, a voice rose from the back of the hall, where Durgan had come along to watch, as he put it, "in a ritual capacity."

"Point of information," said Durgan.

He came down the aisle with his robe flapping and a sheet of parchment held over his head like a banner, and laid it on the table in front of Thane Borrik with a flourish. "Form 40-C," he announced. "Schedule of Remittance for Mercenary Companies. Standard Guild format. I took the liberty of filling it in."

Thane Borrik, who had spent my entire time in the Silverwood refusing to look at Durgan Pell, looked at the form for a long moment. Then he read it, all the way through, with his lips pressed together. Then he picked up a pen and signed it.

"It's adequate," he said gruffly, without looking up.

Durgan went back up the aisle glowing like a lamp.

I wrote PAY: IN WRITING under GRAIN.

And then the elder of the wolf-folk took the branch.

She was the oldest of them, gray-muzzled, with a jeweled collar and pale yellow eyes, and her name was Ashka. She held the stick quietly for a moment. Then she said, in a low, rough voice, the thing nobody else in the room had thought to say.

"I feel that I must ask," she said, "whose land the north becomes. When it is done." She looked at the High Houses. "The beastfolk will march. We have seen the maps. We were not on them."

The silence after that was the longest of the day.

I didn't think about it. That was the problem. I didn't think about what I was doing, or what it would mean. I just did what I'd do if a kid in my class had pointed out that the group project had left her out. I said, "That's fair," and I wrote BEASTFOLK on the parchment, and then I turned to the High Houses and asked them, very reasonably, whether there might be room on the maps for everyone who was marching.

And there was. Of course there was. The north was huge. It took twenty minutes.

It went on like that for three hours. Everybody spoke. Everybody was heard. Every concern went up on the wall. And then, one by one, I worked through them, the way I'd work through a list of parent concerns, looking for the thing that would let everyone at the table walk away feeling like they'd won something. A grain guarantee for the orcs, written into the orders of march. A payment schedule for the dwarves, signed by the Treasurer. A corner of the northern maps for the beastfolk. A provisions compromise for the Starlights and the Old Bloods that let each army company choose, which both sides immediately declared a triumph.

At the end of three hours, I called for a vote on the plan of march.

It was unanimous.

The Hall of Crowns sat in stunned silence for a moment. Then the room broke out in applause. Not the polite, cello-recital kind. Real applause. The elven generals were clapping orc chieftains on the back. Lady Ithrien was weeping into her handkerchief again. Thane Borrik shook my hand with a grip like a vise and said, "Well run, Highness. Very well run." And Warchief Gorza came up to me afterward, towering over me, and put one enormous hand on my shoulder, and looked down at me with an expression I couldn't read.

"Nobody has ever asked the orcs before," she said gruffly. "About anything."

Then she walked away.

And it was only then, standing at the front of the room with the applause going on around me, that I noticed something about the last three hours.

Not one person in that room had said what the army was actually going to do when it got there. We'd talked about grain, and pay, and maps, and whether the Starlights could have dew. We'd talked about wagons, and command, and the order of march. Nobody had said the word. It's amazing how much of a war you can plan without ever once saying what it's for.

I stood at the front of the Hall of Crowns, holding the talking stick, with the cheering going on around me, and I felt the floor go out from under me, just a little. Not literally. I'd had enough of that.

I'd walked into that room to run the worst meeting in the history of the Silverwood. To waste every hour of the eight days we had left. And instead, because I couldn't help myself, I'd done the one thing I was actually trained to do. I'd made everyone feel heard. I'd solved every one of their problems. I'd turned a room full of feuding factions into a team.

I hadn't stalled the war. I'd made it work better.

It was the most productive council in the history of the Silverwood. Lady Ithrien said so, at dinner, about forty times. Every time she said it, I felt a little sicker.

\* \* \*

The Council of the Host broke up a little before sunset, in a warm, pleased, chattering crowd. The orc chieftains left talking with elven generals. Thane Borrik left with a signed payment schedule rolled up under his arm and something very close to a smile. The wolf-folk left last, slowly, looking back at me over their shoulders.

Caelith didn't leave.

He sat in his place at the end of the front row until the hall had emptied out, completely still, his long hands folded on his knee. Then he rose and came over to me, where I was standing alone at the front of the room, still holding the weepwood branch.

"Walk with me, Highness," he said.

It wasn't a request. I knew that much. I handed the branch to Thessa, who'd been waiting by the door with Brakka, and I saw her face, and I shook my head at her, very slightly. *It's fine. Stay here.*

It wasn't fine. But I didn't want her anywhere near what was about to happen.

Caelith led me out of the Hall of Crowns, along a corridor, and up.

I'd never been to the top of the palace before. The tallest of the great trees that made up the Silverwood palace rose out of its center, and a staircase spiraled up inside the trunk, around and around, lit by floating lamps in little alcoves cut into the living wood. We climbed for a long time. Caelith didn't speak, and he didn't seem to get tired. I was breathing hard by the halfway point and trying very hard not to show it. My ears were holding, at least. Thessa had done them that morning.

At the very top, there was a door, and behind the door was a study.

It was a round room, not large, with tall windows on every side. In the last of the evening light, I could see out over the whole of the Silverwood, the canopy rolling away in every direction like a gray-green sea, under rain. A desk sat by the north window. On it there was a lamp, a glass of wine that hadn't been drunk, a small dish full of coins, and a single old letter, worn soft with handling, held open by a smooth gray stone.

Caelith went to the window and stood looking out at the rain, with his back to me, for a long moment.

Then he turned around, and I knew we were done pretending.

"You read the drafts, Highness," Caelith said. "Both of them."

I didn't answer. There didn't seem to be any point. He knew. I didn't know how he knew, but he did, and he wasn't asking. Only six people had been in that room: me, Thessa, Brakka, Durgan, Quillon, and the quiet apprentice who'd brought the lamps.

"Then you know," said Caelith, "how a House is made."

He said it gently, the way you'd say something to a student who'd just figured out a hard problem. Not cruel. Almost kind.

"I want to be honest with you," he went on, "because I think you've earned it. You're cleverer than I expected. The Charter was very clever. The council today was..." He paused, searching for the word, and smiled. "Remarkable. I've sat through four hundred years of councils. I've never seen anything like it."

"Thank you," I said. My voice sounded strange to me.

"So I'll tell you what the council doesn't know." He turned back to the window, toward the north, and the rain. "Our scouts didn't just see one of the Blight, this spring. That was only the first sighting. They followed the tracks. They found smoke. And then, three weeks ago, they found a settlement." He let that sit for a moment. "A few hundred of them, living up in the ice. Like moss on a stone. Three hundred years, and they're still there."

A few hundred people.

I'd been imagining one. Since the banquet, since Caelith had stood up in the Hall of the Eldertree and told the court about a single human sighted in the wastes, I'd been picturing one person. Somebody alone, in the cold. Somebody like me. I'd been trying, every day for weeks, to save one person.

It was a few hundred.

"The council doesn't know," Caelith said. "The council doesn't need to know. The council has agreed to march north and cleanse the Blight from the lands that its Houses have just divided among themselves, in writing, under your seal. That's all the council needs to know." He turned back to me. "When we reach the settlement, my own household guard will go first. Not the orcs. Not the beastfolk. No one who might... talk. We'll take the land, every valley on the Charter's maps. And we'll leave no witnesses."

The rain beat against the windows. I could hear my own heart.

"And you, Highness," said Caelith, "will ride at the front."

"Why?"

He seemed genuinely surprised by the question. "Because the painters will need a face," he said. "There'll be paintings of the Second Cleansing, Highness, for centuries. Tapestries. Songs. History needs a hero." He smiled. "History remembers faces. It always has. And yours is the face of the Lost Prince of the First Line, returned in the Silverwood's hour of need. You're the best thing that could have happened to this war."

I thought about the tapestry in my bedroom. The silver-armored elves. The burning forest. The small round-eared figure in the corner, holding someone's hand. I'd always wondered who the elf at the front of that tapestry was supposed to be.

Now I knew. It was going to be me.

Caelith went to his desk, and opened a drawer, and took out a small rolled sheet of parchment. He unrolled it on the desk, in the lamplight, and weighted it with the smooth gray stone. It was a map: a single valley in the north, with a river running through it, and a lake. And across it, in neat black ink, in his elegant handwriting, was one word.

*Aelindor.*

"The invoice for your summoning will be forgiven," Caelith said. "All of it. Every crown. Durgan Pell will be paid by House Vaunt. And when the north is cleansed, this valley will be yours. Your own Grant. Your own House, Highness. The first new House of the First Line in three hundred years." He looked at me. "Everything a prince could want."

I looked at my name in black ink on the map. I didn't say anything.

Caelith waited. When I still said nothing, his voice didn't change at all. It stayed exactly as gentle as before.

"Of course," he said, "I hope you won't disappoint the Houses. Princes who disappoint the Houses have a way of becoming... Lost. Again."

"I signed Form 12-B," I said. It came out before I could stop it, and it sounded ridiculous even to me. "I'm non-refundable."

Caelith laughed softly. It was the first time I'd heard him laugh at something I'd said and actually find it funny.

"Forms burn, Highness," he said.

Then he came around the desk and stood in front of me, and looked down at me, very kindly, the way you'd look at someone you felt sorry for.

"You're sad," he said. "I know. I've watched you since the archive. You've been sad for weeks. You walk around my palace like a man carrying something heavy." He laid one long hand on my shoulder, lightly, for just a moment. "That's good, Highness. That's very good. Sad men make the best kings."

He lifted his hand away.

"They don't do anything."

I didn't say anything. I didn't tell him about Article Nine, on the seventh page of the Charter of the March, between the axle grease and the oat rations. I didn't tell him that I'd read his rubric, and he hadn't read mine.

I just bowed, the correct bow of a Prince to a High Lord, and went down the long spiral stair, around and around, in the dark.

\* \* \*

Thessa was waiting in my chambers, sitting on the rug by the fire with the weepwood branch across her knees. She didn't ask. She just looked up at my face when I came in, and then she got up and latched the door behind me, and then she sat back down and waited.

So I told her. All of it. The settlement. The few hundred. The household guard going in first so that no one else would see. The painters. The valley with my name on it. Forms burn.

She listened without moving, the way she'd listened on the night she found me with my ears off.

When I was done, she was quiet for a while. Then she said, "Gorza talked to me. After the council. In the corridor."

I looked up.

"She knew who I was. Ashtusk." Thessa turned the weepwood branch over in her hands, slowly. "She said: *Last time, House Vaunt starved us into it. This time, they offer us grain.*" Thessa looked at the fire. "She said: *That's what passes for progress.*"

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say.

I went to my desk and found my copy of the Charter of the March, the one Lord Merrow had made for the Prince, and brought it to the rug, and unrolled the maps from the back of it across the floor in front of the fire. The northern wastes, covered in House names. Valleys and rivers and frozen lakes, all carved up and assigned. And somewhere in there, under all of that neat black ink, there were a few hundred people who didn't know yet that they'd already been divided.

We looked at it together for a long time.

"Eight days," I said.

Thessa nodded slowly. "Then we have eight days," she said.

Not *you*. *We.*

I got up and went back to my desk, and opened my lesson plan, the same sheet of royal parchment I'd started on my first night in the Silverwood, which by now was soft at the edges and smudged with resin and covered in crossings-out. I found *Objective 2: Stop the war*, in my own handwriting.

*Sad men make the best kings,* he'd said. *They don't do anything.*

He was right. That was the worst part. I'd spent weeks being sad, and careful, and clever, and quiet: stalling, delaying, buying time, slipping a sentence into the middle of an article about axle grease. And all that time, Caelith had been patiently building his war on top of everything I did. He'd let me stall because stalling was just a way of not doing anything.

I picked up the silver pen. Under *Objective 2*, I wrote two words.

*Do something.*

Then I sat back and looked across the room at the corner by the balcony door, where my umbrella leaned against the wall, the first one, the one that leaned a little to the left. And at the mirror the size of a garage door, where every morning Thessa glued my ears on crooked. And at the bedsheet cape over the back of the chair, and Gwendolyn, too clean, on her stand.

Caelith wanted a face for his paintings. He was going to get one. Just not the one he was expecting.

## Interlude: The Orders

Three officers of House Vaunt's household guard climbed the spiral stair to the High Lord's study that same night, after the Prince had gone down it.

They were not generals of the Concordance. They had not been at the Council of the Host, and they had not sat beside orcs, or passed a talking stick, or been asked how they felt. They were Caelith's own: elves who had served House Vaunt for centuries, as their parents had served it, and their parents' parents. They stood at attention on the far side of his desk, in the lamplight, and waited.

Caelith gave his orders the way he did everything: quietly, in order, leaving nothing out.

The host of the Concordance would march north on the day the rains ended, as the Prince had foretold. It would march to the settlement the scouts had found. When it arrived, the Concordance troops would make camp a full day's march back, for reasons of supply, which the Treasurer would provide in writing. The household guard of House Vaunt alone would go forward.

"Take the land," said Caelith. "Every valley on the Charter's maps. Leave no witnesses."

The officers did not ask what he meant. They had served House Vaunt for a long time.

"One more thing," Caelith said. "The Prince must be in front. At every moment that matters, in full view. A painter of House Vaunt will travel with the host, and I want the Prince in every sketch." He turned the glass of wine on his desk slowly, without lifting it. "History remembers faces."

The eldest of the three officers hesitated. She had served Caelith's grandmother, and his mother, and she was the only one of them who ever hesitated.

"And if the Prince refuses, my lord?"

Caelith considered the question with real interest, as if it were a puzzle he'd been given to pass the time.

"Then he'll be a Prince who fell bravely in battle," he said, "at the head of his host, against the last of the Blight." He smiled. "The painters can work with that, too."

The officers bowed, and went down the stair.

When they had gone, the High Lord of the Silverwood sat alone at his desk by the north window, with the rain running down the glass. After a while, he reached out and moved the smooth gray stone aside, and drew the old letter toward him. It had been written to him a very long time ago, by his great-grandmother, on the day the iron door was sealed. He had read it so many times that the creases had worn through in places.

He didn't read it now. He only laid one long finger on the signature at the bottom, and traced, slowly, the shape of the V, with the little flourish on its tail.
