# Chapter 4: Spirit Gum Fails in Water

Mrs. Okafor always said a good lesson starts with a clear objective. Mine was not getting murdered.

I wrote it at the top of a sheet of royal parchment at four in the morning: my first complete lesson plan, finished on time, in another world.

I hadn't slept. Every time I closed my eyes, I saw four hundred elves cheering, and Caelith's lovely, empty smile, and a small round-eared figure stitched into the corner of a tapestry, holding someone's hand. So around three, I gave up, lit one of the floating lamps, found a writing desk in the sitting room stocked with parchment and a silver pen that refilled itself, and did the only thing I know how to do when I'm scared. I planned.

It looked like this:

> **LESSON PLAN**
>
> **Unit:** Surviving Verathis
>
> **Lesson 1:** Not Getting Murdered
>
> **Objective:** Student (me) will be able to not get murdered.
>
> **Materials:** One elf costume, worn. One wig (Gwendolyn). One foam sword, bent. Spirit gum, approx. half a thimble. One phone, 38%, off.
>
> **Known problems:**
>
> 1. The glue is running out.
> 2. Water undoes everything.
> 3. Caelith is watching.
> 4. I have been asked to lead a genocide. Of me.
>
> **Procedure:**
>
> 1. Buy time.
> 2. Find allies.
> 3. Get information.
>
> **Glue alternatives:** Tree sap? Research.
>
> **Assessment:** Still alive by Friday.
>
> *(Note: Find out if they have Fridays.)*

I read it over twice. Then I underlined "Buy time," because it was the only thing on the list I knew how to start on.

Here's a secret about lesson plans that nobody tells you in your education courses: the plan isn't really for the class. Twenty-eight seventh graders will blow up any plan you make within the first ten minutes. The plan is for you, so that when everything goes sideways, you have something to hold onto that isn't panic.

Mrs. Okafor told me that, the first week. I'd rolled my eyes a little.

At four in the morning in an elven palace, with a genocide on my calendar, I held onto that sheet of parchment like a life raft. It worked. By the time the sky outside the balcony started going gray, my hands had stopped shaking.

That was when someone knocked on the door and told me it was time for my blessing.

\* \* \*

The Blessing of Dew, it turned out, is a ritual welcome for anyone returning to the Silverwood from a long journey. Mine had been about as long as journeys get, so I was told it would be a big one.

They led me down to a walled garden behind the palace, where the grass was silver with dew and the light was just coming up pink over the treetops. About fifty courtiers had gathered along the paths to watch, in robes the colors of early morning. Lady Ithrien was among them, looking pale and slightly shaky, the way people look the morning after their first real meal in four centuries. She gave me a weak, adoring wave.

In the center of the garden stood a flat white stone, and beside it stood the High Dewkeeper.

He was very old and very solemn, and he wore robes of pale green, like new leaves. Four young acolytes stood around him, each holding a long branch of some silver-leafed tree. The leaves were dripping.

"Kneel, Highness," the High Dewkeeper said gently, "and receive the dew of your homeland."

I looked at the branches. I looked at the dripping. I thought about item two on my lesson plan.

There was no way out of it. You can't invent a vow against dew in the middle of a garden in front of fifty witnesses; even I knew that. So I knelt on the white stone, bowed my head, and prayed to whatever gods watch over latex.

The acolytes raised their branches and shook them.

The dew came down like a cold, sparkling rain. It was actually beautiful. It got in my eyelashes and ran down the back of my neck, under Gwendolyn. And I felt the left ear go.

Not all of it. Just the edge, lifting off my skin like a Band-Aid in a swimming pool. I felt the cool air hit the gap. Another shake, and it would peel off entirely, and I would be kneeling in a garden in front of fifty elves with one round ear sticking out of my wig.

I clapped my hand over it.

I held it there, hard, and closed my eyes, and started counting to thirty.

The acolytes stopped shaking. The garden went silent. When I opened my eyes, everyone was staring at me: the acolytes, the courtiers, Lady Ithrien with her mouth slightly open. The High Dewkeeper had frozen with one hand raised.

"Highness?" he said.

I had about two seconds. I used one of them to think, and the other to lower my voice.

"Forgive me," I said softly. "I'm listening."

The High Dewkeeper blinked. "Listening, Highness? To what?"

"To the forest," I said. "It's welcoming me home."

The tongue-binding made it sound like the most beautiful thing anyone had ever said in that garden. Possibly it was. I watched the High Dewkeeper's ancient face crumple. His lip trembled. A single tear ran down his cheek and joined the dew.

"In four hundred years of the Blessing," he whispered, "no one has ever thought to *listen*."

He placed his own hand over his own ear and closed his eyes.

Then one of the acolytes did it. Then Lady Ithrien, with a little gasp of feeling. Then a courtier on the path, and another, and another, until I was kneeling in the middle of a garden full of elves with their eyes closed and one hand pressed over one ear, all of them listening very hard to a forest that, as far as I could tell, wasn't saying anything at all.

The ceremony took another twenty minutes. I kept my hand over my ear for every second of it.

Walking back to my chambers afterward, still holding it, I did the math. A few shakes of a wet branch had nearly ended me. A few shakes of a wet branch, on a sunny morning, in a garden.

I didn't want to think about what rain would do.

I added it to the list anyway. I'm a planner now.

\* \* \*

Back in my chambers, I spent another precious drop of glue on the left ear. When I held the bottle up to the light, there was enough for one more try. Maybe.

I was still looking at it when someone knocked. I put the bottle away and said, "Come in," in my best prince voice.

The door opened, and the doorway filled up.

It was the one-eared orc from the banquet. He had to duck to come in, and then he straightened up, and kept straightening, and I had to tilt my head back to see his face. Up close, he was enormous, the way a refrigerator is enormous when you're trying to get it up a staircase. His armor was dark iron, dented in a dozen places. His face was a map of old scars. The missing ear had been gone so long the skin had healed smooth. His tusks were as long as my thumbs and had been filed blunt at the tips.

I'm going to be honest with you. He was the most frightening person I had ever seen up close, and I once broke up a fight between two eighth graders in a stairwell.

He looked down at me.

"Brakka," he said.

That was the whole introduction. After a moment, he added, in a voice like rocks being shifted around in a bucket, "The Concordance assigns an orc guard to the Prince. By treaty." Then he walked to the door, turned to face the hallway, and planted his feet like a man planning to stand there until the sun burned out.

The elves had sent me an orc. I wasn't sure yet if that was an honor or an insult. I suspected Brakka wasn't sure either.

Lunch arrived about an hour later, carried by three servants who were visibly struggling. Master Orrin had clearly not come down from the feast. There were two whole roast chickens. A wheel of yellow cheese the size of a hubcap. Four loaves of bread, a bowl of honeyed carrots, and a pie. On top of it all sat a small folded note in shaky handwriting: *For the Old Blood.*

It was enough food for six. I looked at it, and then I looked at Brakka's back in the doorway.

I thought about the boar on the cart, and the sound his stomach had made.

"Brakka," I said. "Have you eaten?"

He didn't turn around. "Guards eat at the end of the watch."

"When does your watch end?"

A pause. "Tomorrow."

"Come eat with me."

Now he turned around. He looked at me, and at the food, and at me again, and something moved across his scarred face that I couldn't read. "Orcs don't eat from elven tables, Highness."

"I'm not asking you to," I said. "I'm telling you about a custom."

I'd done it twice already. I was getting good at it.

"Under the First Line," I said, and let the tongue-binding put some gravel in it, "the Prince's sword-bearer ate from the Prince's own plate. A prince who won't share his bread with his guard doesn't deserve to be guarded."

Brakka stared at me for a long time. Long enough that I started to worry I'd insulted him in some way I didn't understand.

Then he looked at the delicate elven chair by the table, a thing of white wood and silver curlicues that would have turned into kindling under him, and he sat down on the floor instead, cross-legged, with a clank of iron.

I took the tray off the table and sat down on the floor across from him. It seemed only fair.

We ate.

Here's something I didn't expect: Brakka had beautiful manners. He tore his bread into neat, even pieces. He ate with small, careful bites. When he was finished with a chicken leg, he set the bone down on the edge of the tray, lined up with the others, and wiped his tusks with a napkin. It was the most polite anyone had eaten in front of me since I'd arrived, and I'd been dining with the aristocracy.

He didn't talk much, but he talked a little. He'd served in the Silverwood for thirty-one years. The elves, he said, called orcs "the Concordance's spear."

"A spear doesn't eat," he said, turning a piece of bread over in his fingers. "A spear gets put in a rack."

"Has an elf ever eaten with you before?"

He chewed for a while. "No."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I passed him the pie.

When the trays were empty, Brakka sat back, wiped his tusks one last time, and looked at me with an expression I'd later learn was the closest he came to a smile. He nodded slowly, as if he'd just worked something out.

"Feed the spear," he said, "and the spear stays sharp." He tapped the side of his head with one thick finger. "Strategy."

It hadn't been strategy. It had been lunch. But I found I didn't want to correct him.

"Sure," I said. "Strategy."

I was reaching for the last of the bread when I thought of something useful to ask, and asked it as casually as I could manage.

"Does it rain much here?"

"Not now," Brakka said. "Dry season." He picked a crumb off his breastplate. "The long rains come in a few weeks. Then it rains for two months." He made a face. "The elves love it. They dance in it."

I inhaled a piece of bread.

Brakka thumped me helpfully on the back, which nearly dislocated my shoulder and did, at least, get the bread out.

\* \* \*

The second knock of the day was much smaller than the first.

Brakka opened the door. Standing in the hallway was a young orc woman, small for an orc, which still made her about my height. She had gray-green skin, short tusks, and long braids threaded with dozens of charms: little bells, carved beads, a tiny bone fish. A leather satchel hung from her shoulder, stuffed so full of dried herbs that bits of them stuck out of the flap.

She saw me and dropped the satchel.

Herbs scattered across the doorway. She dropped to her knees, scooped them up with both hands, stuffed them back in the bag, stood, bowed, and dropped it again.

"I'm sorry," she said to the floor. "I'm so sorry. Highness. I'm Thessa. Of Clan Ashtusk. Apprentice to the shaman. The Concordance has, I've been... I'm your attendant, Highness." She took a breath. "Since you sent the elves away."

I could see it on her face: somebody had told her this assignment was a punishment. Whether it was a punishment for her or for me, I wasn't sure. The elves had refused to let me be bathed by anyone, so they'd given me an orc. They probably thought it was funny.

"It's nice to meet you, Thessa," I said.

That was when Brakka, trying to be welcoming, I think, gave a deep grunt and said, "*Ashtusk*," in a voice like a landslide.

Thessa jumped about a foot in the air, and her hands lit up.

It was a silver-white glow, like the summoning circle but brighter, gathering around her fingers so fast I barely saw it start. And every fire drill I'd ever run with a room full of seventh graders kicked in at once. I didn't think. I dove.

I hit the floor behind the heavy velvet curtain on the balcony door just as the light went off like a camera flash the size of a room. There was a sound like a deep breath. The air smelled like clean laundry and lightning.

Then silence.

I peeked out from behind the curtain.

A perfect circle about ten feet across, with Thessa at its center, had been scrubbed cleaner than anything I have ever seen. The floor inside it gleamed like a mirror. The lunch trays, which had been sticky with honey and gravy, were spotless; the chicken bones Brakka had lined up so neatly were polished white. Brakka himself was standing just inside the edge of the circle, and his dented iron armor now shone like it had been made that morning. Every scratch was gone.

So was his war paint. The dark red stripes that had run across his cheeks and over his brow had vanished completely.

Brakka slowly raised a hand to his bare face.

"Twelve years," he said quietly.

"Oh, no," Thessa whispered. "Oh, no, no, no."

She started apologizing and couldn't stop. It all came out in a rush: it was Purify, it was the only spell she'd ever been able to cast, she'd been trying to learn others for years, but this was the only one that worked, and it happened whenever she got scared. It stripped away dirt and rust and tarnish, and minor curses, and anything that didn't belong.

"Anything that doesn't belong," I repeated.

I thought about my ears, glued on with half a thimble of spirit gum. I thought about Gwendolyn. I thought about the glitter. I was, from head to toe, a walking list of things that didn't belong.

I looked at Thessa, who was clutching her satchel and blinking very fast, and I recognized her. Not her specifically. The type. Every class has one: the kid who's been yelled at so many times for the same thing that she flinches before you even open your mouth. Yelling at a kid like that doesn't fix anything. It just teaches her to flinch faster.

"Okay," I said. "Here's what we're going to do. We need a signal."

She looked up. "A signal, Highness?"

"If you feel one coming, you say a word. And when I hear the word, I get behind something." I thought about it. "Pineapple."

"What's a pineapple?"

"It's a fruit. It doesn't matter. It's a word nobody ever says by accident."

She tried it out carefully, like a word in a foreign language. "Pineapple."

"Perfect."

She stared at me. I don't think anyone had ever handed her a plan before. They'd only ever handed her blame. Her eyes got very bright, and she nodded hard enough to jingle every charm in her hair.

Behind her, Brakka was still touching the place where his war paint had been. He looked at Thessa, and then at me, and then he grunted.

"Strategy," he said.

Thessa turned to him, wringing her hands. "I know the Ashtusk patterns," she said in a small voice. "I could paint it back. If you want."

Brakka considered this for a long moment. Then he nodded once.

That was how I got my second ally. She cost one bodyguard twelve years of war paint, and I'd do it again.
