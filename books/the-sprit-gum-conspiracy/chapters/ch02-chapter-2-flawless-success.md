# Chapter 2: Flawless Success

The ears survived the landing. I want that on the record before anything else.

The rest of me was less lucky. I came out of the light about four feet above a stone floor, and the floor did not care that I'd been having a nice day. I hit it shoulder first, rolled once in a tangle of bedsheet, and ended up on my back, staring at a domed ceiling five stories up, painted with constellations I didn't recognize.

Glitter drifted down over me like the end of a very sad parade.

I took inventory. Shoulder: hurt. Foam sword: under me, bent into a gentle question mark. Cape: wrapped around both legs. Gwendolyn: sideways. My hands went to my ears before my brain told them to. Both still there. I let out a breath that was mostly relief and partly glitter.

Then I noticed the people.

They stood in a ring around me, at the edge of a circle carved into the floor. Its grooves were still smoking with a thin silver light, and the air smelled like hot metal and pine. There were about a dozen of them, and every one was tall. Not basketball tall. Tall like someone had taken a normal person and gently pulled. They wore long silver robes and had hair down to their waists, and their skin was so pale it looked lit from the inside. Every face in that room belonged on a coin.

And they had ears. Long, tapered, real ears, which moved slightly as they looked at me. One of them twitched.

Mine didn't do that. Mine were $8.99.

Elves. Actual elves.

I have spent a large part of my life imagining meeting elves. In none of those daydreams had they looked at me the way these did, like I was a casserole someone had brought to a party they hadn't been invited to.

Nobody spoke. There's a specific silence that happens when twelve very tall people realize they've made a mistake. I've heard it at parent-teacher conferences.

Then, from somewhere behind the tallest of them, a voice like a gravel truck shouted, "*BEHOLD!*"

\* \* \*

A dwarf shoved his way between two pairs of elven knees.

He was about four feet tall and nearly as wide, with a nose like a ripe strawberry and a beard braided with brass rings and, I'm fairly sure, breadcrumbs. His ceremonial robe was purple with gold trim and had a wine stain down the front shaped like Ohio. In one hand he held a staff topped with a large, visibly cracked crystal. In the other he held a flask, which seemed to be getting more use.

"*Behold!*" he said again, throwing his arms wide. He wasn't saying it to me. He was saying it to the elves. "As promised! As contracted! The Lost Prince returns from beyond the Veil, summoned by the hand of High Ritualist Durgan Pell, Seventh Circle, Guild of Arcane Conveyance, bonded and insured!"

It took me a second to realize I understood him.

He wasn't speaking English. The words were the wrong shape, all long vowels and consonants in places consonants shouldn't go. But the meaning arrived in my head as clearly as if he'd said it in Raleigh, like subtitles only I could see.

"Where am I?" I said, and heard it come out of my own mouth in the same flowing, unfamiliar language. It sounded much more dignified than I meant it to.

The dwarf waved his flask at me without looking. "Tongue-binding, Highness. Comes with the standard package. You speak Common now, no need to thank me." He paused. "Well. There'll be a form."

Then he turned back to the elves, reached into his robe, and pulled out a scroll. He let it unroll. It kept unrolling. It hit the floor and rolled another six feet toward me before it stopped.

"Now," said Durgan Pell. "Regarding the completion bonus."

Not one of the elves looked at the contract. They were all still looking at me.

Well, almost all of them. At the back of the room, one elf stood a little apart from the rest. He was taller still, which I wouldn't have thought possible, with silver hair, a thin circlet on his brow, and the calm, faintly bored expression of a man watching a chess game he'd already won.

He was the only person in the room who didn't look surprised.

I noticed that, and I filed it away. You learn things in a middle school. One of them is that when the fire alarm goes off, the kid who doesn't look surprised is the one who pulled it.

I decided I should stand up. It took three tries, because of the cape.

"Majestic," Durgan said, approvingly, to no one.

The whispering started at the far side of the circle and worked its way around.

"He's very... broad."

"He's *sparkling*."

"Is that a sheet?"

"The tapestries show the Prince as slender as a birch," said an elf lady with a voice like a tuning fork. "This is not a birch."

I'll be honest: I've been called worse than "not a birch." I've been called worse by seventh graders before first period. So I stood there and let it wash over me, and kept my face royal.

Then one of them stepped into the circle.

He had a narrow face, a narrower nose, and the posture of a man who'd been personally disappointed by the entire universe. Later, I'd learn his name was Lord Venrick. At the time, I just thought of him as Pinched.

"This is no prince," Pinched announced. "Pell has dragged a troll out of some swamp and put a wig on it."

I was more offended on Gwendolyn's behalf than my own.

"A troll?" Durgan was outraged. "Trolls don't *sparkle*."

"The contract specifies the Lost Prince." Pinched turned to the others, raising his voice. "This is plainly a failure. And the law of summoning is clear. A failed summoning must be unmade. Return it to the Void."

"Now, now," Durgan said, stepping between Pinched and me with his flask raised. "If my lords will consult section nine of the contract, the Guild's warranty is very clear. A subject may only be unmade within thirty days of delivery, with the original receipt." He paused. "And the completion bonus is non-refundable."

For a second, I'd thought he was defending me. He was defending his bonus. I was being discussed like a blender.

A murmur went around the circle. It wasn't a murmur of protest. It was the murmur of people agreeing that, sadly, rules were rules.

I didn't know what the Void was. I didn't need to. Nobody says "unmade" about something they're planning to send home with a gift bag.

So I did the only thing I'm actually good at under pressure, which is think like a video game.

Rule one of every RPG: when you wake up somewhere strange, you do not tell the NPCs you have no idea what's going on. You stay in character. You talk to everyone. You learn the rules before you break them.

And there's a rule from teaching, too. In every room, there's one person everyone else glances at before they react. Find that person, and talk to them, not the loudest kid in class.

When Pinched said "unmade," every head in that circle had flicked, just for a moment, toward the silver-haired elf at the back.

I walked straight past Pinched. He flinched, possibly from the glitter. I stopped in front of the silver-haired elf, looked up, and up, and dropped my voice into the register I'd practiced all summer in the bathroom mirror. Aelindor's voice. The one he uses when he comes home at the end of the second game.

"I have crossed a very long way," I said. "I expected a warmer welcome in my own house."

The tongue-binding turned it into something even grander, all rolling vowels. It sounded incredible. I'd like to take more credit for it than I can.

The silver-haired elf looked down at me for a long, long moment. He didn't answer. He didn't need to. The whole room was watching him decide.

Durgan, sensing that the bonus was in play, scurried up beside me and jabbed his flask at my chest.

"His name!" he cried. "Look, my lords, it's written upon his very heart!"

Twelve elegant heads leaned toward the convention sticker over my heart. Twelve elegant faces squinted at HELLO MY NAME IS. Nobody could read it. That was obvious. They looked at it the way I look at a calculus textbook.

"What script is this?" the tuning-fork lady breathed.

"It says Aelindor," I said.

Which was true.

A gasp went around the circle. Pinched sneered. "Anyone can scratch a name."

That's when the silver-haired elf spoke for the first time. His voice was soft and pleasant, and every person in the room went still the moment they heard it.

"Send for Ferreth."

\* \* \*

We waited.

I stood in the middle of the smoking circle, sweating under Gwendolyn and trying to look like a prince who was used to waiting for things. Pinched glared at me. The tuning-fork lady glared at Durgan. Durgan took a long pull from his flask and sidled closer.

"Don't you worry, Highness," he murmured. "I've never had a summoning returned." He thought about it. "Not this year."

It was the least comforting thing anyone had ever said to me, and I once had a dentist say "oops."

After about ten minutes, the doors at the far end of the chamber creaked open, and a cart rolled in, pushed by two young elves in gray. On the cart was a book the size of a carry-on suitcase. Behind the cart, shuffling, came the oldest person I have ever seen.

He was an elf, technically, but he'd been one for so long that he'd started to look like a piece of furniture that had been left in an attic. His robes had dust in the folds. His spectacles were as thick as the bottoms of jam jars. He moved at the speed of a glacier with somewhere to be.

"A question of *lineage*!" he wheezed, delighted. "No one has asked me a question of lineage in eighty years!"

"Master Ferreth," said the silver-haired elf. "The Codex of the Old Kings. The First Line. What does it say of their appearance?"

"Oh, the First Line! Yes, yes." The old archivist flung the book open, and a cloud of dust rolled across the chamber. Three elves coughed delicately. He ran a crooked finger down the page, his nose nearly touching it, and read aloud.

"'And the Kings of the First Line were mighty of stature, and great of appetite, and radiant, and the forests rejoiced at their passing.'"

I went cold all over.

I knew that line. I'd quoted that line to Trent in the check-in line, two hours earlier and one universe away. It's in *Elderlight III*, in a book on the second floor of the Silverwood royal library, word for word. I'd read it eleven times. I could have recited it along with him. There's a leather-bound replica at the booth for three hundred dollars. This one had actual dust on it.

How does a video game know what's in this book?

I didn't have time to think about it, because the archivist was shuffling toward me. He stopped six inches away and peered up through those enormous spectacles until his whole eye filled the lens. Glitter got on his nose. He didn't seem to mind.

He poked my upper arm with one bony finger. "Mighty of stature," he announced. "Indisputably."

Then he squinted at my face. "Great of appetite?"

"I've had nothing but a cold brew since breakfast," I said, with total honesty. "I could eat that book."

A ripple went around the room. One of the elves actually took a step back.

The archivist reached up and touched my cheek with one fingertip. He held it up to the light. It glittered.

"And *radiant*," he whispered. Then, louder, with eighty years of pent-up enthusiasm: "Radiant beyond all doubt!"

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then the tuning-fork lady sank to one knee. Then the elf beside her. Then the next, and the next, a ring of silver robes folding to the floor like dominoes. Durgan dropped with a groan and a loud clank of flask. Pinched was second to last, and he did it with his jaw clenched so hard I could see the muscle jump.

The silver-haired elf was last.

He knelt slowly and gracefully, the way water settles, and he smiled at me. It was a lovely smile. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Welcome home, Highness," said High Lord Caelith Vaunt.

Of everything in that enormous book, he'd asked about one passage. The one I'd pass.

Every person in that room was kneeling to me. I graduated high school with a 2.7 GPA.

\* \* \*

Bells started ringing somewhere outside. Then more bells, farther off, and then what sounded like a whole city's worth of them, echoing across a place I still hadn't seen.

Four guards in silver armor escorted me out of the summoning tower and down a long corridor of pale stone. Up close, the walls didn't look built so much as grown: tree roots wove through the masonry like veins, and lights floated in little glass bulbs along the ceiling with nothing holding them up. I tried to look like I'd seen it all before. I've never wanted to take a picture of something so badly in my life.

My chambers were bigger than my apartment building. There was a sitting room, a dressing room, a balcony with its curtains drawn, and a bed roughly the size of a parking space. There was also a bathtub carved from a single piece of crystal, deep enough to drown a horse in, already steaming.

I looked at that bathtub the way you'd look at a loaded gun.

The guards bowed and left. The doors closed. And for the first time since a floor opened up under me in the Sovereign Crystal Grand Ballroom, I was alone.

I sat down on the edge of the bed. It took my weight with an expensive sort of sigh.

Inventory.

Left ear: lifting at the edge again, damp with sweat. I pressed it down and counted to thirty. Right ear: holding, for now. Spirit gum: I took the travel bottle out of my belt pouch and held it up to the floating light. About half a thimble left. Maybe two more applications, if I was careful and nobody threw water at me.

Phone: 38%. No service.

"Of course," I said to the phone. I turned it off. It felt like turning off a lifeline, and it was.

Then I sat there, and for a little while I didn't think about the elves at all. I thought about Dev, and whether anyone had picked his robot head up off the ballroom floor. I thought about rent, due on the first, and the five hundred dollars I'd never know if I'd won. I thought about my fish, Mr. Pickles, who has never once in his life been fed on time. I thought about Mrs. Okafor, and my third period on Monday, and Jaylen Price with his Eraser Monitor lanyard, wondering where his teacher went. I thought about Lily, waving at me with both hands, thinking it was the best part of the show.

I let myself have about five minutes of that. Then I wiped my face very carefully, because of the glitter, and looked around the room for something else to think about.

The tapestry on the wall opposite the bed was the length of a school bus. It showed a battle in a forest: elves in silver armor, tall and shining, driving a ragged horde out of the trees. The forest behind the horde was on fire. The horde carried axes and torches, and their stitched faces were twisted and desperate, and every one of them had been given stubby little round ears.

Weird artistic choice, I thought. Maybe the artist just wasn't good at ears. Honestly, I could relate.

I lay back on a bed the size of a parking space, held my left ear in place, and looked up at the round-eared monsters on the wall until I fell asleep.

I'd find out at dinner the next night what they were. Looking back, I should have figured it out sooner. I teach history. Primary sources are kind of my thing.
