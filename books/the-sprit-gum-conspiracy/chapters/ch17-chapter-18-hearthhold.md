# Chapter 18: Hearthhold

For three months, I'd been terrified of getting caught as a human. I got caught as an elf.

But first, we had to find them.

Thessa and I rode north for a day and a half, and the farther we went, the emptier the world got. The rolling gray hills flattened into a high white plain under a high white sky, and the wind came across it with nothing to stop it, so steady and so cold that after a while I stopped feeling my face. There were no trees. There were no animals that I could see. There were no roads, no fences, no smoke. Just snow, and rock, and more snow, all the way to the edge of the world.

We found Kell Tarn on the afternoon of the first day: a long frozen lake in a shallow bowl of rock, the place where the patrol had first seen a human that spring. It was very beautiful and completely empty. We rode all the way around it. We found nothing at all.

That night, we camped in the lee of a boulder with a fire made from Thessa's satchel of dried moss, and I sat with my knees pulled up to my chin and thought.

I'd been doing it wrong. I'd been looking for people, the way you'd look for your keys: walking around and hoping to spot them. But you can't find people who've hidden from an entire empire for three hundred years by looking for them. They're too good at it. If they weren't, they'd be dead.

So I tried thinking like a historian instead. Like a kid with an index card taped to his desk.

Forget *where are they*. Ask *what would they need?*

Water, first. Water that doesn't freeze solid all winter, which up here meant a spring, or a river fast enough to keep moving under the ice. Shelter from the wind, which came from the north, which meant a valley, deep enough to sit below it. Sun, as much as they could get, which meant that valley would face south. Wood, for fires and building, which meant trees, which meant soil, which meant somewhere that wasn't this. And somewhere hidden. Not just from the ground, but from the sky: the owl-folk scouts flew over these wastes every year. Somewhere you couldn't see from above unless you were right on top of it.

I said all of that to Thessa, out loud, by the fire. She listened, and nodded slowly, and then said something I hadn't thought of.

"And they'd leave marks," she said. "For each other. So they don't get lost in the snow. Hunters always do."

The next morning, we stopped riding around looking for people. We started reading the land instead, the way you'd read a document. Where did the snow lie thinner? Where did the wind seem to bend? Which way did the land fall away? Thessa watched the ground, and the little clumps of tough gray grass that poked up through the drifts, and told me which way water ran underneath. I watched the shape of the hills.

And a little before noon on the second day, on a low rise above a fold in the land that you couldn't see until you were almost standing on it, we found a cairn.

It was a little pile of stones, about knee-high. That was all. There were lots of stones up there. But these were stacked too neatly. Flat ones on the bottom, round ones on top, a single pointed stone at the very peak, pointing south and down, into the fold.

Nature doesn't stack rocks like that. People do.

\* \* \*

We followed the cairn down into the fold, and the fold became a ravine, and the ravine bent and dropped and bent again, sheltered on both sides by walls of dark rock that broke the wind so completely that, for the first time in two days, I could hear myself breathe. There was another cairn at the first bend, pointing on. Then another.

And then the ravine opened out, and there were trees.

I hadn't seen a tree in four days. These were pines: dark green, straight, and old, filling the bottom of a long valley that ran south and down, out of the wind and toward the sun. Snow lay on their branches. Under them, the ground was soft with needles. It smelled like Christmas. I didn't realize how much I'd missed the smell of trees until I was in the middle of one.

Pudding walked in among them as if she'd come home. Thessa's pony followed.

And then, about fifty yards into the forest, Thessa reined in her pony, and stopped dead, and sat there, staring at the ground.

I rode up beside her and looked where she was looking.

It was a stump. Just a pine stump, sawn off clean and level, about knee-high, old enough that its top had gone gray. And driven into the ground beside it was a wooden stake, about as long as my forearm, with a set of notches cut neatly into one side, like a tally.

And beside the stake, growing up out of the needles, was a young pine tree. About as tall as me. Straight, and healthy, and green.

I looked around.

There was another stump, ten yards on, with a stake and a sapling beside it. And another past that. And another. Once I knew what to look for, they were everywhere: all through the forest, every place a tree had ever been cut, a notched stake to mark it, and a young tree growing in its place. Some of the young trees were knee-high. Some were taller than the stakes. Some, I realized, looking up, were nearly as tall as the old pines, and the stumps beside them had almost rotted away into the forest floor.

Somebody had been cutting trees in this forest for a very long time. And every single time they'd cut one, they'd planted another.

Thessa slid down off her pony, and walked to the nearest stake, and knelt down beside it in the snow and needles. She reached out and touched the notches, very gently, with one finger, the way you'd touch a word carved in stone.

"The notches are the year," she said quietly. "When it was cut. When it was planted." She looked up at the sapling, and then at the forest all around us, and her voice caught. "Someone here takes only what the tree can spare."

It was what she'd said to me in the weepwood grove, on my first week in the Silverwood, when I'd tried to scrape the resin off with a butter knife. *You don't take more than it can spare.* And then she'd watched the elves of the Silverwood hack that same grove bare, to glue on their ears.

I didn't say anything. I let her kneel there as long as she needed.

When she finally stood up, I looked past her, through the trees, down the valley to where the forest thinned out. And I saw something that made me grab Pudding's reins so hard she snorted.

The hillsides on the far side of the valley weren't hillsides. They were steps. Long, level terraces, cut into the slope one above another like a giant staircase, each one held up by a wall of fitted gray stone, each one covered in snow, with the rows of what had been fields showing faintly underneath. And set into the south-facing slope below them, catching the low winter sun, there was a long, low line of something that flashed.

Glass. Windows. Long, low buildings, half dug into the hillside, with glass roofs, and behind the glass, even from that distance, a faint green.

Greenhouses.

I'd been in Verathis for three months. I'd been in the greatest palace in the world, among the oldest and most refined people who had ever lived. And the most advanced piece of technology I'd seen in all that time was the wine-bottle barometer strapped behind my saddle.

The vermin had greenhouses.

\* \* \*

I was so busy staring at the greenhouses that I didn't hear the rope.

There was a sharp *twang* somewhere above us, and a rushing sound, and then the sky came down. It was a net: a big, heavy, knotted thing of tarred rope, weighted at the edges with stones, which had been strung up between two pines and hidden in the branches, and which dropped over Pudding and the pony and Thessa and me all at once. Pudding, to her eternal credit, didn't bolt. She just stood there under the net looking deeply put-upon. The pony tried to bolt and got about three feet. Thessa yelped. I got a mouthful of tarred rope and fell off Pudding sideways into the snow.

And out of the trees, from every side, came the people of Hearthhold.

There were about a dozen of them. Young, most of them, my age or younger. They wore thick coats of felted wool and fur, patched and repatched, and heavy boots, and they were armed with whatever they'd had to hand: slings, a couple of long hunting knives, three pitchforks, a wood-axe, and a single bow, carried by the girl at their front. She was maybe sixteen, sharp-faced and freckled, with a long brown braid and the bow drawn to her cheek and the arrow pointed straight at my face. Her hands were shaking. She held it steady anyway.

And every one of them was looking at me with an expression I'd never seen on anyone's face before. Not even Venrick's. It was hate, and it was terror, both at once, so strong it made the air feel thin.

"Leaf-Lord," one of the boys said. He spat in the snow.

I realized, lying there on my back in the snow with a net over me, what they were seeing. A tall figure in a cape, with long, pointed ears, and a fall of silver-blond hair, and glitter on his face, catching the light like frost. The Lost Prince of the First Line, in all his radiance. The thing that their great-great-great-grandparents had run from, through the burning trees.

Then another boy looked at Thessa. At her gray-green skin, and her tusks, and her braids full of charms.

"And a Spear," he said.

Thessa flinched. I saw it. Spear: the word the elves used for orcs, the thing that gets pointed and thrown and put back in the rack. She'd come four hundred miles to save these people. They'd called her the same name.

So that was how it happened.

"Wait," I said, trying to sit up under the net. "Please. Just listen. I'm not here to hurt anyone. I came to—"

Somebody laughed. It wasn't a nice laugh. It was the bitter, tired kind, the kind you'd laugh if a wolf knocked on your door and asked to borrow sugar.

"Sure you didn't," said the boy with the axe.

They hauled us out from under the net. They tied my hands in front of me with a length of rough cord, tight, and then Thessa's. Two of them took Pudding and the pony by their bridles. Another one found the bundle strapped behind my saddle, and unwrapped it, and held up the barometer, with its wine bottle and its bowl and its little scratched reed.

He stared at it. "What's this?"

"It's—" I began.

"Don't touch it," the girl with the bow said sharply. "Could be poison. Some Leaf-Lord trick." And the boy with the barometer set it down in the snow very, very carefully, and backed away from it, and then picked it up again by the very end of its blanket at arm's length, the way you'd carry a dead rat.

They marched us down the valley toward the village. Somewhere along the way, one of the guards, a broad young man with a pitchfork, jabbed it toward Thessa and called her a Spear again. The third time.

Thessa, who was walking with her chin up and her bound hands held in front of her, turned to him and said, politely, "I'm an apprentice shaman, actually."

The guard considered this for a moment, frowning.

"A Spear shaman," he said.

Thessa sighed. It was a very deep sigh. Then she leaned toward me, as we walked, and said very quietly out of the corner of her mouth:

"Highness. This would be a good time for one of your plans."

"I'm working on it," I said.

\* \* \*

The village of Hearthhold sat at the bottom of the valley, beside a long frozen lake, and by the time they marched us into it, everyone in it had come out to look.

They came out of their low stone houses with the turf roofs and stood along the lane in the snow, wrapped in their felted coats and their shawls: old people and young people, mothers holding children on their hips, a blacksmith with his hammer still in his hand, a woman with flour on her apron. They didn't say anything. They just watched us go by, with the same look I'd seen on the faces of the young people in the forest. Hate, and terror, and something underneath them both that was older than either.

And I looked back at them.

I hadn't expected it to hit me the way it did. I'd known, intellectually, that they'd be human. That was the whole point. But I'd spent three months in a world of elves and orcs and dwarves and wolf-folk, three months of silver hair and gray-green skin and tusks and fur and long, tapering, pointed ears, and I'd stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing the noise of a refrigerator. And now, walking down that lane with my hands tied, I was surrounded by people.

Round ears. Freckles. Gray hair and brown hair and red hair, and bald heads, and crooked noses, and bad teeth, and laugh lines. A heavy-set man with glasses, actual glasses, wire-rimmed and patched with thread at the hinge, who looked so much like my high school chemistry teacher, Mr. Hadley, that I nearly said his name out loud. A skinny boy of about twelve with a cowlick, standing with his arms folded and his chin stuck out, who could have been Jaylen Price's cousin.

They looked like the people at the bus stop on Hillsborough Street. They looked like the parents at back-to-school night. They looked like home.

I started to cry.

I didn't mean to. It just happened, all at once, the way a sneeze happens. My eyes filled up and spilled over, and the tears ran down through the glitter on my face in long, sparkling streaks, and I couldn't wipe them away because my hands were tied. And all around me, the people of Hearthhold saw the Leaf-Lord weeping, and decided, I could tell, that it was fear.

It wasn't. But I couldn't have explained if I'd tried.

They stopped us in the middle of the village, in front of a big stone building with a heavy wooden door. And there, before they did anything else, they tested me.

It was the girl with the bow who did it. Her name was Wenna, I'd learn later. She went into one of the houses and came back out with a heel of dark bread, and held it out to me at arm's length, the way you'd offer a treat to a dog you weren't sure about. The whole village leaned in to watch.

I didn't understand at first. Then I heard an old woman in the crowd murmur it to the child on her hip, the way you'd teach a child its letters: *Leaf-Lords can't eat bread. It burns them.*

I looked at the bread. I'd been living on dried apples and a handful of oats for two days.

I took it in both my tied hands and ate it in four bites.

It was the best bread I'd ever had. It was dense and dark and a little sour, with seeds in it, and I swallowed the last bite, and licked my thumb, and looked up at Wenna, and asked, before I could stop myself, "Is there any butter?"

The village of Hearthhold stared at me in total horror.

Then a ripple went through the crowd, a frightened murmur, and I heard what they were saying. Not *he ate the bread, so he's not a Leaf-Lord*. Oh, no. What they'd concluded, every one of them, was that I must be a very, very powerful Leaf-Lord, if I could eat bread and ask for butter.

Somebody knelt down and pulled off one of my boots and checked the bottom of my foot. I didn't understand that one either, until I heard the whisper. *Roots.* Leaf-Lords had roots.

I didn't have roots. They looked even more worried.

It was the exact same thing, I realized, standing there with one boot off in the snow. Exactly what Silvaine did with her crystal. When it glowed, the Prince was overloading it. When it blazed, the Blight was in the luggage. Whatever the evidence said, it always ended up meaning what you already believed.

While all that was going on, an old man had picked up the barometer. Not by the blanket, this time. He'd taken it gently in both hands, the bottle and the bowl and the reed, and was turning it to the light, peering at the scratches on the reed through narrowed eyes. He was ancient and stooped and wrapped in about four scarves, and he'd come down off the ridge that morning, I'd learn, after a long night on watch. His name was Tobiah.

"That's no poison," he said slowly. "That's a weatherglass." He looked up, at me, with a strange expression. "My grandfather made one just like it."

The crowd went quiet. The people of Hearthhold, it turned out, had kept their science. Three hundred years at the top of the world, hiding from an empire, and they'd remembered how to read air pressure from a bottle of water.

And they all turned and looked at me, and I watched the suspicion on their faces get worse, not better. Because now they had a new question. What was a Leaf-Lord doing with a human instrument?

I didn't have an answer that I could say out loud.

There were other moments, too, in that long, cold walk through the village. A small boy, maybe seven, broke away from his mother, ran right up to me, kicked me as hard as he could in the shin, and shouted, "That's for my great-great-grandpa!" before his mother caught him by the collar and hauled him away. On the doorpost of the house where we'd stopped, I saw a carving, old and worn smooth: tall figures with long, pointed ears and cruel faces, driving a crowd of small, round-eared people out of a burning forest. It was the tapestry over my bed in the Silverwood. The exact same picture. Only this time, the people with the pointed ears were the monsters.

Weird artistic choice, I thought. It didn't feel funny this time.

And over the heavy wooden door of the stone building, the village meeting hall, carved deep into the lintel, there were seven words. Thessa saw them first. She read them aloud, very softly, as they pushed us through the door.

*Take only what you can give back.*

The last thing I saw before the door closed behind us was Pudding.

The village children had found her. Nobody in Hearthhold had seen a horse like her in generations; they had a few shaggy hill ponies, but nothing like a tall white mare. And in the time it had taken me to eat a heel of bread and lose a boot, a dozen children had surrounded her, and were braiding her mane with bits of colored yarn, and feeding her winter carrots from their mittens, and leading her proudly off toward a warm shed full of straw. She didn't look back.

Pudding was the first member of our party to be accepted in Hearthhold. It took her about forty minutes.

\* \* \*

The meeting hall was one long, low room, built of stone, with thick timber beams overhead and a big hearth at one end where a peat fire was smoldering. There were benches along the walls and a long table down the middle, worn smooth by three hundred years of elbows. They sat us on a bench by the cold end of the room, as far from the fire as possible, with our hands still tied. Two guards stood by the door with pitchforks: the broad young man who'd named Thessa a Spear shaman, and a gray-haired woman with a face like a closed fist.

We waited for a long time.

Then the door opened, and a small, white-haired woman came in out of the snow, stamping her boots, and closed the door behind her. The guards both straightened up when they saw her.

She wasn't tall. She wasn't armed. She was old, seventy or more, small and straight-backed, wrapped in a long coat of gray felted wool, with her white hair pulled back in a plain knot and her hands red and rough from a lifetime of cold and soil. But she walked across that hall as if it belonged to her, and I suppose it did.

She stopped in front of our bench and looked at us. She didn't say anything for a long while. She just looked: at me, at the cape and the glitter and the wig and the long, crooked, pointed ears; at Thessa, at her tusks, her charms, her tied hands. She looked the way you'd look at weather coming in, calmly, measuring how bad it was going to be.

Then she pulled up a stool, and sat down on it, facing us.

"My name is Ruth Ashgrove," she said. "I'm the eldest of the elders here." Her voice was dry and quiet and completely steady. "And you are an elf lord in a bedsheet, and a Spear."

I opened my mouth. Then I closed it. That was, honestly, a pretty fair summary.

"There's an army," I said. "Coming north. About four thousand. Elves, orcs, dwarves, beastfolk. They're two days behind me. Maybe less."

Ruth Ashgrove didn't even blink. "We saw their fires," she said. "Two nights ago. From the ridge."

"They know you're here," I said. "Their scouts found you weeks ago. They're coming to—" I stopped. There wasn't a gentle way to say it. "They're coming to kill all of you. And take the valley."

"Yes," said Ruth. "I imagine they are." She folded her rough hands in her lap. "So tell me, Leaf-Lord. Why would one of them ride two days ahead of his army, with a single Spear for company, and walk right into our forest?" Her eyes didn't leave my face. "To count us? To see where our walls are weakest? To pick out the best valley for himself before the others arrive?"

"To warn you," I said.

She looked at me for a long, long moment.

Then she smiled. It wasn't unkind. It was tired, and very old, and a little sad, the smile of someone who's been told a lot of lies in her life and has stopped being angry about them.

"No," she said gently. "I don't think so."

She stood up. She put the stool back where she'd found it, against the wall, neatly.

"We'll decide what to do with you at first light," she said, and went out, and the door closed behind her, and the guards settled back against it with their pitchforks.

Thessa and I sat there in the cold end of the meeting hall for a long time without saying anything. The peat fire glowed at the far end of the room. Outside, the wind had picked up again. Somewhere in the village, a dog barked, and somebody shushed it.

I looked down at my hands, tied together in my lap. At the glitter still on my knuckles. At the edge of my bedsheet cape, and my green tunic with its silver vines, which I'd embroidered on a couch in Raleigh while Netflix asked if I was still watching.

I'd spent three months building the best disguise in the history of the Silverwood. It had gotten me through a summoning chamber, a banquet, a Blessing of Dew, an archery trial, a barrel, a crystal, a spring, and a search of my own luggage. It had fooled the High Lords of the Silverwood, and the seers, and the Inquisition. It was the most convincing thing I'd ever made.

And here, it was the one thing standing between me and the only people in this world who were like me.

Words weren't going to work. I could see that now. I could have talked to Ruth Ashgrove all night, and every word I said would have sounded exactly like a Leaf-Lord's lie, because that's what she'd been raised to hear. Just like the elves had been raised to hear it from humans. You can't argue someone out of something their whole world has taught them. You can only show them something they can't explain away.

I knew what I had to show them. I'd known since the moment I saw their faces in the lane.

I looked over at Thessa. She was already looking at me. She knew too.

"In the morning," I said quietly.

She nodded.

I'd spent three months surrounded by people who would kill me if they knew what I was. Now I was surrounded by people who would kill me because they didn't.
