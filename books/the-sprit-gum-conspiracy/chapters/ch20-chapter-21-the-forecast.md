# Chapter 21: The Forecast

For three months, rain had been the most dangerous thing in my life. On the morning of the forecast, I decided to invite it.

But first I had to do the math.

The water in the weatherglass had climbed all night. I'd checked it four times by lamplight, lying on my side in the snow beside it, and every time it was a little higher. By the time the sky went gray over the eastern rim of the ravine, it had risen nearly two full marks above where it had been at sunset. It was still rising. Slowly, but steadily, like a tide coming in.

I sat up in my blanket, with my breath steaming in the cold, and took out my lesson plan.

Not the front of it. The back. Because in the back of my lesson plan, on the last three sheets of royal parchment, there was something I'd never told anyone about, not even Thessa. A table.

I'd started it the night I built the first barometer, back in my chambers in the Silverwood, in my first week. It's what my seventh graders had done for the weather unit: every day, at the same time, you write down the reading, and what the sky is doing, and what happens next. A data table. It's the most boring thing in science, and it's the most important. And I'd kept it up, every single day, for three months. Through the procession, and the long rains, and the march north. Every reading. Every storm. Every scratch on the reed, written down in pencil next to a day number, because I didn't know the names of the months here.

A few rows, so you get the idea:

> *Day 12: Two marks up overnight. Storm by noon. Procession canceled. Feyhallow wrong.*
>
> *Day 33: No reading. Brakka broke it.*
>
> *Day 84: Clear. Elves visibly disappointed.*

I'd done it because Mrs. Okafor had made me do it with my students, and once you've made a class of twelve-year-olds keep a weather log, you feel like kind of a hypocrite if you don't keep one yourself.

I'd never thought it would matter this much.

I ran my finger down the columns. The storm on the morning of the procession: the water had jumped two marks overnight, fast, and the rain had come about fourteen hours after it started rising. A short, hard, sudden storm. The start of the long rains: no reading, because Brakka had just knocked the bottle off my balcony. But the end of the long rains had given me good numbers. A slow, steady fall over three days, and then clear skies, right on the second new moon.

And now this. Two marks over a night, like the procession storm. But slower. Steadier. Still climbing at dawn, with no sign of stopping.

A slow rise means a big system. Mrs. Okafor had drawn that on the whiteboard for my class, with a cartoon cloud the size of a car. The bigger the storm, the longer it takes to arrive, and the longer it stays.

I worked it out on the back of the parchment with a stub of pencil. I did it twice, because I didn't like the answer.

A big storm. A long one. Arriving at about noon tomorrow.

I sat there in the snow with the pencil in my hand and felt the cold go all the way through me, in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

The trial was going to happen in this valley. There was nowhere else for it to happen. In the open, or under some kind of tent, in front of the assembled Houses of the Silverwood and the whole army of the Concordance and every man, woman, and child in Hearthhold. Sometime in the next few days. And it was going to rain. Hard. For a long time. And spirit gum fails in water. And homemade spirit gum, made of weepwood resin and dwarven firewater, fails even faster.

My ears were going to come off. In front of everyone. There was nothing I could do to stop it.

I sat with that for a long time. Long enough that Thessa woke up, in her blanket beside me, and looked at my face, and then at the weatherglass, and then at the table on the back of my lesson plan, and sat up very slowly.

"How long?" she asked.

"Noon tomorrow," I said.

She didn't say anything. She just looked at me. She knew what it meant as well as I did.

And then something happened in my head that I've thought about a lot since. I'd been afraid of the rain for three months. I'd built a whole life around being afraid of it: barometers, umbrellas, vows, barrels, rashes. Every single day in Verathis, I'd planned my life around keeping the water off my ears. And sitting there in the snow at the bottom of that ravine, looking at my own handwriting, I realized that I was tired. I was so tired of being afraid of the rain.

You can't stop a storm. I'd always known that. But I'd forgotten the other half. The half that every weather forecaster on Earth knows.

If you can't stop a storm, you can at least schedule it.

I looked at Thessa. And I watched her see it happen on my face before I'd said a word.

"You *want* it to happen," she said slowly.

"No," I said. "I want to choose when."

I turned to the front of my lesson plan, past *Objective 1*, and *Objective 2: Stop the war*, and *Do something*. And underneath them, in pencil, I wrote one more line.

*Objective 3: Choose the hour.*

\* \* \*

The host of the Concordance arrived in the late afternoon.

We heard them long before we saw them. First as a low murmur from the south, like distant traffic. Then as a rumble, coming up the ravine. Then, all at once, they were pouring around the last bend and out into the valley below Hearthhold's wall: four thousand people, and their wagons, and their horses, and their banners, spreading out across the snow until the whole floor of the valley was a sea of color and noise. The silver companies of the High Houses. The dark iron columns of the orcs. The dwarves with their wagons and their payment schedules. Wolf-folk loping at the edges. Owl-folk wheeling overhead. Up on the wall, I saw the people of Hearthhold go absolutely still. They'd never seen so many people in one place in their lives. Neither had their grandparents.

I stood at my little camp in the middle of the open ground and watched them come, and looked for one face in particular.

I didn't have to look long.

There was a disturbance in the orc column. A shout. Some jostling. And then Brakka came out of the ranks at a dead run, in full armor, across the snow toward me, and I felt the ground shake under my feet. I felt it in my boots. Soldiers scattered out of his way. A dwarf dove into a snowbank. He came across that open ground like a landslide that had remembered something important, and I braced myself, and he stopped, about three feet in front of me, with snow flying up around his boots.

He stood there, breathing hard. He looked at me. At my face, and my crooked left ear, and the glitter, and the cape. At Thessa, beside me. At Pudding, with her braided mane.

And then he didn't do anything at all. He just stood very still, the way he'd stood outside my door every night for three months, with his arms at his sides.

"You came back," he said.

"I came back," I said.

He nodded, once. That was all. But I saw his hands. They were shaking.

The next one to reach me was Durgan.

He came stumping across the snow from the supply train with his ledger under his arm and a pen behind his ear, and he didn't say hello. He just opened the ledger, tore out a page, and held it out to me.

"Invoice," he said. "Three days' travel at speed. On a wagon. With the goat." He paused. "The goat was extra."

I took it. I didn't even look at it. I was grinning too hard.

"Also," said Durgan darkly, "the Inquisitor never paid my two crowns."

The goat itself rode into the valley about ten minutes later, perched on top of the oat wagon like a small brown king, chewing something that had once been an official document. It looked at me with total indifference, and went back to chewing. It had gained, I'm fairly sure, about ten pounds on the march. It was the healthiest creature in the army.

They kept coming. Okko, the young Ashtusk soldier, waving at me from the orc column with a huge, shy grin, until his sergeant cuffed him. Warchief Gorza, on a shaggy black horse the size of a small building, who saw me and nodded once, gravely, like a general acknowledging another general. Lady Ithrien, riding sidesaddle under a Prince's Canopy, waving her handkerchief at me from across the valley, with her cushion and her crooked ear-clip. Lord Venrick, riding very upright, blue with cold, refusing on principle to wear a cloak. Master Feyhallow, with his leaf. And Corvel, of course, sketching before his horse had even stopped moving, looking from the wall to the host to me, and back again, as if he couldn't believe the composition he'd been handed.

And last, riding alone at the very edge of the column, in her black and silver robes, with a flat leather case at her hip: Silvaine.

She didn't wave. She didn't nod. She rode slowly into the valley, and her eyes went, the instant she arrived, straight across the whole crowded field, to me.

And stayed there.

\* \* \*

The assembled Houses met that evening, to set the terms of the trial.

They met in a great round tent of white canvas that the High Houses' servants had raised at the center of the camp, lit by floating lamps and furnished with carpets and folding chairs and a long table at the head. Every High House was there: Vaunt, Aldervane, Velloran, Calloway, and a dozen others. The war leaders of the Concordance were there too, since the Charter said *assembled*, and it had been Thane Borrik, of all people, who had pointed out, very stiffly, that the dwarven companies were part of the assembly and would expect to be assembled. So Gorza sat at the long table with her arms folded, and Thane Borrik sat beside her with a pen, and the elder of the wolf-folk sat at the end, very straight, and the owl-folk elder perched on the back of a chair and swiveled her head to stare at everything.

And I sat at the head of the table, as the Prince, commander of the host by right of the Trial of the First Line. With Caelith at my right hand, smiling.

Caelith spoke first. He was very reasonable. He proposed that the trial of the Blight of Hearthhold should take place here, in this tent, at dawn tomorrow. It was warm, and dry, and dignified. It would be over quickly.

There was a murmur of agreement. Several High Lords nodded.

It was, I realized, the only proposal in the world that could ruin everything. A tent at dawn meant no rain. And no rain meant the trial would go Caelith's way: the High Houses would hear the evidence, decide that a book written by vermin proved nothing, and hand the valley to the Charter's maps by lunchtime. I'd have bought one day for Hearthhold, and nothing else.

So I didn't argue.

I just closed my eyes, and lifted my hand, and laid it over my left ear.

The tent went quiet. It always did, now. I'd seen the Listening work on a garden full of courtiers, and a courtyard full of lords, and the whole Council of the Host. I let the silence run long. I counted to ten. And somewhere around six, I heard a creak of chairs, and a soft rustle, as the High Lords of the Silverwood lifted their own hands to their own ears.

Then I opened my eyes.

"The forest has spoken," I said, in Aelindor's voice, and let the tongue-binding make it ancient. "The trial of the people of Hearthhold must not be held in a tent. It must be held under the open sky, where the forest can witness it. Before every soldier of the host, and every soul of the valley. At noon tomorrow."

There was a pause.

Outside the tent, through the open flap, the sky over the valley was perfectly clear: a deep blue evening, with the first stars just coming out. There wasn't a cloud anywhere. Nobody in that tent, except me and Thessa, had any reason in the world to think it would be any different at noon.

And I was the Weather Prophet. I had told them the long rains would end on the second new moon, seven weeks in advance, and they had. As far as the assembled Houses of the Silverwood were concerned, when I said *noon tomorrow*, the sky had been consulted.

At the far end of the table, Master Feyhallow, who had traveled north with the host, drew his leaf from his robe, and held it up to the lamplight, and turned it this way and that, and closed his eyes.

"The leaf agrees," he announced.

Of course it did.

Venrick raised his hand to be recognized. I recognized him. He rose and made a formal point of order: that it would be very cold, at noon, out in the open, and that some of the more elderly members of the High Houses might suffer. He said it with enormous concern, and with no cloak.

"Noted," I said. "The chair rules that the forest outranks the cold."

There was a small laugh around the table. Even Gorza's mouth twitched.

And then everyone looked at Caelith. Because, in the end, everyone always did.

He was looking at me. He had that chess-game expression again, the one I'd first seen in the summoning chamber, and I watched him consider it, piece by piece. A trial under the open sky. Before the whole host. Every soldier, every lord, every orc and wolf-folk and dwarf. And the people of Hearthhold, all of them, on their wall.

I saw him decide that he liked it.

Of course he did. It was a stage. The biggest stage the Silverwood had ever built, with the largest audience in its history. And on it, before every witness in the Concordance, the Lost Prince of the First Line would hear the case of the Blight, and the assembled Houses would condemn it, and the painters would paint it for a thousand years. *The Trial of the Last of the Blight.* Corvel could start sketching tonight.

"Under the open sky," Caelith said, softly. He smiled at me, and inclined his head, like a man accepting a gift. "How fitting, Highness. Noon, then."

He thought he was arranging his triumph. He was. He just didn't know whose.

\* \* \*

It was full dark when I went to Silvaine's tent.

She'd pitched it at the very edge of the camp, apart from everyone, the way she always did: a small, plain, perfectly square tent of black canvas, with a single lamp burning inside, so that it glowed in the dark like a lantern. I stood outside it for a full minute before I could make myself call her name.

I had Quillon's copy of the Codex under my arm.

The Codex had been with me the whole way north, wrapped in oilcloth in my saddlebag, the way Quillon had pressed it into my hands the morning we left. When Silvaine had searched my luggage, she'd gone through every trunk, every crate, every bundle, every sock. She'd held my phone in her hand. She'd sniffed my resin. But she'd never touched my saddlebag, because it was on Pudding, and Pudding had been tied up on the far side of the wagon, and you don't search a Prince's horse. And between the pages of the Codex, flattened and hidden, were the two drafts of the report on the Burning of the Sister Trees, and the land surveys from the iron room, dated two years before the Purge, with the names of the Houses written across the villages.

I'd thought, for a long time, about who to give them to.

Not Ruth; the High Houses would never believe a word from the Blight. Not Gorza; they'd call it an orc trick. Not the High Houses themselves; they'd burn them before morning. It had to be an elf. It had to be someone the High Houses couldn't dismiss, someone who'd read every word, and check every seal, and refuse to do anything with any of it until she'd done it properly, by the book, in front of witnesses.

There was exactly one person like that in Verathis. And she'd spent three months trying to catch me.

"Inquisitor," I said. "May I come in?"

There was a pause. Then: "Enter."

Inside, the tent was as plain and tidy as she was: a camp bed made with hospital corners, a folding desk, a lamp, a stack of ledgers, and the flat leather case with her crystal in it, sitting closed on the corner of the desk. She was sitting at the desk, writing. She didn't get up. She just set down her pen and looked at me, and at the book under my arm.

I didn't make a speech. I'd learned that from her, actually: when you have evidence, you don't need to argue. You just put it on the table.

So I did. I opened the Codex on her desk, and took out the papers, one by one, and laid them out in front of her in the lamplight. The first draft of the fire report. The second. The surveys, with their neat black ink across the human villages, and their dates in the corner.

"These are from the iron room," I said. "Behind the sealed door in the royal archive. I opened it under the authority of the Archive Charter. Master Ferreth was there. So was one of his apprentices, who I'm fairly sure has been reporting everything I do to the High Lord." I hadn't meant to say that last part; it slipped out. "Read them. Please."

She read them.

She read them the way she did everything, slowly and precisely, with her finger moving along every line. She read the first draft of the fire report twice. She read the second draft three times, and when she came to the single crossed-out line, and the three words in the margin, and the initial with the flourish on its tail, she stopped, and stayed stopped for a long time. Then she took a small glass lens from a drawer of her desk, and held it over the seal of the royal archive in the corner of each page, one at a time, and studied them, and then studied the ink of the margin note, and then compared it, very carefully, to something on the last page of the surveys.

It took nearly an hour. I stood the whole time. She never asked me to sit.

When she finally set down the lens, her face hadn't changed at all. But her hands, I noticed, were resting very flat on the desk, the way you hold your hands when you don't trust them not to shake.

"The archive's seals are genuine," she said. "Both drafts. And the surveys."

"Yes."

"Why," said Silvaine, "are you giving these to me?"

I'd thought about how to answer that. In the end, I just told her.

"Because you'll check them," I said. "Because you just did. Because you respect evidence more than anybody I've met here. And because you won't do anything at all with them unless it's done properly, by the law, in front of witnesses." I paused. "Tomorrow, there'll be a lot of witnesses."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then, without taking her eyes off my face, she reached out and laid one long hand flat on top of the leather case at the corner of her desk. Where the crystal was.

"My crystal blazed at your tent, Highness," she said quietly.

The lamp hissed. Outside, very far away, someone was singing "The Spring of Silvermere," badly.

"I know," I said.

She didn't open the case. She just looked at me. And then her eyes moved, slowly, deliberately, from my face to my ears. To the long, pointed, slightly crooked left one. She looked at it for a long time. I didn't move. I didn't reach up. I didn't do anything at all. I just stood there in her tent and let her look.

Then she lifted her hand off the case, and picked up her pen again, and opened her ledger.

"Tomorrow," said Silvaine of House Thistlevane, "before the assembled Houses, I will ask questions." She dipped the pen. "Of everyone."

"That's all I want," I said.

I turned to go. I'd gotten as far as the tent flap when she spoke again.

"Highness."

I turned back. She had opened a drawer of her desk and taken out two small silver coins, and was holding them out to me across the lamplight, precisely, between two fingers.

"For the ritualist," she said. "For the tankard. At the Golden Bird." A pause. "The Inquisition settles its debts."

I took them. I didn't trust myself to say anything.

I bowed, the correct bow of a Prince to an Inquisitor, and went out of her tent into the cold dark, and walked back across the camp toward my own fire, very slowly, because my legs didn't seem to be working quite right.

I didn't know what I'd just done. I'd either saved Hearthhold, or given the Inquisition everything it needed to hang me. Possibly both.

\* \* \*

Brakka was sitting by his fire at the edge of the orc lines, alone, sharpening a sword he didn't need to sharpen.

He looked up when I came into the firelight, and moved over on his log without a word, and I sat down beside him. For a long while, neither of us said anything. The fire popped. Down the line, the orc companies were settling in for the night, and somewhere a group of them were still singing, not very well. Across the valley, on Hearthhold's wall, the torches burned in a long, steady row.

I'd thought all the way across the camp about what to say to him. I still didn't know.

I couldn't tell him the truth. Not even now. Not the night before. Because if I told him, and he knew, and he stood beside me tomorrow anyway, then whatever happened to me would happen to him too. And I'd already decided, back at dawn on the march, that I wasn't going to let that happen. Not to Brakka.

But I couldn't ask him to just obey me, either. Not this time. I'd been giving Brakka orders for three months. *Stay here. Guard the door. Stay with the camp.* And every single time, he'd done it, without asking why, because that's what a spear does.

I didn't want a spear tomorrow.

"Brakka," I said. "Tomorrow, something's going to happen."

He stopped sharpening. He didn't look at me. He just held the sword across his knees, very still, and waited.

"I can't tell you what," I said. "I'm sorry. I really am. But when it happens, I don't want you to do what I tell you. I don't want you to do what your orders say. Or what Caelith says, or Gorza, or anybody." I took a breath. "I want you to stand wherever you choose. Wherever *you* choose. That's all."

Brakka didn't answer for a long time.

Then he turned his head, slowly, and looked at me. His scarred face was lit orange by the fire, with the war paint Thessa had painted on him for the third time, still a little crooked, and the one ear, and the old, tired, steady eyes. He looked at me for so long that I started to worry I'd said something wrong.

"Highness," said Brakka. "I chose a long time ago."

I didn't understand. "When?"

He almost smiled. It was the closest I'd ever seen him come.

"At lunch," he said.

And then he went back to sharpening his sword, as if that settled everything. And I sat there beside him on the log, and didn't say anything, because I couldn't. After a while, he passed me a heel of bread from his pack, without looking, and I ate it.

\* \* \*

Thessa and I went over the plan one last time in my tent, by the light of the little lamp.

It wasn't a complicated plan. The best ones never are. Noon. Under the open sky, at the foot of Hearthhold's wall, in front of everyone. I'd preside, as the Prince. Caelith would make his case. Ruth would make hers. Silvaine would ask her questions. And then the rain would come, and I would let it.

"And when it starts," I said, "and the ears start to go, and everyone's looking—"

"Pineapple," said Thessa.

"Pineapple," I agreed. "On purpose, this time."

Thessa hesitated. Then, carefully: "And the committee?"

I looked at her. "...The committee?"

"They know the word," she said. "They'll want to be involved."

I thought about seven children in a circle on the floor of the meeting hall, with a spoon and a slate and a rule of order for everything. I thought about how badly I'd wanted witnesses.

"Tell them noon," I said.

She nodded slowly. She'd been saying that word to warn me for three months, in a panic, to save me from her own magic. Tomorrow, she'd say it calmly, in front of four thousand people, and mean the opposite. I watched her think about that, in the lamplight, turning one of the charms in her braids between her fingers. Then she looked up at me, and her face was steady.

"I'm ready," she said.

After she'd gone to her own tent, I sat for a while alone. I checked the weatherglass one more time. Still climbing. I checked my lesson plan, with its three objectives in three different inks. I checked my belt pouch: E.M.'s note, and Wenna's arrow, and the empty spirit gum bottle from the Radisson.

Then I stood up, and went over to the corner of the tent, and picked up my umbrella.

It was the first one I ever made, back in the Silverwood, on the eleventh day of the long rains: split reeds and waxed silk and a handle of ash, the one that leaned a little to the left. It had kept the rain off my ears more times than I could count. I'd carried it four hundred miles.

I set my umbrella by the tent flap, where I could reach it in the morning. Then I lay awake and listened to the sky, which was perfectly clear, and wouldn't be for long.
