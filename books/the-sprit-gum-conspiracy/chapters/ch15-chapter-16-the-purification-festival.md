# Chapter 16: The Purification Festival

The long rains ended at midnight on the second new moon, exactly as I'd foretold. Nobody in the Silverwood was more surprised than me.

I'd been watching the bottle for days. About a week after the Charter was signed, the water in its neck had started to creep down, a hair at a time, the way it does when the pressure outside begins to climb. By two days before the new moon, it had dropped below my most recent scratch on the reed. By the night before, it was sitting almost exactly at the baseline I'd set on that one dry morning, weeks ago.

I lay awake that night listening. I couldn't help it. And at some point a little after midnight, I realized that the sound I'd been hearing for nearly two months, the steady patter on the leaves outside my balcony, the gurgle of the gutters, had stopped.

I got up and went out on the balcony in my nightshirt. The air was cold and still and smelled like wet earth. Above the black canopy of the Silverwood, for the first time in fifty-one nights, there were stars.

In the morning, the sky was a perfect, scrubbed, glittering blue.

The court was astonished. I don't think anyone had actually believed me. They'd nodded, and done their arithmetic on their fingers, and gone back to arguing about commas. And now here it was: the long rains over, on the very night the Prince had named, seven weeks before. Lady Ithrien wept at breakfast. The High Dewkeeper put his hand over his ear and kept it there for most of the morning. And Master Feyhallow, the Royal Augur, appeared in the Court of Fountains at midday, raised his leaf to the clear sky, and announced to a crowd of courtiers that the end of the rains had been foreseen, and that the leaf and the Prince were in perfect agreement.

I'll be honest with you, since I couldn't be honest with anyone in the Silverwood: I guessed. I picked a date that sounded far away, using a flashlight, a Styrofoam ball, and a pencil, and the sky happened to agree with me. That's all. It was the luckiest thing that had ever happened to me, and I'd once found a twenty-dollar bill in a library book.

But nobody in the Silverwood knew that. As far as the Silverwood was concerned, the Weather Prophet had looked seven weeks into the future and been right to the hour. And when the host of the Concordance marched out of the palace two days later, it marched behind a Prince whom every single soldier in it believed could see the sky's mind.

It was a long march out. There were thousands of them. The silver companies of the High Houses went first, banners lifted, armor shining in the new sun. Behind them came the orcs in their dark iron, marching in thick, heavy columns, and the dwarf companies with their wagons and their signed payment schedules, and the beastfolk at the flanks, wolf-folk loping easily alongside the column, owl-folk scouts flying high above. Somewhere in the supply train, perched on top of a wagon full of oats as if it owned it, rode the goat.

And at the very front of all of it, on a gentle old white mare named Pudding, rode the Lost Prince of the First Line, holding a foam sword.

I had ridden a horse exactly once before in my life, at a petting zoo, when I was seven. Pudding seemed to understand this. She went very slowly and never did anything surprising, and I loved her for it immediately.

Riding beside me, on a horse of his own, was Corvel.

He was the painter of House Vaunt, and Caelith had assigned him to the Prince's side for the whole of the march, so that every heroic moment could be preserved for posterity. He was a slender, eager elf with paint on his fingers and a sketchbook open on his saddle, and in the first hour, he sketched me eleven times.

"Chin up, Highness," he kept saying, squinting at me over the top of his pencil. "A touch higher. Yes. Now could you look a little more..." He searched for the word. "*Inevitable?*"

I didn't know how to look inevitable. I tried. Corvel sighed, and kept sketching, and every so often reached over and very gently adjusted the angle of my foam sword.

\* \* \*

The host marched north for nine days.

The Silverwood thinned behind us. The great trees gave way to smaller ones, and then to scattered stands of pine on open hillsides, and then to long rolling country of heather and stone, where the wind came down from the north with a cold, clean edge on it that I could feel through my cape. The sky stayed blue. The barometer, wrapped in a blanket in a crate on the supply wagon behind the goat, stayed steady. Every evening, when we made camp, I unwrapped it and checked it, and every evening it said the same thing: clear.

And every night, after camp was made and the fires were lit and Brakka had taken his post outside my tent, Thessa and I sat inside it by a little lamp and planned.

*Do something.* That was the whole objective now, and it meant one thing. The household guard of House Vaunt was going to reach the settlement and leave no witnesses. Somebody had to get there first. Somebody had to warn them.

The question was how. The Prince couldn't just ride off into the wastes on his own. Not with Corvel at his elbow sketching every move he made, and Caelith a few lengths behind, and four thousand soldiers watching.

I'd brought the copy of the Codex of the First Line with me. Quillon had pressed it into my hands the morning we left, wrapped in oilcloth, with tears in his eyes, and made me promise to bring it back. I spent three nights going through it by lamplight, looking for a rubric I could use. On the third night, I found one, in a chapter on the wars of the Old Kings.

> *Before the host goes into the field, let the heir ride ahead and see it with his own eyes, so that he may know the ground his people will fight upon.*

"That's it," I said. "That's our excuse. The heir rides ahead. It's in the Codex."

Thessa read it twice, the way she'd learned from me. "When?"

"Near the end. When we're close enough to find them." I unrolled the maps from the back of my copy of the Charter of the March and looked at the north, all carved up into House names. Somewhere in there, under all that ink, were a few hundred people. The scouts' first sighting had been near a frozen lake called Kell Tarn, the one House Calloway and House Velloran had fought over for a week. "Somewhere up there."

We'd gotten that far on the third night, and we were both feeling almost hopeful, when Lady Ithrien put her head in through the tent flap, beaming, with news.

"Highness! I've only just remembered. We reach Silvermere the day after tomorrow!"

I'd never heard of Silvermere. She was delighted to explain.

Silvermere was a shrine town in the hills, built around a sacred spring. And by a tradition older than the Purge itself, every host of the Silverwood that marched to war stopped there for the Purification of the Host. Every soldier, every lord, every general was washed in the sacred spring, fully immersed, to purify them for the work ahead.

"The Prince goes first, of course," Lady Ithrien said happily. "In front of everyone. It's the most beautiful ceremony. And Corvel will be there to paint it!"

After she'd gone, Thessa and I sat and looked at each other across the lamp for a long time, and neither of us said anything at all.

There was something else that happened on the march, too, which I want to tell you about, because it mattered later.

The soldiers had started to treat me differently. Not the elves of the High Houses, who were the same as ever: polite, admiring, and very slightly afraid of my table manners. The others. The orcs and the beastfolk, the ones who'd sat in the front row at the Council of the Host for the first time in their lives, and been asked how they felt, and been heard. Word had gotten around about that council. Word had gotten around about the grain guarantee, too, and the place on the maps for the beastfolk. And they'd started calling me something, around their fires. Thessa heard it first and told me.

The Listening Prince.

They brought me things in the evenings. Small things. A wooden spoon an orc had carved by the fire. A handful of dried apples from a dwarf. A drawing from a little otter-folk drummer boy of what I'm fairly sure was me, on Pudding, with enormous ears. I kept all of it.

On the seventh night, a young orc soldier came to the edge of the firelight outside my tent and stood there for a long time before he worked up the nerve to speak. He was barely more than a boy, with short tusks and a nervous way of shifting his feet, and he had the Ashtusk bear-skull stitched on his sleeve.

"Highness," he said at last. "Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

He looked at the ground, and then up at me, and asked the question that nobody, in three hours of the most productive war council in the history of the Silverwood, had asked.

"What are we marching *for*?"

I couldn't tell him the truth. I wanted to so badly that it hurt. But four thousand soldiers were camped around us, and some of them belonged to House Vaunt, and any one of them could have been listening.

So I told him the only true thing I could.

"I'm still asking that myself," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, as if I'd told him something important, and went back to his fire.

\* \* \*

The night before Silvermere, Thessa couldn't sit still.

She sat in my tent for an hour, trying out ideas, each one worse than the last. I could fall off Pudding on the way into town and sprain something. ("Then they'd carry you into the spring to heal it, Highness.") The Prince could take a sacred vow of silence and refuse to go in. ("That doesn't stop them putting you in.") Brakka could create a diversion. ("Brakka's idea of a diversion is hitting something.") She could cast Purify on the spring, and make it so clean and so cold that nobody would want to go in. That one we actually considered for a while, until we realized that all the soldiers would just think it was a miracle and want to go in even more.

By midnight she was pacing. Her charms were jingling constantly. Her hands had started to glow faintly at the fingertips, on and off, the way they did when she was too worried to stop them, and I was keeping one eye on the tent flap, ready to dive out.

"Go to bed," I told her finally. "We'll think of something in the morning. We always do."

She nodded, miserable, and went out to her own little tent beside mine.

I'd just lain down when I heard her, through two layers of canvas, say very faintly: "*Oh, no.*"

Then the whole camp lit up white.

It was the biggest Purify I'd ever seen her cast. It wasn't aimed at anything. It just went off, in every direction at once, like a firework that's been knocked over: a silent, rolling flash that swept out across the tents and the wagons and the picket lines and the cookfires, and was gone. Somewhere a horse whinnied. Somewhere an orc shouted. And then the whole camp was quiet again, except for the wind.

In the morning, we found out what she'd hit.

The first thing anyone noticed was the latrine trench. It had been dug along the far edge of the camp, downwind, the way latrine trenches are, and it had been in use by four thousand soldiers for five days. And it was spotless. Not clean. *Spotless.* It gleamed. You could have eaten off the bottom of it, though nobody tried. By the time I walked over to look, a crowd of orc and beastfolk soldiers had gathered around it in silent awe, and a wolf-folk corporal was explaining to everyone that the Prince's attendant had blessed the latrines in the night, and that it was a miracle. Several soldiers had put their hands over their ears.

The second thing was Brakka. He'd been sleeping outside my tent, right next to Thessa's, with his face turned toward hers. His war paint, which she'd so carefully repainted after my first week, and again after the archive, was gone. Again. He touched his bare cheeks, and looked over at Thessa's tent, and sighed the sigh of an orc who has accepted his fate.

And the third thing was Corvel.

I heard him before I saw him. It was a wail, high and thin and heartbroken, the kind of sound you'd expect from a man who'd just watched his house burn down. Then Corvel came running through the middle of the camp in his nightshirt, barefoot, holding his sketchbook open over his head with both hands.

Every page was blank.

Five days of sketches. *The Prince Departs the Silverwood.* *The Prince Gazes North.* *The Prince Upon His Steed, Resolute.* Hundreds of drawings of me looking inevitable, all gone, every line and smudge and shading stroke scrubbed off the paper as cleanly as if it had never been there. Purify had looked at them, apparently, and decided they didn't belong.

Thessa came out of her tent, saw Corvel, saw the sketchbook, and put both hands over her face. "Highness," she whispered, through her fingers. "I'm so sorry."

I looked at the blank pages flapping over Corvel's head, as he ran past us for the third time.

"Thessa," I said, "that's the best thing you've ever done."

She lowered her hands. "*What?*"

"Your magic strips away anything that doesn't belong," I said. "Dirt. Rust. Curses. And it looked at five days of drawings of me pretending to be a hero, and decided they didn't belong either." I was grinning. I couldn't help it. "It can't stand a lie."

She stared at me. Then, slowly, the corners of her mouth started to twitch.

And as I stood there looking at the blank pages, I got an idea.

Corvel, for the record, recovered remarkably fast. By that evening, he had redrawn everything, from memory, at tremendous speed. And I noticed, looking over his shoulder, that the new versions were better than the old ones. In the new *Prince Departs the Silverwood*, Pudding had become a tall, wild-eyed white stallion. My foam sword was silver now, and gleaming. My chin had gotten noticeably more inevitable. He hadn't lost his drawings at all. He'd improved them.

\* \* \*

Silvermere sat in a fold of the hills, a small town of white stone built in a ring around the sacred spring, so that every street and every house faced inward toward it. The spring itself lay in a wide, round basin of white marble, perfectly clear and very deep, with a thin mist rising off its surface in the cold morning air. I could see the bottom from the edge. It looked about fifteen feet down. It looked about four degrees.

The priests of Silvermere wore robes of plain white and were very solemn. There were about a dozen of them, and they stood in a line along the rim of the basin while the whole host of the Concordance crowded into the town around them: the High Houses in the front, in their silks and their cushions and their crooked ears, and behind them, filling every street, thousands of soldiers.

The eldest priest gave a sermon. It was short. I've remembered every word of it.

"Before the host goes north to purge the land of the Blight," he told us, raising his hands over the misty water, "the host must itself be purified. Let no stain go with you into the north. Let you be made clean, that you may make clean."

The crowd murmured its approval. Behind me, Corvel was sketching furiously in his brand-new book.

I stood there and thought: they're going to wash four thousand people in holy water so that they'll be pure enough to commit a massacre. And not one person in the town of Silvermere seemed to find that strange.

Then they called the Prince.

I walked forward through the hush to the rim of the basin. I could feel every eye in the town on me. I could feel the cold coming up off the water. I could hear Corvel's pencil scratching behind me. I stood at the very edge of the white marble, and looked down at the clear, deep, freezing water of the sacred spring, and thought about my ears, and about a little jar of resin, and about what would happen in the next ten seconds if I stepped off the edge.

Then I turned around to face them.

I reached up, slowly, and pulled aside the collar of my tunic, and turned my head, so that all of Silvermere could see the side of my neck.

The rash was real. It wasn't makeup. Two months of homemade glue and latex and humidity had left a raw, angry red stripe running down from behind my ear, along my jaw, and onto my neck, where the edges of the latex had been rubbing day after day. Thessa's calendula salve had helped, but not enough. In the cold morning light, it looked dramatic. It looked like a brand.

"The Mark of the First Line," I said, into the silence. I dropped my voice into Aelindor's register, and let the tongue-binding make it ancient. "The Old Blood may be washed by no water but the rain's."

There was a pause. Then a long, rising murmur, all the way back through the crowd.

The priests turned and huddled together, whispering. Somewhere off to my left, I heard a familiar sob, and knew without looking that the High Dewkeeper, who'd traveled with the host, was weeping again. The eldest priest turned back to me. He didn't look convinced. He looked like a man who has administered the same ritual for six hundred years and has never once had a customer refuse.

"Where," he asked, "is this written, Highness?"

And before I could answer, Thessa stepped forward from the crowd.

She was very pale. Her hands were steady. She didn't look at me.

"In the Codex of the First Line," she said, clearly, so that everyone could hear. "The fourteenth leaf of the Book of Kings."

It wasn't. There was no such passage. I'd read the whole Codex on the march, every night, by lamplight, looking for rubrics. She knew that. She'd read it with me.

It was the first lie she'd ever told for me. She told it beautifully.

The eldest priest looked at Thessa, and at my neck, and at the High Dewkeeper, who was now openly sobbing. Then he bowed. "Then the Prince is excused," he said. "The rain shall purify him, in its own time."

The ceremony moved on. Caelith went next, as the highest of the High Lords. He went into the sacred spring without the slightest hesitation, all the way under, and came up again without a gasp, his silver hair streaming, perfectly composed. He climbed out, and a servant wrapped a cloak around him, and as he walked past me, dripping, he paused just long enough to speak.

"No water but the rain's, Highness?" he said lightly. "Then let's hope the rain holds off."

He walked on. I felt the cold of it all the way down my back.

Lord Thandril of House Velloran went next. He walked to the edge of the spring, looked down at the freezing water, and then, very slowly, turned around, pulled aside his own collar, and revealed a small, unconvincing red mark on his neck that I am fairly sure had not been there at breakfast.

The priests looked at it for a long moment. Then they excused him too.

After that, it was a landslide.

One after another, the lords and ladies of the High Houses came to the edge of the spring, looked down, and discovered that they too bore the Mark of the First Line. Some of the rashes were real, from nervous scratching. Some were very obviously painted on, in berry juice, with a little beetle-shell glitter on top for authenticity. Lady Ithrien's was shaped like a heart. Within half an hour, about half of the High Houses had been excused from the Purification of the Host on sacred dermatological grounds.

Not Venrick.

Venrick walked to the edge of the spring, looked contemptuously at the rash-bearers lined up behind him, and stepped straight off the edge in his full silver robes. He went down like a stone. He came up again a moment later, blue to the lips, gasping, with his hair plastered flat over his face. And he climbed out of that spring prouder than anyone I have ever seen, before or since. A true Starlight.

The orcs and beastfolk, meanwhile, went into the spring in their hundreds, cheerfully, by company, and came out shouting and laughing and shaking themselves off like dogs. Nobody had told them about the Mark of the First Line. Nobody had thought they'd need to know. Warchief Gorza went in last of her people, with a great bellowing splash, and came up laughing so hard that she had to be helped out.

By mid-afternoon, Durgan had set up his folding table beside the basin.

The sign on it read: *CERTIFICATES OF SACRED RASH.* Underneath, in smaller letters: *Form 51-R. Old Blood. Blessed. Five crowns.* The High Dewkeeper, still red-eyed, stood beside the table and laid his hand over his ear and blessed each certificate as Durgan stamped it. By sunset, a line of lords and ladies stretched halfway around the spring, some with painted rashes, some with real ones, and some, as far as I could tell, who had no rash of any kind and were simply cold. Everyone got a certificate.

I watched it all from the steps of the shrine, next to Corvel. He'd been sketching nonstop for hours, and when I finally looked over his shoulder, I saw the picture he'd made of me at the edge of the spring, holding my collar aside, my face lit by the morning sun, my chin unbelievably inevitable.

Underneath it, in his careful hand, was the caption.

*The Prince's Humility Before the Sacred Waters.*

I stood there and watched him turn me refusing to do something into a virtue, in real time, with a pencil. He hadn't even hesitated. He'd heard the story, and he'd found the version of it that made the Prince look good, and he'd drawn that one.

I wondered, for the first time, what he'd draw if he ever saw the truth.

\* \* \*

The host made camp that night in the hills just north of Silvermere. I was sitting outside my tent by the fire with Thessa, eating dried apples from the dwarf, when the soldiers nearest us went quiet and moved aside, and Silvaine of House Thistlevane walked into the firelight.

She'd come on the march. Of course she had. I'd glimpsed her once or twice on the road, riding alone at the edge of the column with her black and silver robes pulled tight against the wind, but she'd kept her distance. Now she stood across the fire from me, perfectly composed, with a flat case of dark leather hanging at her hip on a strap.

I knew what was in it. So did Thessa. I felt her go very still beside me.

"The new crystal," Silvaine said, following my eyes. She laid one hand on the case, almost fondly. "Grown fresh, from the seed, as I said. It has never been near you, Highness." She paused. "It isn't yet attuned. The growers tell me it needs a full turn of the moon in darkness before it can be opened. A few more days."

"I look forward to it," I said. My voice came out almost normal.

Silvaine didn't smile. She never did. But she inclined her head very slightly, as if I'd made a fair move in a game.

"I heard about the spring," she said. "The Mark of the First Line." She tilted her head. "May I see it?"

I couldn't think of any reason to say no that wouldn't be worse than saying yes. So I pulled my collar aside again, and turned my head toward the firelight.

Silvaine came around the fire and bent down to look. She didn't touch it. She just looked, for a long time, very closely, the way a doctor looks at an X-ray, or the way I'd look at a primary source, with her hawk-gray eyes moving slowly along the raw red line from behind my ear down my jaw to my neck.

Then she straightened up.

"That is a real rash, Highness," she said.

"It is."

She took out her ledger and a small silver pen, and wrote something down. I couldn't see what. She wrote for longer than I'd have liked.

"Curious," said Silvaine, still writing, not looking up, "that the Old Blood should suffer exactly like ordinary skin."

She closed the ledger, bowed to me, the precise and correct bow of an Inquisitor to a Prince, and walked back out of the firelight into the dark.

Neither Thessa nor I said anything for a long while. The fire popped. Somewhere behind us in the camp, Brakka was humming the Prince's song.

Silvaine wrote it down. She was writing a lot of things down lately.

I'd told them the Old Blood could be washed by no water but the rain's. I'd made it up on the spot, at the edge of a cold spring, with a painter watching. It was, I'd realize later, the truest thing I ever said in the Silverwood.

The rains were over. The road was dry. And somewhere ahead of us in the north, a few hundred people had no idea we were coming.
