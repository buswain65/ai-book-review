# Chapter 8: The Weather Prophet

On the morning of the Procession of the Old Blood, I checked my barometer and realized the entire city was planning to watch my ears fall off.

The water in my wine bottle had risen two lines overnight. In Verathis, that was about to make me a prophet. On Earth, it would have made me a seventh-grade science project.

The procession was my fault, in a roundabout way. Durgan's Form 22-D had lasted almost exactly one day. That was how long it took Brakka to sing "The Siege of the Cellar" in the guardroom, where an off-duty elven guard heard it, and told his sister, who was a lady's maid, who told her lady, who was Lady Ithrien. By that evening, the entire court knew that the Lost Prince had cleared House Velloran's wine cellar of a demon horde of a thousand wings, armed with an ancient weapon called Skillet. Lord Thandril was so mortified that he'd gone to stay with relatives in the country.

The court, naturally, wanted to celebrate.

It was going to be enormous. An open carriage, gilded, drawn by six white stags. A route through the whole city of the Silverwood, which I still hadn't seen, so that every elf, orc, dwarf, and beastfolk in the capital could line the streets and throw flowers at the Prince who had returned. Lady Ithrien had told me all about it, twice, with her hands clasped in front of her like a child on Christmas Eve. It would begin at noon.

At dawn, I went out on the balcony in my nightshirt and checked the bottle.

The water in the neck had climbed, overnight, from my baseline scratch to well above the second one. I looked at it for a long time. Then I looked up at the sky.

The sky was perfect. A pale, cloudless, morning blue, the kind that makes you want to call in sick to go to the beach. There wasn't a wisp of anything anywhere.

That's the trick, though. That's the whole reason barometers exist. The sky only tells you what's already happening. The bottle tells you what's coming. When the pressure drops that far, that fast, the storm is usually less than a day away. Mrs. Okafor and I had spent an entire class period on this. Jaylen Price had asked if it worked on tornadoes, and then if it worked on his mom's moods. I'd said I wasn't qualified to answer the second one.

I did the math. A storm by this afternoon. An open carriage. Every citizen of the capital lining the route. And the Lost Prince of the Silverwood, held together by tree sap and dwarven liquor, waving to the crowd while the rain peeled his ears off one at a time.

I went inside, got out my lesson plan, and wrote a new line under EMERGENCIES.

*Cancel parade. Somehow.*

\* \* \*

The final preparations were being made in the Court of Fountains, a long, airy courtyard where the gilded carriage stood waiting with its six white stags, their antlers wound with ribbon. Courtiers bustled everywhere with armfuls of flowers. Caelith stood at the center of it all, giving quiet orders. Venrick stood beside him, looking at the carriage the way you'd look at a rival's wedding cake.

And beside Caelith stood a very old elf in robes the color of a summer sky, holding a single green leaf up to the morning sun.

"Master Feyhallow," Lady Ithrien whispered to me, appearing at my elbow as if she'd been summoned by the scent of drama. "The Royal Augur. He reads the weather, Highness. He's done so for two hundred years."

Master Feyhallow turned the leaf slowly in the sunlight, studying its veins. He closed his eyes. He breathed in deeply through his nose. Then he opened his eyes, lowered the leaf, and smiled the gentle, untroubled smile of a man who has never once been wrong about anything that mattered to him.

"Clear skies," he announced. "A perfect day. The leaf is unambiguous."

The courtiers murmured happily.

"He's never wrong," Lady Ithrien breathed. "He's predicted sunshine every day for two hundred years."

I thought about that. "How often is it sunny in the Silverwood?"

She thought too. "Oh, nearly every day, Highness."

"And the other days?"

"Those," said Lady Ithrien, "were unforeseeable."

I've met this guy before. Every school has one: the teacher who's been there thirty years, never changes a lesson, and whose results are always excellent, because he only teaches the honors class. He isn't a fraud, exactly. He just never has to find out whether he's any good.

I went over anyway. I bowed the correct bow for a royal official, which I'm fairly sure was the fourth kind, and I tried reason.

"Master Feyhallow. I believe there'll be a storm this afternoon. A big one."

The Royal Augur looked at me kindly, the way you'd look at a child who's just told you the moon is following the car. He held up the leaf. "The leaf, Highness, is unambiguous."

"I'm sure it is. But I have reason to think—"

"Perhaps," said Venrick, loudly enough for the courtyard to hear, "His Highness is simply nervous of crowds."

A few courtiers tittered.

I could have argued. I could have gone and gotten the bottle, and explained air pressure, and drawn a diagram on the paving stones with a piece of chalk. But I've taught science to twelve-year-olds. You can't win an argument about evidence with someone who's never needed evidence before. They don't hear the evidence. They hear you being difficult.

So I used the court's own invention against it.

I closed my eyes. I lifted my hand, slowly, and placed it over my left ear. And I stood there, perfectly still, in the middle of the Court of Fountains.

The tittering stopped.

I let the silence go on for about five seconds, which is the exact length of time I let a silence go on when a class is misbehaving. Then I opened my eyes.

"The forest," I said quietly, "is telling me it will rain."

Nobody said anything. Then, beside me, Lady Ithrien put her own hand over her own ear, and closed her eyes, and listened very hard. So did the courtier next to her. So did a stable hand holding one of the white stags.

Caelith watched all of this with an expression of quiet amusement. Then he looked at me, really looked, the way he had at the banquet, as if I were a page in a book he hadn't finished yet.

"Then by all means," he said, "we'll postpone. One day." He smiled. "Let us see what the forest has to say."

Master Feyhallow inclined his head graciously. "The leaf does not lie, Highness."

"Neither does the forest," I said, and went back inside, to wait for the sky to agree with me.

\* \* \*

The morning went on being perfect.

I sat in the window seat of my sitting room and watched the sky stay blue. Thessa sat beside me, twisting a charm in her braids. Brakka stood behind us with his arms folded, watching the sky as if it had insulted him personally. The goat was asleep on the rug.

Durgan was not with us. Durgan was out in the Court of Fountains, moving among the courtiers with a small notebook, and it took me about an hour to work out that he was taking bets.

"Two to one against rain," he reported cheerfully, when he came back to refill his flask. "Five to one against, from Lord Venrick's people. I've taken the other side of all of it."

"You bet on me?"

Durgan looked genuinely offended. "Highness. I summoned you." He tapped the notebook. "If you're wrong, it reflects on my work."

Ten o'clock came. The sky was blue. Eleven came. The sky was blue, and I was starting to sweat, which was a problem in its own right, and I could feel Venrick, somewhere in the palace, getting ready to enjoy himself.

Then, at about a quarter past eleven, the wind changed.

It was a small thing at first. The curtains on the balcony door stirred, then billowed inward. The air smelled different: wet and green and metallic, like a penny dropped in a puddle. Out over the treetops to the west, a line of darkness appeared along the horizon, as if somebody had drawn it with a ruler.

It came on fast. I've seen summer storms roll in over the Triangle, where the afternoon goes from blue to black in the time it takes to find your car keys, but I've never seen anything like the speed of that sky. In ten minutes, the sun was gone. In fifteen, the whole Silverwood had gone the dim, bruised green of an old aquarium.

At eleven-thirty, half an hour before the procession was meant to begin, the sky opened.

It didn't rain so much as collapse. The water came down in solid gray sheets that bounced a foot off the stone. In the Court of Fountains below my window, the fountains were instantly redundant. The gilded carriage filled up like a bathtub. The six white stags stood in the downpour with their ribbons plastered flat to their antlers, looking deeply betrayed. Every flower intended for the procession came loose from its garland and went sailing away down the gutters, in a long, bright, sodden river of petals.

The courtiers who'd been in the courtyard came running for the arches with their robes held over their heads, shrieking. Behind them, alone in the middle of the flooded courtyard, stood Master Feyhallow, holding his leaf.

I watched him lift it to the black sky and study it. It was soaked through and limp, and drooping over his fingers.

He was too far away to hear. But I could read his lips perfectly. I'd known what he was going to say since breakfast.

*Unforeseeable.*

I sat in my window seat, completely dry, with both hands resting on my knees. Not on my ears. I didn't need to.

Behind me, Brakka unfolded his arms. "Strategy," he said, with deep satisfaction.

Durgan, meanwhile, had already left to collect.

By evening, the court had a new name for me. It went through the palace even faster than the goose word had, and by the time Lady Ithrien arrived to tell me about it, the servants were already using it in the halls.

The Weather Prophet.

The Dewkeepers added a line to the liturgy that same night. It went, roughly: *And may the Prophet hear the rain before it falls.* Everyone said it with one hand over one ear.

Venrick, I was told, had gone to bed early.

\* \* \*

The next morning, there was a line outside my door.

It started at my chambers and ran down the corridor, around a corner, and down a flight of stairs. Brakka had been standing in front of it since dawn with a look on his face that said nobody was getting in without a reason. Everybody had a reason.

The first person in line was a miller. He was an elf, but not the kind I'd met so far: he had flour in the creases of his knuckles and a sunburned neck, and he held his cap in both hands and turned it around and around while he talked. The river that drove his wheel flooded when it rained too much, he explained. If he knew when the rains were coming, he could open the sluice gates in time and not lose the mill.

It hadn't occurred to me before then that the Silverwood had elves who worked for a living. Every elf I'd met wore silk and ate petals. This one smelled like bread.

Behind him was a farmer, an orc woman with a basket of seed potatoes she wanted to know when to plant. Behind her, a dwarf whose roof leaked. And behind the dwarf, holding a small, trembling lamb under one arm, was one of the wolf-folk.

"You're a shepherd?" I asked.

The wolf-folk shepherd looked at the lamb, and then back at me, with enormous dignity. "Highness," she said, "I'm aware of the irony."

I answered every one of them honestly. I had them follow me out to the balcony, one at a time, and I showed them the bottle, and the water in the neck, and the scratches on the reed. "It's just air pressure," I said, over and over. "When the water goes up, rain is coming. It's not magic. It's a bottle."

Nobody believed me. The miller looked at the bottle with deep reverence. The orc farmer nodded slowly, as if I'd just told her a profound mystical truth. The wolf-folk shepherd bowed to it. The lamb bleated at it.

Only once did anyone get more than a forecast. When the orc farmer asked about her seed potatoes, I told her rain was coming in four days, and then stalled, because I had no idea what you do with that information if you're a potato. Thessa, standing quietly behind me, said, "Plant on the slope, not the hollow. The rain will run off instead of sitting. They won't rot."

The farmer looked at her properly for the first time, at the Ashtusk charms in her braids, and nodded, and thanked her. Not me. Her. Thessa went pink to the tips of her tusks and spent the rest of the morning standing a little straighter.

By mid-morning, I had stopped explaining. By noon, Durgan had set up a table.

It was a small folding table, which I recognized from the outside of his pack, placed at the head of the line just outside my door. On the front of it, he'd hung a sign, lettered by hand in large, confident capitals:

*CONSULTATIONS.*

"Durgan," I said.

"Highness." He had his ledger open. He had a little cashbox. He had a quill behind his ear. He looked like a happier dwarf than I'd ever seen. "Every prophet needs a business manager."

"We are not charging that man for knowing when it's going to rain on his mill."

Durgan opened his mouth. I gave him the look. It's the look I give a kid who's about to tell me the dog ate his homework, and it works on dwarves, it turns out, better than it works on twelve-year-olds.

"Anyone who grows food," I said, "or makes it, or herds it, asks for free."

Durgan made a sound like a man passing a kidney stone. He closed his eyes. He breathed in. He breathed out.

"*Sliding scale*," he said, as if the words were being pulled out of him with tongs.

Then he went and found the nobles.

That afternoon, Lord Aestrin of House Calloway paid fifty crowns to find out whether it would rain on his garden party. Lady Ithrien paid thirty for a picnic. Someone sent a servant, anonymously, with forty crowns and a request to know whether to bring a cloak to the Prince's archery trial. The servant wore Lord Venrick's colors. Durgan charged him double.

Free for the people who grow the food. Expensive for the people throwing garden parties. I'd invented progressive taxation by accident.

Late that afternoon, a servant in sky-blue livery came to the table, paid twenty crowns for a three-day forecast, and asked, in a very low voice, for complete discretion. The next morning, the Royal Augur held his leaf up to the sun in the Court of Fountains and announced that it would rain in three days.

It rained in three days.

From then on, Master Feyhallow was never wrong again. He was getting his forecasts from me, at a professional discount. Durgan recorded the discount in his ledger under *Unforeseeable expenses*.

By the end of the week, Durgan's ledger said we'd taken in six hundred and forty crowns. After the geese, that put me at thirty-nine thousand, one hundred and sixty to go. It wasn't much. But for the first time since the invoice, the number was going down.

On the last afternoon, the line went quiet all at once. When I looked up, Silvaine of House Thistlevane was standing at the table.

She held a small slip of paper with her question written on it, in handwriting so precise it looked engraved. She wanted to know whether it would rain on the day her crystal came back from repair. It was due, she informed me, in about two weeks.

My mouth went dry. A barometer can't see two weeks ahead. I told her clear skies anyway.

She thanked me with exactly the correct bow, paid in full without being asked, and walked away down the corridor. I didn't breathe again until she'd turned the corner.

Caelith came at the end of the last day.

He didn't wait in line. The line simply wasn't there anymore when he arrived, the way a school hallway empties when the principal comes around the corner. He walked out onto my balcony, glanced at the bottle on the rail with polite interest, and turned to me.

"One question, Prophet," he said. "When will the long rains end?"

I understood immediately what he was asking.

The army would march north the day the rains ended. He'd told me so himself. Whatever day I named would be the day it began.

And I couldn't see it. A barometer can tell you what's coming tomorrow, maybe the day after. It cannot see two months ahead. Nobody in the world could see two months ahead.

But nobody in the Silverwood knew that.

"That isn't something the forest tells me all at once," I said slowly.

Caelith smiled. "Then listen closely, Highness," he said. "The whole Concordance is waiting on your answer."

He left. I stood on the balcony and looked at the bottle.

On my lesson plan, under Procedure, the first item was *Buy time*. I'd underlined it twice, the first night. And I realized, standing there, that for the first time since I'd arrived, I had something Caelith needed, and couldn't simply take.

\* \* \*

That night, I couldn't sleep, so I went out on the balcony.

The storm had washed the whole sky clean. The stars over the Silverwood were so thick and bright that they looked fake, like a ceiling in a planetarium. The water in my bottle had dropped back down to the baseline. Clear skies, tomorrow and the day after.

I sat on the stone floor with my back against the rail and thought about my dad.

I hadn't let myself do that yet. Not properly. I'd thought about Dev and Mr. Pickles and Mrs. Okafor and my third period, and I'd thought about rent. But I'd been careful not to think about my dad, the same way you're careful not to put weight on a sprained ankle.

My dad is an accountant. He has never farmed anything in his life; he once killed a cactus. But every summer when I was a kid, we drove three hours from Raleigh to the beach at Emerald Isle in his old pickup, with the windows down because the air conditioning had been broken since before I was born. And the CD player in that truck had been stuck for fifteen years on a single album of seventies country music. It was the only thing it would play.

There was one song on it he always turned up. A fast one, with a fiddle that sounded like it was being chased, about a guy who's perfectly happy with his simple life on the farm and wouldn't trade it for anything. My dad sang every word of it, off-key, at the top of his lungs, slapping the steering wheel, every single time, for three hours each way. My mom used to put her hand over her eyes. I used to pretend to be embarrassed. I knew every word by the time I was six.

I don't know why I started singing it. I think I just needed to hear something from home.

I sang it quietly at first, badly, mostly from memory, slapping my knee where my dad would have slapped the steering wheel. My voice cracked on the high part. It always does. It always did on his, too.

When I finished, I realized it was very quiet behind me.

Brakka was standing in the balcony doorway. I don't know how long he'd been there. He was completely still, in a way I'd only seen once before, on the bottom step of a wine cellar. But it wasn't fear on his face this time. I didn't have a word for what it was.

"Again," he said.

So I sang it again, louder. And this time, halfway through, I heard a little jingle of charms from the sitting room, and Thessa came out in her nightgown with her braids half undone and sat down on the stone beside me and started clapping along. She was completely off the beat. She didn't care, and neither did I.

Then Durgan appeared, in a nightcap, holding Skillet in one hand and a spoon in the other, and began to keep time against the bottom of the pan with a series of loud, metallic *clangs*. Then the goat woke up and wandered out and bleated, enthusiastically, in roughly the right key.

When it was over, Brakka stood there for a long moment, breathing.

"It is a war song," he said at last.

"It's about farming," I said.

Brakka considered this with great seriousness. "Farming," he said, "is war against the ground."

I couldn't argue with that.

On the next balcony over, an elf in a silk sleeping mask threw open her shutters, stared at the four of us and the goat, and slammed them closed again.

Brakka didn't notice. He was humming. Under his breath, over and over, he was humming the fiddle part, getting it slightly wrong and then going back and trying again, and his lips were moving, and I realized he was already putting words to it.

An orc, a dwarf, a shaman, and a goat, singing a farm song badly on an elven balcony. It wasn't home. But it was close enough.

My dad never could carry a tune either. I hoped that wherever he was, someone was singing it badly for him too.
