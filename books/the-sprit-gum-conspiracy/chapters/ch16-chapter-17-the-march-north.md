# Chapter 17: The March North

Every road trip I've ever been on has had the same three phases: excitement, boredom, and somebody getting accused of being a human in the luggage.

Okay. That last one was new. But the first two were right on schedule.

The excitement lasted until about a day north of Silvermere, where the hills flattened out into the beginning of the wastes: long, empty, rolling country of gray grass and gray stone and gray sky, with a wind that came straight down from the top of the world and went straight through my cape. After that, it was boredom. Day after day of the same gray, the same wind, the same creak of wagon wheels, the same sound of four thousand people walking. Corvel ran out of heroic things to sketch and started sketching rocks, then improving them.

And so, like every bored person on every long road trip in history, the party started finding ways to entertain itself.

Durgan had started a list. He'd opened a fresh page in his ledger, the morning after Silvermere, and titled it, in large, angry capitals, *INCIDENTS OF BLIGHT INTERFERENCE*. And then, every time something went wrong for Durgan, he wrote it down.

On the first day, a wheel came off his wagon. *Blight interference.* On the second day, his flask turned out to be empty. *Blight interference.* On the third day, the goat ate four of his forms, including a half-finished invoice for his hazard pay from the archery trial. *Blight interference (goat-assisted).* By the end of the week, the list was three pages long, and it included the wind, the cold, the quality of the oatcakes, a blister, and on one memorable morning, his own snoring, which he insisted had been worse than usual and could only be explained by malign influence from the north.

Brakka, meanwhile, had written a new ballad.

It was called "The Spring of Silvermere," and it was, like all his ballads now, set to my dad's old road-trip song. It told the story of the Purification of the Host, very heroically, with a verse about the Mark of the First Line, a verse about Venrick, and a verse about the latrine. He sang it at every campfire, every night, and the orc and beastfolk soldiers had started joining in on the chorus, which went, roughly:

> *Oh, the Prince would not bathe, for the Prince is too grand,*\
*And the rain is the only thing touching his hand!*

It was not good. They sang it with tremendous feeling.

And me? I'd started grading.

I couldn't help it. I'd been sitting on Pudding for twelve days with nothing to do but look at the army, and somewhere around day four, my brain had quietly switched into teacher mode, and started assessing everyone. By day twelve, I'd graded the entire host of the Concordance, in my head, on a rubric I'd invented.

The generals of the High Houses got a B-plus for camp layout: tidy, efficient, but nothing creative. Venrick's cavalry got an A-minus for discipline and a C for attitude, since he kept insisting they ride on the windward side of the column, out of principle. The dwarf companies got a solid A; their wagons never broke down, and they had a payment schedule. Durgan's tent, which collapsed on top of him every single night without fail, usually around two in the morning, got an F. The orc companies got an A for marching and an A-plus for singing, though not for the quality of the singing, which was a C-minus. That was for enthusiasm.

And the goat got the only A-plus in the army.

The goat had thrived on the march. It rode on top of the oat wagon like a small brown king. It had eaten oats, forms, a saddle blanket, two of Corvel's discarded sketches, and, on the ninth night, an entire pie it had stolen off the High Houses' supper table while Venrick's back was turned. It was fatter and glossier and happier than it had ever been. It was, by any reasonable measure, the most successful member of the expedition.

The young Ashtusk soldier who'd asked me what we were marching for had started walking near Pudding, too. His name, it turned out, was Okko. He didn't say much. He just turned up beside us every morning, and fell into step, and fed Pudding bits of apple from his pocket when he thought I wasn't looking. Pudding loved him. I didn't mind at all.

\* \* \*

On the fourteenth night of the march, the moon came up full.

I knew what that meant. So did Thessa. We'd been watching it grow for a week, every night, the way you'd watch a countdown clock. A full turn of the moon in darkness, Silvaine had said. And now it was over.

She opened the case at dusk, in the middle of camp.

She did it in the open, on purpose, I think. The word had gone around that the Inquisitor's new crystal would be unsealed that evening, and by the time the sun went down, a crowd had gathered in the wide space between the cookfires: soldiers, orcs, beastfolk, a few curious lords. Corvel was there with his sketchbook, of course. Even Caelith had strolled over from the High Houses' end of camp to watch, with his hands clasped behind his back and an expression of mild interest.

I was in my tent. I'd gone in before it started, because I couldn't bear to watch, and because Thessa had pointed out, very reasonably, that it would be a lot harder for Silvaine to point a crystal at me if I wasn't standing in front of her. So I sat on my camp stool, in the dim light, and listened, with Thessa sitting very still beside me and Brakka at his post outside the flap.

Through the canvas, I heard the crowd go quiet. I heard the click of a latch, and the creak of leather.

Then a soft, collective intake of breath, as Silvaine lifted the new crystal out of its case into the dusk.

Thessa told me later what it looked like. It was smaller than the old one, about the length of a finger, and perfectly clear, so clear it was almost invisible except where it caught the light of the cookfires. Silvaine held it up on its silver chain and turned slowly, in a full circle, the way you'd turn a compass to find north.

It began to glow.

Faintly, at first. A pale, uncertain blue, barely brighter than the moon. A murmur went through the crowd. Silvaine stood very still for a moment. Then she began to walk.

I heard her footsteps. Slow, measured, precise. I heard the crowd parting for her, a shuffle of boots and paws. And I heard the footsteps coming closer.

The light came first. It began to show through the canvas of my tent, very faintly, a pale blue glow on the wall that faced the middle of the camp. Then brighter. Then brighter still. I watched it climb up the side of the tent like a sunrise, and I couldn't move. Beside me, Thessa had grabbed my wrist so hard it hurt.

The footsteps stopped right outside.

The whole wall of my tent was blazing blue now, so bright that I could see the weave of the canvas, and the shadow of Brakka's broad back against it, and beyond him, the slim, upright shadow of Silvaine, holding up the crystal on its chain. I could hear the crowd behind her, completely silent. I could hear my own heart.

Then Silvaine spoke, in her clear, carrying, reasonable voice. And she reasoned it through, out loud, step by step, the way she always did.

"This crystal," she said, "has never been near the Prince. It was grown fresh, from the seed, in darkness. It cannot have been contaminated, or overloaded, or misaligned, as its predecessor was."

A pause.

"And it blazes at the Prince's tent."

I closed my eyes.

"But the Prince himself," Silvaine went on, "has been tested. In the Hall of Crowns, before the whole Concordance. The old crystal was faulty, but the Prince is of the Old Blood. The archives confirm it. The seers confirm it. The forest itself confirmed it, at the Trial of the First Line." Another pause. I could almost hear her thinking. "Therefore, the crystal is not responding to the Prince."

I opened my eyes.

"It is responding to something *in the Prince's tent*," said Silvaine. And then, with a ring of absolute certainty, so that the whole crowd could hear: "The Blight is hiding in the Prince's luggage."

\* \* \*

There's a particular kind of calm that comes over you when something terrible is happening and there's no time to panic about it. Teachers know it. It's the fire-drill calm. I stood up off my camp stool, smoothed my cape, checked my ears with both hands, and went outside.

The crowd had grown. Silvaine stood just outside the tent flap with the crystal held up in front of her, blazing blue, so bright that everyone around her was squinting. Brakka stood between her and the tent like a wall, arms folded. And off to one side, Caelith was watching the whole thing with an expression of quiet, delighted interest, as if it were a play put on for his benefit.

"Highness," said Silvaine, with the correct bow. "Forgive me. I must search your baggage."

I looked at Caelith. Caelith smiled.

"By all means, Inquisitor," he said. "Let the law be satisfied."

So she searched it.

My baggage was in a wagon beside my tent: four wooden trunks, a crate, and a few bundles. She had two soldiers lift down each one in turn and open it, while she stood over it with the crystal and went through the contents with her own hands, one object at a time, and the whole camp watched.

The first trunk held my spare clothes, which were the same tunic, washed, and a second bedsheet cape Durgan had made for me. Nothing. The crystal stayed bright.

The second trunk held Gwendolyn's care kit: brushes, pins, the little silver leaves Thessa used for the braids. Silvaine examined a hairpin very closely for a long time. Then she put it back. The crystal stayed bright.

The crate held the barometer.

She lifted it out, wrapped in its blanket, and unwrapped it, and held it up to the firelight: a wine bottle, upside down in a bowl, with a reed waxed to the side, scratched with little marks.

"What is this, Highness?"

"A divining bottle," I said. "Of the First Line. It's how the Prophet reads the sky."

There was a murmur of awe from the crowd. Several soldiers put their hands over their ears. Silvaine looked at the bottle for a long moment, then wrote something in her ledger, and wrapped it up again very carefully.

The third trunk held my little jar of resin. She uncorked it and sniffed it, and recoiled slightly from the firewater.

"Arrow glue," said Thessa, instantly, before I could open my mouth. "For the fletching, Inquisitor. Ashtusk recipe."

Silvaine looked at Thessa for a moment longer than I liked. Then she wrote that down too.

And then she reached into the bottom of the third trunk, under my spare socks, and took out my phone.

I'd forgotten it was there. I'd wrapped it in a scrap of cloth and tucked it away on the night I arrived, at thirty-eight percent, and hadn't touched it since. Now Silvaine unwrapped it and held it up in the blue light of the crystal: a flat black slab of glass and metal, smooth and cold and perfectly dark, unlike anything in the whole world of Verathis.

The crowd went silent. I felt Thessa stop breathing beside me.

Silvaine turned it over in her hands. She looked at her reflection in the black screen. She ran her fingertip along its edge, and found the button on the side, and pressed it. Nothing happened. It was off. She pressed it again. Nothing.

"And this, Highness?" she asked, very quietly.

I had about two seconds. I used both of them.

"A scrying stone," I said. "Of the First Line. It wakes only for royal blood."

Silvaine looked at me. Then she looked at the phone. Then she pressed the button again, firmly, with her thumb, and held it. And held it.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. It was off. If she'd held it for three more seconds, it would have turned on, and played its little startup chime, and shown the whole camp a lock-screen photo of me and Dev at a minor-league baseball game holding giant foam fingers. But she let go at two.

She wrapped it back up in its cloth, and put it back in the trunk, and wrote in her ledger for a very long time.

Off to one side, Corvel was sketching furiously. I glanced at his page: a noble, serene Prince standing calmly aside while his baggage was searched, hands folded, chin raised, untroubled. Underneath, the caption read *The Prince Submits Himself to the Law.* In the real version, the Prince was sweating through his tunic.

The crystal was still blazing.

That left one trunk. The fourth, and the biggest: a heavy, iron-bound thing at the very back of the wagon, which I didn't recognize. I didn't remember packing it. The soldiers heaved it down and set it on the ground in front of Silvaine, and stepped back.

And as they did, the trunk moved.

Just a little. A small shift, as if something inside had rolled over in its sleep.

The crowd gasped as one. Silvaine's face went perfectly still, and then perfectly fierce. She handed the crystal to a soldier, drew a slender silver dagger from her belt, stepped up to the trunk, set her hand on the lid, and in one swift, practiced movement, threw it open.

Inside, curled up on a nest of blankets and empty sacks, with his arms wrapped around his flask and his beard spread out over his chest like a rug, was Durgan Pell, fast asleep.

He'd been riding in the luggage. For three days, it turned out. Ever since his tent had collapsed for the fifth time, he'd decided that walking was beneath a High Ritualist of the Seventh Circle, and had climbed into the biggest trunk in the Prince's wagon every morning with a pillow and his flask, and slept there until camp.

Silvaine stood over him with her dagger raised and looked down at him for a long, long moment.

A dwarf. With ears that were, as she had noted in her ledger on several occasions, insufficiently pointed. Hiding in the Prince's luggage.

She lowered the dagger. Her face cleared. She looked, for the first time since I'd known her, genuinely satisfied.

"Durgan Pell," she said, "I arrest you on suspicion of being the Blight."

Durgan opened one eye. He looked up at the Inquisitor, and the dagger, and the blazing crystal, and the crowd, and the stars. He closed the eye again.

Then he opened both of them, sat bolt upright in the trunk with a roar of outrage that echoed off the hills, and, before he had even finished climbing out, demanded a quill. The first thing he wrote with it, before anything else, was one more entry on the list in his ledger: *Arrested on suspicion of being the Blight. (Blight interference.)* Then, within a minute, sitting on the edge of the open trunk, under arrest, he had already begun to draft an invoice for wrongful detention, disturbance of rest, and emotional damages to a member of the Guild of Arcane Conveyance in good standing.

I couldn't help it. I'd been grading the whole army for twelve days, and as I watched Silvaine stand guard over Durgan, who was still writing on the edge of his trunk, my brain finished the assessment on its own. Reasoning: A. Evidence: A. Procedure: A-plus. Conclusion: F.

Silvaine had arrested forty-five people now. She'd come within a tent flap of the forty-sixth.

\* \* \*

It was Caelith who got Durgan out of it. And the way he did it was the most frightening thing I saw on the whole march.

He strolled over from the edge of the crowd, still smiling, still with his hands clasped behind his back, and looked down at Durgan, who was sitting on the edge of the trunk writing furiously, and at Silvaine, standing over him with her dagger.

"Inquisitor," said Caelith pleasantly, "I admire your diligence. But consider." He nodded at Durgan. "This dwarf summoned the Prince. If he is the Blight..." He let the sentence hang there for a moment, in the cold air, with the whole camp listening. "...then so, surely, is the Prince."

He said it lightly. Like a joke. Like the most obviously absurd thing in the world, to make a point. The crowd laughed, a little, uncertainly.

Silvaine didn't laugh.

She opened her mouth to answer. Then she closed it again. She stood there with her dagger in one hand, looking at Durgan, and then, slowly, at me. Not at my face. At my ears.

It lasted maybe two seconds. It felt like the whole of the long rains.

Then she sheathed her dagger, and took the crystal back from the soldier who'd been holding it, and put it away in its case, and closed the latch. The blue light went out. The whole camp seemed to get darker and colder at once.

"The prisoner is released," Silvaine said quietly.

She didn't say another word all evening. She walked back to her own tent at the edge of the camp, and didn't come out. I kept looking over at it, all night. There was a lamp lit inside, very late.

I'd seen Silvaine angry. I'd seen her soaked, and outmaneuvered, and wrong. I'd never seen her quiet before. Quiet was much worse.

\* \* \*

That night, the four of us sat around the fire outside my tent, and for a long time nobody said anything at all.

I'd told them, before supper, that I'd be riding ahead in the morning. That Thessa would come with me, as my attendant. That it was in the Codex: the heir sees the field before the host. And they'd all just nodded, the way you nod when someone tells you something you were half expecting and don't want to hear. Even Brakka. Especially Brakka.

So we sat there. The goat was asleep against Brakka's leg. The fire popped. Somewhere off across the camp, a company of orcs was singing "The Spring of Silvermere," badly.

It was Durgan who broke the silence, which surprised everybody, including, I think, Durgan.

"My license," he said abruptly, to the fire, "has been suspended. Twice."

We all looked at him.

He took a long drink from his flask and didn't look at any of us. "By the Guild. The first time, for a summoning that went... somewhat wrong. A bear. It was supposed to be a hero. It was a bear." He scowled into the fire. "The second time was a matter of paperwork. Mine was too thorough." Another drink. "I owe money, in Under-Karrak. To people who don't send invoices. They send cousins."

Nobody said anything.

"My mother has a forge," said Durgan, more quietly. "In the deep levels. It's been in the family for eleven generations. She's two hundred and ninety years old, and she still works the bellows herself, every morning, because she won't let anyone else touch them." He turned the flask over in his hands. "If I don't pay what I owe, they'll take the forge."

He looked up, finally, at me.

"That's what the bonus is for," he said. "Not the wine." He paused. "Well. Not *only* the wine."

I didn't know what to say. So I just nodded, the way you nod at a kid who's just told you why he's really been acting out all semester. Durgan nodded back, once, gruffly, and went back to his flask, and that was the end of it. But I never quite looked at his invoices the same way again.

Then Brakka told the story of the goose.

He'd told me the short version, in the cellar. Now he told the whole thing: how he'd been seven years old, and small for an orc, and gone down to the river on his own to fish, and met a goose "the size of a pony, with the soul of a demon." How it had chased him a full mile along the riverbank. How it had caught him at the ford. How his father had found him an hour later, sitting in the shallows, missing one ear, still holding his fishing pole. And as he told it, sitting by our fire, with the goat asleep against his leg, something happened I'd never seen before.

Brakka started to laugh. At himself. At the goose, and the pole, and the ford. His shoulders shook, and he wiped his eyes with one huge knuckle, and laughed until Thessa and Durgan and I were laughing too.

Thessa sang next. Just a few lines, very softly, of one of her grandmother's songs, in old orcish, about a river in spring. I didn't understand the words. The tongue-binding only covered Common, I'd learned, not the old orcish of her grandmother's songs. But I didn't need it to.

And then they all looked at me.

I couldn't tell them the truth. Not Brakka, and not Durgan, not yet. So I told them what I could.

I told them about my dad's truck, with the broken air conditioning and the CD player stuck on one album. I told them about the beach, and the way the sand gets so hot in the summer that you have to run across it. I told them about my students: Jaylen, and his eraser lanyard, and the girl in the third row who always asked one more question right as the bell rang, and the kid in the back who'd never once spoken in class until the day I taught the weather unit, and then didn't stop.

"I miss home," I said. It was the truest thing I'd said in weeks.

Brakka nodded slowly, looking into the fire. Then he said, with great seriousness, as if quoting something very old and wise:

"Home is where your spear is fed."

I started to laugh. Then I stopped, because I realized he meant it. And I realized, sitting there in the cold with the three of them and the goat, that he was right.

Durgan held out his flask to me, without a word.

I'd never touched it. Not once, in three months. I took it, and drank, and it was like swallowing a lit match, and I coughed for about a minute while Brakka thumped me on the back, nearly dislocating my shoulder again.

It was the best night I'd had in Verathis.

\* \* \*

We left before dawn.

I went to Caelith's tent first, because I had to. He was already awake, sitting at a small camp desk by lamplight with a cup of tea, as if he'd been expecting me. Maybe he had. I bowed, and told him I would ride ahead to see the field, as the heir of the First Line has always done, and I quoted the Codex, word for word.

Caelith listened politely. Then he smiled.

"Of course, Highness," he said. "The Old Kings always did. Take your attendant." He sipped his tea. "The painter will follow tomorrow."

He didn't ask where I was going. He didn't need to. As far as Caelith was concerned, the Prince was supposed to be out in front. It was exactly where he wanted me.

Thessa had our horses ready by the time I came back: Pudding, saddled and blinking in the dark, and a shaggy little gray pony for Thessa, borrowed from the dwarves. She'd packed two days of food, a waterskin, her satchel, my jar of resin, and a spare cloak each. I strapped the barometer behind Pudding's saddle, wrapped in its blanket. The goat watched all of this from on top of the oat wagon with deep suspicion.

Brakka was waiting by the horses.

He was in his full armor, with his sword on his back and a pack over one shoulder, and he'd clearly been standing there for some time. When he saw me, he straightened up.

"I come too," he said.

"No," I said.

It was the hardest word I'd said in the Silverwood. Harder than the battle cry, harder than *Do something*. Because I knew, by then, more or less what was waiting for us in the north. And I knew that if Brakka came, if he was there when the truth came out, there was no version of what came next where he didn't end up standing between me and the whole Concordance, with his sword drawn. And they'd kill him for it. For hiding me. For being my friend.

I couldn't tell him that. So I told him the next best thing.

"I need you here," I said. "Guarding the camp. Watching the household guard. If anything changes, I need someone I trust to see it."

Brakka looked at me for a long time, in the gray half-light before sunrise. I don't know what he saw. I think he knew I wasn't telling him everything. I think he'd known for a while.

He didn't say "Strategy." He didn't argue. He just reached out one enormous hand and set it on my shoulder, lightly, the way you'd set it on something you were afraid of breaking.

"Come back," he said.

That was all. Then he let go, and stepped back, and stood beside the dead campfire with his arms folded, the way he'd stood outside my door every night since my first week in the Silverwood.

Thessa and I mounted up, and rode north out of the camp, past the sleeping tents and the picket lines and the little banked fires, and out into the open wastes, under a sky just starting to go gray in the east.

I looked back once, from the top of the first hill. The camp was a scatter of little fires in the dark. One of them was Brakka's, and he was still standing beside it, watching us go.

Somewhere ahead of us, in the cold, there were a few hundred people. We had two days to find them.

## Interlude: Hearthhold

The two lookouts on the south ridge saw the fires a little after midnight.

They were an odd pair, Wenna and old Tobiah: she was sixteen and had the sharpest eyes in the village, and he was seventy-three and had stood watch on that ridge every third night for fifty years without ever once seeing anything but snow, and once, memorably, a moose. They sat in a little stone hut at the top of the ridge with a shuttered lantern and a pot of nettle tea, and they took turns at the slit in the wall that looked south over the wastes.

It was Wenna's turn. She looked for a long time before she said anything. Then she said, very quietly, "Tobiah."

He came and looked.

Far to the south, so far that they were only pinpricks, there were lights on the dark land. Not one or two. Not the campfire of a lost hunter or a party of traders. Dozens of them. Hundreds, maybe, scattered across the plain like someone had spilled a bag of embers. Too many to count. More than they had ever seen in one place in their lives.

Tobiah looked at them for a long, long time. Then he put his hand on Wenna's shoulder.

"Run," he said. "Wake Ruth."

She ran.

She ran down the ridge path in the moonlight, the way she'd run it a thousand times as a child, down past the forest. It was a careful forest: every tree in it counted, every stump marked with a notched stake, and beside every stump a sapling, planted the same season the tree was cut, so that the forest never got smaller, only older. She ran past the terraces, where the hillsides had been cut into long, level steps held up by walls of fitted stone, so that the snowmelt ran down slowly from field to field instead of washing the soil away. She ran past the long, low greenhouses dug into the south-facing slopes, half underground, roofed with glass the village had made itself from the sand of the lake shore, kept warm all winter by banked peat fires. Through their misted panes, in the moonlight, she could see the green of growing things.

She ran past all of it without looking, because she'd seen it every day of her life, and didn't know yet that it was remarkable.

The village of Hearthhold sat at the bottom of the valley, beside the frozen lake: a few dozen low stone houses with turf roofs, and one larger building in the middle, the meeting hall. Over its door, carved into the lintel stone so long ago that the letters had gone soft, were seven words. Every child in Hearthhold learned them before they learned to read. They were the first rule of the village, older than anyone could remember, the rule that had kept them alive for three hundred years at the top of the world.

*Take only what you can give back.*

Ruth Ashgrove opened her door before Wenna had finished knocking.

She was the eldest of the village elders, small and straight-backed and white-haired, with hands roughened from sixty years of turning soil. She looked at Wenna's face in the moonlight, and at the way the girl was breathing, and she didn't ask what was wrong. She just took her coat down from its hook.

"How many?" she said.

"Too many to count, Elder."

Ruth nodded slowly. She stood in her doorway for a moment, looking up at the ridge, and the stars above it. She didn't look frightened. She looked like a woman who'd been expecting a guest her whole life, and her mother before her, and her grandmother before that, and who had finally heard the knock.

"Wake the village," she said. "Quietly. No bells."

And by the light of a few shuttered lanterns, without a word of complaint, the people of Hearthhold began to prepare for a last stand that they knew they couldn't win.
