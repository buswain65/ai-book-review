# Chapter 6: Assigned

Fred's first order as a member of the Chosen One's party was to stand somewhere he wouldn't be in the paintings.

"There," said the handler, pointing with her clipboard. "Behind the third wagon. No, further. Further. There." She squinted at him, then at an imaginary frame in the air. "Lovely. You're out of shot."

Fred stood out of shot and looked at the Hero's Caravan.

It was camped in a meadow outside the capital, and it was less a caravan than a small, gold-plated city on wheels. At its center rose Kaito's pavilion, a great white tent with gold ropes and a banner showing a sword and a sunrise. Around it, in careful rings, stood the wagons. Each of the four companions had a wagon painted in her own colors. There was a painters' wagon, which smelled of turpentine, and a bards' wagon, which sounded of lutes. There was a wagon with RADIANCE TONICS painted on its side in letters a yard high, with a crate of small blue bottles stacked by its steps. There was a Bureau of Destiny office wagon with a chimney, a filing cabinet bolted to its roof, and a queue. And at the very back, behind the cooks and the farriers and the wagons of spare banners, came the baggage train, where the people who did the actual work lived.

That was where Fred reported.

The handler found his name on her clipboard and frowned at the hyphen, the way everyone did. Then she found his line, and her face cleared.

"Sanitation Support," she said. "Oh, good. We've been waiting for you. You'll be responsible for the Gilded Privy and the auxiliary facilities."

"What are the auxiliary facilities?"

Without looking, she pointed to the far edge of the meadow, where a long trench ran along the tree line behind a canvas screen.

"Those," she said. "Try not to be seen going in."

The Gilded Privy stood alone on a little rise of its own, where the light was best.

It was a wagon, but only technically. Every inch of it was covered in gold leaf. It had carved columns at the corners, a weathervane on the roof in the shape of a smaller gold privy, and a door with a crystal handle. Inside were a velvet seat in royal blue with a gold-tasseled cushion, a shelf of scented oils, and a mirror in a jeweled frame. On the wall facing the seat hung a portrait of Kaito Reklamo in full armor, gazing into the middle distance with the wind in his hair. Wherever you sat, his eyes followed you. Fred suspected this was on purpose.

The Gilded Privy was spotless. As far as Fred could tell from the dust under the velvet seat, nobody had ever used it for anything. It existed to be painted, and it was painted often, from a variety of flattering angles.

He polished it anyway. Gold leaf wanted a light hand and a soft cloth, and he was careful with the portrait. Then he took his mop and bucket to the auxiliary facilities.

The trench was everything the Gilded Privy was not. It served forty-one wagons, three painters, two bards, a dozen handlers, the cooks, the farriers, the grooms, the sponsor's representative and the Chosen One's four companions, and for some time it had been served in return by nobody at all. Fred looked at it for a long moment, the way he had once looked at aisle 4.

Then he rolled up his sleeves and did them both properly.

On the second morning, Fred was summoned for orientation.

The Director of Companion Relations had a wagon of her own, with lace curtains and a potted fern, and she sat behind a small desk with a very large binder. She was a pleasant, round-faced woman with her hair in a bun so tight it seemed to be holding her face in place. She smiled at Fred the whole time she talked, as if the smile were a policy.

"Welcome to the Companion Program," she said. "We're so glad you're here. Now, a few ground rules." She opened the binder. "The Chosen One's companions are valued members of our team. They are engaged on seasonal contracts, selected through rigorous focus-group testing to represent the archetypes our audiences love most. Mage. Elf. Beast-kin. Priestess." She ticked them off on her fingers. "Staff may not address a companion directly. If you need to speak with a companion, please raise a ticket."

"A ticket," said Fred.

She slid a form across the desk. It was pink, and it had eleven boxes. The last one said REASON FOR CONTACT (NOTE: 'BEING FRIENDLY' IS NOT A REASON).

"Tickets are reviewed weekly," said the Director. "We aim to respond within the season."

Fred folded the pink form and put it in his shirt pocket, next to the one from the waiting hall. He was building up quite a collection.

After that he saw the companions often, but only from a distance, and only from out of shot. Luna Starweaver, the mage, stood beside Kaito at every public appearance with her hands raised, and wherever Kaito walked, a soft golden glow followed him and small sparkles drifted down around his hair. Sylvia Moonwhisper, the elf, stood on his other side with her great bow, looking ethereal. Mittens, the rogue, crouched at his feet with her knives drawn and her cat ears pricked. Seraphina the Pure walked a step behind, hands folded, radiating calm. They were all very beautiful, they all smiled the same smile, and behind each of them, one step back, stood a handler with a clipboard.

Fred did not raise a ticket. It didn't seem like the sort of thing a ticket would fix.

As it turned out, he didn't need one.

On his third night with the caravan, Fred was finishing up at the trench. It was late. The camp had gone quiet, the pavilion's lamps were out, and the stars had come out instead, so thick and bright over the tree line that the sky looked dusty with them. Fred had found that he liked the trench at night. It was dark and cool, the canvas screen hid it from the rest of the caravan, and nobody painted it.

He was rinsing his bucket when the screen moved and the mage came through.

She didn't look like Luna Starweaver. Her midnight-blue robe was hitched up to her knees, her starry hair was pulled back in a rubber band, and she was smoking a thin, crooked cigarette that smelled of cloves. There were dark circles under her eyes. Fred, who had worked nights for eleven years, recognized a professional.

She saw him and stopped. He stood very still, holding the bucket, in case she needed to raise a ticket.

"Oh, thank god," said the mage. "You're not a handler."

"I'm Sanitation Support," said Fred.

"Brenda," she said, and sat down heavily on an upturned crate. "Don't call me Luna back here. Back here I'm Brenda." She blew out smoke. "They won't come back here, the handlers. Too dirty. It's the only place in this whole circus where nobody's selling anything."

The next night she came back, and she brought the elf. The elf's name was Gwen. She came from a suburb on the far side of the capital, from a cul-de-sac with a trampoline. She said she had been cast as an archer purely because she was an elf, and that she had never in her life been able to hit anything with a bow. "The bards always say I do," she said. "Every song. Sylvia's arrow flew true. Sylvia's arrow has never flown true. Sylvia's arrow mostly goes in the river."

The night after that, the rogue came. Her name was Patrice. She pulled off her cat ears the moment she came through the screen and scratched her scalp with both hands and a groan of pure relief. "Glued," she said, when she saw Fred looking. "Contractually required. Clause nine. Nobody reads the contract but me." And the night after that came the priestess, Marge, in her white robes, with ink on her fingers and a stack of Kaito's expense reports under her arm. She worked through them by lantern light while the others talked.

None of them ever raised a ticket. They just came to the trench every night to smoke and complain and tell the truth, and Fred mopped around them and listened. He didn't say much. He found he didn't need to. It was rather like the Quik-Stop at three in the morning. People came in and talked, and he was part of the furniture, and somehow that made it easier for them.

On the fifth night, the bard came too.

He was a thin young man in a feathered cap, with a lute and a sheaf of paper, and he looked so pleased with himself that Brenda groaned out loud.

"Listen to this," said the bard, sitting down on Fred's spare bucket without asking. "Just finished it. It's the big one. The Ballad of the Battle of Grimhollow." He cleared his throat and strummed. "*Then Kaito raised his shining blade, and lo, the demon host did fade...*"

"What Battle of Grimhollow?" said Fred.

The bard stopped strumming. "The Battle of Grimhollow."

"Has it happened?"

"Not yet." The bard looked at him as if he had asked whether water was wet. "It's in the schedule."

"But how do you know what happens?"

The bard held up his papers. On the top page, in gold ink, it said VICTORY (GLORIOUS). Underneath, in smaller letters, it said *Approved by the Bureau of Destiny, Narrative Office.*

"Because I wrote it," he said.

Fred cleaned the Gilded Privy last thing every night, whether it needed it or not. It never needed it. He did it anyway, because the gold leaf caught the dust, and because he liked to end the day with something that shone.

On the sixth night he was on his knees by the velvet seat with his cloth when the crystal door handle turned.

He looked up. Kaito Reklamo stood in the doorway.

Fred had never seen him this close, and never like this. The white armor was gone. He wore a plain gray shirt and soft trousers, and his feet were bare. There was no glow on his face. Up close, without Brenda's sparkles, his skin was a little blotchy, and there was a small, ordinary pimple on his chin. His famous hair was flat on one side, as if he had been lying on it. He looked younger than Fred had thought, and much more tired.

They looked at each other.

Fred waited to be told to leave. He was out of shot, but only just.

Kaito didn't tell him anything. He came in, closed the door behind him, and turned the little brass key in the lock. Then he sat down on the wagon's step with his back against the gold-leaf wall and his arms around his knees, under his own portrait.

Fred went back to polishing.

Neither of them said anything for a long time. Outside, the camp was quiet. A horse shifted somewhere. An owl said something to another owl. Fred worked his way around the jeweled mirror, along the carved columns and around the frame of the portrait, carefully, the way you'd clean around someone sleeping.

At last Kaito said, to nobody in particular, "It's the only door here that locks."

Fred nodded. He didn't ask anything. It didn't seem like the sort of thing that wanted a question.

He kept polishing, and Kaito kept sitting. It was, Fred thought later, the most comfortable silence he'd had in weeks. He suspected it was the same for Kaito, and he was right.

After an hour, Kaito stood, unlocked the door and went out without a word. In the morning he was the Chosen One again, glowing and smiling, with his hair swept up and his armor on, and he didn't look at Fred once. But that night the door handle turned again, and the night after that too.

On the eighth day, the dispatch rider came.

Fred heard him before he saw him: a drumming of hooves on the road from the capital, much faster than any horse had a right to go. A small, lean figure with a dispatch bag came flying into the meadow, hauled his horse to a stop in front of the Bureau office wagon, slapped a sealed packet into a handler's hands, and turned to go. Then he saw Fred and grinned.

It was Thirty Minutes.

He was thinner than he'd been at boot camp, and browner, and there was a new scar along his jaw, but the grin was the same. "Hyphen!" he called. "Hey, Hyphen! How's the Chosen One's toilet?"

"Very shiny," said Fred. "How's the Front?"

Thirty Minutes's grin flickered. "Loud," he said. Then it came back. "You're coming, you know. All of you. Got it right here." He jerked his chin at the packet. "Battle of Grimhollow. Ten days. As scheduled."

"As scheduled," Fred repeated.

"Gotta go. Thirty minutes or it's free." He wheeled his horse around. "See you at the Front, Hyphen!"

And he was gone, back down the road in a thunder of hooves and a spray of mud.

In the Bureau office wagon, the handlers had already broken the seal. Word spread through the caravan the way word does, wagon to wagon. The painters began mixing battle colors. The grooms began polishing armor. The Director of Companion Relations sent round a memo. Brenda swore. Gwen went to look for her bow.

Fred looked at his mop, and then at the orders. Somewhere in the caravan, a bard was tuning his lute for a victory that hadn't happened yet.
