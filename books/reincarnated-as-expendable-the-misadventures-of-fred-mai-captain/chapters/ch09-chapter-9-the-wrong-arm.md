# Chapter 9: The Wrong Arm

Sister Verna Walleye had failed the Healers' Guild eye exam eleven times, and been promoted after every one.

Fred learned this later. When he first woke up, all he knew was the tent.

It was long and low and brown, and it smelled of boiled cloth and lamp oil and something underneath both that he didn't want to name. Cots ran down both sides in two long rows, every cot had someone in it, and most of the someones were making some kind of noise. Gray light came in through the canvas. At the far end, over the entrance, hung a hand-painted sign in cheerful letters: HEALING. Underneath, smaller, it said MIRACLES BY APPOINTMENT. Nobody seemed to be making appointments.

Fred lay still and took stock. He was warm. He was dry. His left side ended in a fat white bandage a little way below the shoulder, and the bandage throbbed in a slow, distant way, like a neighbor's music through a wall.

There was something hard under his pillow. He reached under with his right hand and pulled it out.

It was the ledger, with its black cloth cover singed at one corner and CASUALTIES in gold on the front. Tucked inside the front cover, folded carefully, was a pair of round wire glasses with one lens cracked.

He held them for a long time. He waited to cry, because it seemed like the thing to do, and found that he couldn't. It didn't feel like not caring. It felt more like a shelf that had been emptied too fast, before anyone had time to decide what to put on it instead.

To keep his mind busy, he counted the cots. One, two, three, down the left side. Twenty, twenty-one, back up the right. He was at forty-one before he noticed what he was doing, and whose habit it was. He stopped counting.

"Farmer brought them," said a voice from the next cot. It belonged to a soldier with a bandaged head and a cheerful face. "Your book and those specs. Tucked them under your pillow himself. Said you'd want them. Said not to let the orderlies file them." He grinned. "You're the mop man, aren't you? The one who drained the war?"

Fred put the glasses back inside the ledger and the ledger back under his pillow.

"I'm Fred," he said.

Sister Verna arrived at noon, like weather.

Fred heard her first: a brisk, starched rustle moving up the tent, and a voice saying "Bless you, bless you, bless you" at regular intervals, like a clock. Then she was at the foot of his cot. She was a tall woman in a white habit so starched it could probably have stood up on its own. Over it she wore an apron that had started the day white and was no longer white in any meaningful sense. Her face was serene, smooth and utterly certain. Her eyes were blue and kind. One of them looked at Fred. The other looked, steadily and with great interest, at the tent door.

"Bless you," said Sister Verna Walleye. "The Light has brought you to me."

"Thank you," said Fred, who wasn't sure which eye to answer.

Behind her stood an orderly, a cheerful young man with a tray. While Sister Verna examined Fred's bandage with the eye that was on duty, the orderly leaned in and told Fred about her in a proud whisper, the way you might tell a visitor about a local monument.

Eleven times, he said. Eleven times she had sat the Healers' Guild eye exam, and eleven times she had failed it. And every time, the Guild had promoted her. A healer who failed the eye exam couldn't be put in charge of patients, so the only safe thing to do was put her in charge of the people who were in charge of patients. She was a Healer-Lieutenant now. "There's a wall," said the orderly. "In her office. You should see it."

Fred did see it, later, when he was walking again. Over Sister Verna's desk hung twenty-two frames in two neat rows, alternating: a failed eye exam, a promotion certificate, a failed eye exam, a promotion certificate, all the way along. At the end, a twenty-third frame hung empty, ready.

"Wonderful news," said Sister Verna, straightening. "You qualify."

"For what?"

"For a replacement." She beamed gloriously at a point just to the left of his ear. "The Light provides. Orderly! The bank, please."

The limb bank arrived on a cart.

It was a long, low wooden cart, painted white with a red sun on each side, and it took two orderlies to push it down the aisle between the cots. It was packed with rough gray salt in great heaps, and out of the salt, at every angle, stuck arms and legs. Some were pale, some were brown, and some were green or gray or a mottled purple. Some still had boots on. One still had a glove. They came, the orderly explained cheerfully, from the battlefield, from both sides, because the Light did not discriminate, and anyway it saved paper.

Each limb had a paper tag tied round its wrist or ankle, and on each tag, in a round, careful hand, someone had written a description. LEG (LEFT?), GOOD CONDITION. HAND, LIGHTLY USED. FOOT, SOME TOES. Fred read them because he couldn't stop himself. He had always read labels.

Sister Verna walked along the cart with her hands folded, looking at it with one eye and at the tent roof with the other, humming. Now and then she stopped, touched a limb and murmured, "No. No. Oh, no." Then she stopped, her face lit up, and she reached into the salt with both hands and drew out an arm.

It was enormous. It was a deep, mossy green, with knuckles like walnuts and a forearm as thick as Fred's thigh. On the inside of the forearm, in faded blue ink, was a tattoo: a lopsided heart with a word inside it in blocky capitals. The word was MUM. Its tag said ARM, LARGE, ONE CAREFUL OWNER.

"Perfect," said Sister Verna.

"Is it," said Fred, "a left arm?"

"The Light guides my hand," said Sister Verna serenely. The orderly coughed and looked at the ceiling.

Fred remembered what happened next only in pieces. There was a great deal of golden light, so bright he had to close his eyes. There was chanting, low and fast, in a language he didn't know. There was a smell like a struck match. And there was a sound, very distinct, over and over, exactly like an office stapler. Then the light faded, Sister Verna said, "Bless you," and moved on to the next cot, and Fred opened his eyes and looked down.

The green arm was attached to his left shoulder. The join was a neat pink seam, as if it had always been there. The arm was much too big; it hung down past his knee. When he thought about moving it, it moved, a beat late, as though it had considered the request first.

With a cheerful chime, a small glowing window opened in front of his eyes:

ITEM EQUIPPED: ARM (ORC). COMPATIBILITY: PENDING.

The arm reached up, without being asked, and scratched its own elbow.

Over the next day, it became clear that the arm had opinions.

It had belonged, everyone agreed, to an orc from the demon side. That much was obvious from the color, from the tattoo, and from the arm's general attitude, which was that of a soldier who had been on the other side of a war until very recently and saw no reason to change now.

When an officer came through the tent on rounds, everyone who could saluted. Fred's right hand went up. The green arm stayed exactly where it was, at his side, its fingers drumming slowly on the blanket.

When General Bellweather's aide came round with a clipboard to take statements about the battle, the arm waited until his back was turned and then made a gesture at him. It was a complicated gesture involving three fingers and the thumb, and Fred had never seen it before. But two orcish prisoners on the far side of the tent saw it, gasped, and burst into delighted, scandalized laughter, so Fred assumed it was very rude.

At mealtimes, the arm stole bread. It did it smoothly, without fuss, from the trays of the men on either side. Fred had to give it all back, roll by roll, apologizing.

On the second afternoon the farmer came to visit and, being a farmer, suggested an arm wrestle. The arm put the farmer's hand flat on the crate in under a second, then sat there, palm down on top of it, as if waiting for applause.

Fred tried to be reasonable with it. He was a reasonable man. He lined up his things on the upturned crate beside his cot, the way he liked them: the ledger squared to the edge, Mook's glasses folded on top, his notes and forms stacked beside them, corners together. The arm reached over and nudged the ledger crooked. Fred straightened it. The arm nudged it again. Fred straightened it again. They did this eleven times. On the twelfth, Fred left the ledger straight and let the arm have the pile of forms. It pushed them slightly askew and then, apparently satisfied, left them alone. After that, it was understood between them: one crooked thing. The rest was Fred's.

On the second evening, a stretcher came through the tent carrying a captured demon officer, gray and horned and unconscious, on his way to the prisoners' end. As he passed Fred's cot, the green arm shot upright and snapped off a perfect, crisp, parade-ground salute, so smart that Fred heard the knuckles crack.

The whole tent went quiet.

Very carefully, Fred used his right hand to lower the green one. "It's new," he said, to nobody in particular. "We're still getting to know each other."

That night he tried to write in the ledger. He wanted to add the names of the nine who hadn't come back from the Forlorn Hope, properly, in their own rows, the way Mook would have done. He held the pencil in his right hand, as he always had. But the green arm reached across, took the pencil from his fingers, and wrote one word at the bottom of the page in heavy, blocky runes that slanted backward. Then it gave the pencil back.

Fred looked at the word for a long time. He couldn't read it. Nobody in the tent could, except the orcish prisoners. When he showed it to them, they went very quiet and wouldn't say.

Later that night, he turned over and found something on his pillow that hadn't been there before. He hadn't heard anyone come. It was a small brass key, still warm from somebody's hand: the key to the Gilded Privy. The only door that locks. There was no note. There didn't need to be.

Fred held it for a long time. Then he put it in his shirt pocket, with Mook's glasses and the notes and all the forms nobody had answered.

On the third day, the Forlorn Hope came to visit.

Not all of them; there wasn't room. But the farmer came, and the Boiler Stoker, and the Arrow Retriever, and they stood at the foot of Fred's cot in their mended uniforms looking awkward and pleased, the way people look when they've brought a present and aren't sure about it.

"We chipped in," said the Boiler Stoker, and held out a small wooden box. "All of us."

Inside, folded with great care, was a new pair of socks. They were thick gray wool, and very good. Under them lay a sheet of paper. At the top, someone had written in careful block capitals: TO THE CAPTAIN. FROM THE FORLORN HOPE. THANK YOU FOR OUR FEET. Underneath were signatures, all one hundred and ninety-one of them, crammed into every corner, and quite a lot of them were just an X.

Fred couldn't say anything. He held the socks in his right hand. The green arm reached past him, took the empty box, and tried to eat it.

"Captain," said the farmer, while Fred was wrestling the box away from his own arm, "we meant to say. Thank you."

"I'm not a captain," said Fred, from inside the struggle. "It's a hyphen."

"We know," said the Boiler Stoker. "Captain."

They meant it. That was the strange thing. They weren't making fun. Fred didn't know what to do about that, so he put on the socks.

Thirty Minutes came after they'd gone, at speed, leaning in the tent doorway just long enough to deliver the news. The official account of the Battle of Grimhollow had been approved. It had come down from the Narrative Office that morning. The victory was credited to the Chosen One.

"And there's a song," said Thirty Minutes, and his face did something complicated. "You'll hear it. Everyone's going to hear it. Sorry, Hyphen." And he was gone.

They heard it that evening. A young bard with a feathered cap and a hopeful expression came round the hospital with his lute to cheer the patients. He stood in the middle of the tent and sang the new Ballad of Grimhollow, all nineteen verses. In it, the gallant few charged bravely and were nobly overwhelmed. In it, the Chosen One came down from the ridge in shining armor and raised his holy sword over the field, and the waters rose at his command and swept the demon host away. The chorus went:

*He raised his blade, the waters fled,*

*And every demon soon was... wet!*

The bard paused before "wet" every time, as though it were clever. By the fourth chorus the whole tent was singing along. Fred didn't sing. The green arm, he noticed, didn't either.

Night came down soft on the hospital tent. Somebody turned the lanterns low, so they glowed orange along the aisle like a row of small, tired suns. Somebody at the far end was snoring, deep and steady. The orcish prisoners murmured to each other in their own language. And from across the camp, faint through the canvas, the bards were still at it, the new song drifting over again and again: *and every demon soon was... wet.*

Fred lay on his back with the ledger on his chest, the new socks on his feet, and the green arm lying on the blanket beside him like a large, sleeping dog. He thought about Mook. He thought about the nine names, and the key in his pocket, and a box signed with a hundred and ninety-one names and a great many X's. He still didn't cry. He still didn't know why not.

In the dark, the orc arm reached over, all by itself, and patted him twice on the shoulder. Fred decided not to ask whose idea that had been.
