# Chapter 8: The Battle

At 09:40 exactly, the Forlorn Hope went over the top, and the schedule went with it.

For the first thirty seconds, it looked almost like the board. Two hundred people climbed out of the trench in a long, ragged line and started forward across the valley floor. Up on the ridge somebody blew a bugle, and the painters leaned toward their easels. Then the mud took hold.

It was deeper out here than it had looked from the ridge. It came up over boots, then over ankles, then over knees. It held on with a cold, patient grip, so that every step was a negotiation. The neat line Fred had made in the trench lasted perhaps a minute. Then it was a crowd, then a scatter, then just people, each fighting the mud alone, with the smoke rolling over them and the noise pressing in from every side.

The noise was the thing nobody had warned him about. It wasn't one sound but a hundred at once: the drums from the far ridge, the bugles from the near one, shouting, splashing, the crack of something breaking, a horse screaming somewhere off to the right. It filled Fred's head so completely that there was no room left in it to be frightened. He found this useful.

"Keep left!" he shouted, because he had to shout something. "Stay together! Mind the..."

The first arrows came down like rain on a tin roof, hissing into the mud all around. Somebody near Fred went down without a sound. Somebody else stopped to help and was hauled onward by the farmer, who had a grip like a vise. The Arrow Retriever, with his empty quiver, looked at the arrows sticking out of the mud all around him, laughed once, high and strange, and kept going.

Ahead, the black banners were close now. Fred could see the demons beneath them: a long line of dark shapes behind a low wall of sharpened stakes, waiting. They didn't seem to be in any hurry. They didn't need to be.

Then the ground fell away under Fred's feet, and he slid down a muddy bank into a hollow, and half the Forlorn Hope slid down after him.

It was the old valley road. Fred hadn't seen it from the ridge. It ran straight across the valley floor, sunk a few feet below the fields on either side, worn into a ditch by centuries of carts and boots. It was full of water to the shins, but its banks were high enough to keep the arrows off. In a moment the whole company was pressed against the near bank, breathing hard, while arrows hissed overhead and thudded into the far one.

Fred counted. It was a habit. One hundred and ninety-one. He counted again, in case. One hundred and ninety-one.

They were pinned. They couldn't go forward, and they couldn't go back. He looked at the sky. It seemed to be about a quarter to ten. Seven minutes until the Chosen One. Fred did the arithmetic again and liked it no better.

He saw the runner before anyone else did, because he was looking back, the way you look back at a shop door when you're waiting for the manager.

A small figure was coming down off the near ridge at a speed that didn't look possible, straight across the open ground where the arrows fell thickest. He didn't zigzag. He didn't duck. He simply ran, flat out, his dispatch bag bouncing on his hip and his light boots hardly seeming to touch the mud, and the arrows fell behind him, always a step too late, as if they couldn't keep up. He ran the way he must once have delivered pizzas: as if somebody somewhere were timing him, and the order would be free if he was late.

He came over the lip of the road in one long leap and landed in the water next to Fred with a splash.

"Thirty Minutes," said Fred.

"Hyphen." Thirty Minutes was gasping, bent double, hands on his knees. "Message. For you. Not official." He pulled a folded scrap of paper from inside his jacket. It wasn't Bureau paper, or any kind of official paper. It looked like the back of a page torn from a speech. On it, in fast, messy handwriting, were four words:

*Hold on. I'm trying.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be.

"He grabbed me," said Thirty Minutes, still panting. "On the stand. Behind the painters. The handlers didn't see. He said run. So I ran." He straightened up, peered over the bank at the black banners, and ducked back down as an arrow went past his ear. "He's trying to get them to let him go early. They won't. It's not in the schedule."

Fred folded the note and put it in his shirt pocket with the others.

Thirty Minutes was looking around the sunken road. Then he was looking along it, to the left, where it ran toward the lowest part of the valley, and his face changed.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, I know this bit."

"What bit?"

"This road. I run it every day, dispatches up and down." He pointed. A little way along, the road crossed a low stone arch, half buried, where a stream had once run under it. Above the arch, on the uphill side, Fred could see a long, dull sheet of standing water stretching away into the smoke. It was water from every ditch and trench and churned field on the near side of the valley, all of it backed up behind the road like a bath behind a plug. "That's the old culvert. The whole valley floods every spring because of it. All the water comes down off the ridge and gets stuck right there." He shook his head. "It's blocked. Been blocked forever. Nobody ever cleans it."

Fred looked at the culvert.

He looked at it for a long time. He looked at the low stone arch, and the water pressing against it, and the black banners on the other side, where the ground fell away toward the demon trenches. He looked at it the way he had once looked at aisle 4, at the latrines at boot camp, at the trench behind the caravan.

He looked down at his mop.

"Nobody ever cleans it," he said.

"Everybody up the bank," said Fred. "That bank. The high one. Right up, as high as you can get, and hold on to something."

"The enemy's that way," said the farmer.

"I know," said Fred. "Mind the step."

They went. Fred didn't know whether it was because he was a captain, or because they had dry feet, or because there was nothing else to do. But they went, all one hundred and ninety-one of them, scrambling up the uphill bank of the old road into the gorse and the rocks. The arrows thinned out, and the demons, seeing the Forlorn Hope apparently retreating sideways, began to cheer.

Fred went the other way. He waded down the sunken road to the culvert, with his rusty sword in his belt and his mop in both hands, and ducked under the arch.

It was dark under there, and it smelled the way the chute had smelled on his first morning. And it was full. Water dripped from the stones overhead in a slow, patient rhythm, like a clock that had been counting down for eighty years.

It was full the way a drain is full when nobody has touched it for a very long time. There were broken spears, dozens of them, jammed crosswise. There were old banners from both sides, rotted to rags: the gold sunrise of Fekfosio tangled up with demon black, so old that both had gone the same gray. There was a demon helmet with one horn. There was a cartwheel. There was, inexplicably, a mattress. And wedged among it all, soggy and brown, was a sheet of paper. Fred could just read the gold ink at the top: THE BALLAD OF THE BATTLE OF GRIMHOLLOW. Underneath, in smaller letters, it said VICTORY (GLORIOUS). It wasn't this year's. The date at the bottom was eighty years ago.

He set to work.

He had done this before, he told himself. Not this exactly, but this kind of thing. The men's room at the Quik-Stop, the night of the chili festival. The latrine block at boot camp. You found the end of the blockage. You got your tool behind it. You pulled steadily, not too hard, and you didn't stop. He hooked the head of the mop behind the first spear and pulled. The spear came, and the next one shifted.

With a cheerful chime, a small glowing window opened in front of his eyes:

LATRINE SKILL INCREASED: LEVEL 3 (PENDING APPROVAL)

He blinked it away and pulled again.

He was so busy that he didn't see the demon come down the road behind him until it was already under the arch. It was big and gray-skinned and horned, splashing through the water with an axe held high, and it looked, Fred thought later, about as surprised to find a man with a mop in a drain as he was to see it. For a moment they simply looked at each other. Then the axe came down, and Fred turned away from it without thinking, the way you turn away from a dropped tray, and felt something like a hard shove at his left side. He staggered, caught himself on the mop, and went on pulling, because he was nearly through and he had always finished a job.

The mattress gave way.

It went all at once, with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle the size of a mountain. The whole jam of spears and banners and helmets and eighty years of ballads lurched, shuddered and shot out of the downhill end of the culvert. And behind it, with a deep, rising roar that Fred felt in his teeth, came the water.

All of it. Every ditch and trench and puddle and churned field on the near side of Grimhollow, held back behind that road since spring, came through the culvert at once. Fred was knocked flat against the wall of the arch, holding on to his mop, while the valley poured past him. It burst out of the far end in a brown wave as tall as a house. It raced down the slope. It went over the stakes and into the demon trenches and through them, and Fred, half drowned and clinging to the wall, heard the demons' cheering turn into something else entirely.

When the roar slowed to a rush, and the rush to a gurgle, Fred crawled out of the culvert and looked.

The valley floor below the road was a lake: brown, churning and steaming, with spears and banners and helmets turning slowly on its surface. Of the demon army there was nothing to see but a few black banners far off, bobbing away downstream. A demon war drum floated past, upside down, still giving a faint thump each time the current knocked it against a stake. Somewhere in the distance, growing fainter, a great many demons were shouting at each other in a tone Fred recognized from every staff meeting he had ever attended.

Up on the high bank, one hundred and ninety-one people from the Forlorn Hope stood in the gorse, soaking wet, staring.

Somewhere up on the ridge, a bell struck. Fred's head was ringing too much to count it, but it was, he worked out later, 09:51.

At 09:52 exactly, the Chosen One arrived.

He came over the near ridge at full gallop on a white horse, his white armor blazing, his sword held high, Brenda's sparkles streaming behind him like a comet's tail. The bugles sounded. The four companions rode at his back in a perfect wedge. It was, Fred had to admit, magnificent. It was exactly on schedule.

He galloped down the slope, across the mud, all the way to the edge of the old road, and stopped, because there was no enemy. There was a lake.

The Chosen One sat on his white horse at the water's edge, sword raised, looking at nothing. The sparkles drifted down around him and fizzled out on the brown water.

There was a scramble on the ridge. Fred could see it even from where he sat: handlers running, clipboards waving, a painter gesturing furiously at his easel. A rider galloped down after Kaito with fresh instructions. After a moment, Kaito got down from his horse, climbed onto a rock at the water's edge, and raised his sword over the flood in the pose from the paintings. Foot on the rock. Gaze into the middle distance. Wind in the hair. The water lapped at his boots. Somewhere behind him, a handler was whispering "Chin up, chin up," over and over, like a prayer.

On the ridge, the painters began to paint.

Behind the painters, the bards were in a huddle. Fred learned later what they were arguing about. The ballad said "a gallant few," which rhymed with "true." But the Forlorn Hope had lost nine people at Grimhollow, not a hundred and ninety, and a hundred and ninety-one survivors didn't rhyme with anything. "It doesn't scan," said the senior bard, in real distress. "You can't have a gallant hundred and ninety-one. Nobody would sing it."

On his rock, sword in the air, Kaito turned his head, only a little, so the painters wouldn't notice. His eyes went along the bank, past the gorse and the Forlorn Hope, and found Fred, sitting in the mud by the mouth of the culvert with his back against the stone and his mop across his knees.

It was only then, following Kaito's eyes, that Fred looked down at himself and noticed that his left arm wasn't where he had left it.

There was a great deal of red on his tunic, and the sleeve ended where it shouldn't. It didn't hurt. That seemed wrong, and he thought vaguely that it would probably start hurting soon, and that he ought to find somebody about it. He looked back up at Kaito. Kaito was still looking at him, and for once, in the middle of a portrait, the Chosen One wasn't smiling at all.

With a cheerful chime, a small glowing window opened in front of Fred's eyes:

ACHIEVEMENT UNLOCKED: UNCLOGGED A WAR (PENDING APPROVAL)

Then the farmer and the Boiler Stoker were sliding down the bank toward him, shouting for a medic, and the window and Kaito and the lake all went soft and gray at the edges for a while.

At 10:15, the victory display began.

It was on the board, after all: VICTORY (GLORIOUS). Nobody on the ridge saw any reason to change it. The decorative artillery had been loaded since dawn with the special shells, gold and white and blue, and at a quarter past ten, on the general's signal, it fired them all.

Fred saw it from a stretcher halfway up the near slope. He had not wanted the stretcher and had been put on it anyway. The sky over Grimhollow filled with color. Great gold flowers opened above the lake and hung there, white ones burst inside them, and blue rain fell through the white. The colors shone back up from the brown lake, so that for a moment the flood looked almost beautiful, which was, Fred supposed, the point. The nobles on the viewing stand stood and cheered, and the band struck up. Down on his rock, the Chosen One, still posing, was lit gold from above, which the painters agreed afterward was a gift.

One of the shells didn't go up.

It left the gun with a cough instead of a bang and rose in a short, lazy arc, nowhere near the sky. Fred watched it without much interest, the way you watch a ball that's going to land somewhere else. It came down at the bottom of the ridge, a little apart from the rest of the camp, on a long brown tent with a neat hand-lettered sign.

It went off. Gold and white and blue, very pretty, at ground level.

Fred got off the stretcher.

People tried to stop him. He let them try, and then he went anyway, down the slope, leaning on his mop with his one hand like a walking stick, because it was the only thing he had that was any use. By the time he reached the bottom, the fire was out. The tent was mostly gone. The sign lay face up in the mud: CASUALTY OFFICE. The chalk underneath had been washed away.

They had already taken Mook away by the time Fred got there. One of the orderlies, an older man with kind eyes, told him it had been quick. Mook had been at his desk, he said, and wouldn't have known anything about it. Fred nodded. He didn't know whether to believe it. He decided to.

What was left of the folding table lay on its side in the ashes. Under it, in the mud, Fred found Mook's glasses, round and wire-framed, with one lens cracked. Fred wiped the good lens clean on his shirt, out of habit, and put them in his pocket with the notes. He found the pencils, all of them, scattered and blackened, the row that had been laid out so neatly the morning before. And he found the ledger.

It had fallen face down into a puddle, which was why it hadn't burned. The black cloth cover was singed at one corner, but the gold letters still said CASUALTIES. Fred picked it up with his one hand, sat down carefully on the overturned table, balanced the book on his knee and opened it.

The pages Mook had ruled the morning before were still there, columns straight. And they weren't empty anymore. At some point in the night, in his tiny, neat handwriting, Mook had filled them in. He had written out the names of the Forlorn Hope, all two hundred of them, in order, with their units, in case. The farmer was there. The Arrow Retriever. The Boiler Stoker. Fred was there, near the top, with the hyphen written very small.

Mook had left the CAUSE column blank for every one of them. He hadn't crossed any of them out.

Fred sat for a long time with the ledger on his knee. Then he turned to the next clean page, ran his finger down to the first empty row, and picked up one of Mook's pencils from the ashes. It was a stub, black at one end. It would do.

Fred wrote it as neatly as he could with the wrong hand: MOREOVER, M. — CASUALTY OFFICE — CAUSE: VICTORY.
