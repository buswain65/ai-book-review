# Chapter 4: Boot Camp

Sergeant Gangrene Gruk was four feet tall, and every inch of him was shouting.

He came out of the fog at dawn like a small, furious weather front. Batch #4712 had been standing in the mud of the parade ground since before first light, in rows, as instructed by a corporal who had then gone back to bed. The camp lay around them in the gray. There were long, low huts with tar-paper roofs, a latrine block steaming gently at the far end, and an obstacle course of beams, ropes and pits that looked like a gallows designed by a committee. Over everything hung the smell of wet wool and old porridge. A crow sat on the flagpole and watched them with the air of a bird that had seen a great many batches, and eaten parts of several.

Then Gruk arrived, and nobody looked at the crow again.

He was a goblin. Fred had never seen one before, but there was no mistaking it. He was green where he wasn't scarred, and he was mostly scarred. His tusks had been filed to points. One ear was missing, and the other looked as if it had lost an argument with the first. He wore a sergeant's jacket with the sleeves cut off, and he walked along the front row with his hands behind his back, looking up into each face as though it had personally offended him.

"RECRUITS," he said. It was less a word than a weather event. "YOU ARE IN THE HOLY KINGDOM OF FEKFOSIO. YOU ARE HEROES. YOU ARE CHOSEN. YOU ARE ALSO, AS OF THIS MORNING, MINE."

Nobody said anything. It seemed safest.

"I AM SERGEANT GRUK. YOU WILL CALL ME SERGEANT. YOU WILL NOT CALL ME ANYTHING ELSE. IF YOU HEAR ANYONE CALL ME ANYTHING ELSE, YOU WILL FORGET IT IMMEDIATELY. IS THAT CLEAR?"

"Yes, Sergeant," said Batch #4712, more or less together.

"ROLL CALL."

He had a list, and he read it the way other people wring a chicken's neck. When he reached the pizza courier, he looked him up and down and saw the insulated bag, which the courier had somehow kept through a chute, a pool and a hose.

"WHAT'S IN THE BAG?"

"Nothing, Sergeant. It's a pizza bag. Thirty minutes or it's free, Sergeant."

"THEN YOU'RE RECRUIT THIRTY MINUTES. THAT'S HOW LONG YOU'LL LAST AT THE FRONT."

The salaryman, who was still holding his laptop, became Recruit Spreadsheet before he had finished opening his mouth.

Then Gruk came to Fred. He looked at the list. He looked at Fred. He looked at the list again, and his lip drew back from one filed tusk.

"MAI," he read. "HYPHEN." He stopped.

The whole batch waited. Even the crow leaned forward.

"I DON'T SAY THAT WORD TO A RECRUIT," said Gruk at last. It was very quiet for him, which was still quite loud. "I HAVE NEVER SAID THAT WORD TO A RECRUIT, AND I AM NOT STARTING WITH YOU. YOU ARE RECRUIT HYPHEN. UNDERSTOOD?"

"Yes, Sergeant," said Fred. He found that he didn't mind. It was the first nickname anyone had ever given him, and it was, at least, accurate.

Gruk did not move on. He stepped closer, until the tip of one tusk was level with the second button of Fred's polo shirt, and looked up at him with small, yellow, unblinking eyes.

"I heard about you," he said. "I heard they had to salute you up at the Hall. I heard there was a whole fuss."

"It was a typo, Sergeant."

"IT WAS A TYPO," Gruk agreed. "AND I'LL TELL YOU SOMETHING, HYPHEN. I'LL TELL YOU FOR FREE. YOU'LL MAKE CAPTAIN..." He paused, savoring it. "...WHEN DEMONS FLY."

The batch laughed. It was the nervous laugh of people who have worked out who isn't going to be shouted at for the next few seconds, and Fred didn't hold it against them. He even smiled a little himself. It was quite a good line.

Gruk moved on down the row. When he was out of earshot, the recruit on Fred's left, a thin young man with a farmer's tan, leaned over and whispered without moving his lips.

"Know how he got the name? Gangrene?"

"No," Fred whispered back.

"Lost a leg to it. In the war. Grew it back out of spite."

Fred looked down at Gruk's legs. There were two of them, both short and bowed and very much there.

"Which one?" he whispered.

"Nobody knows," said the farmer darkly. "That's the point."

Kit was issued from the quartermaster's store, a stone building at the edge of the parade ground. From the outside it looked like a barn. From the inside it looked like the stomach of something that had swallowed a hardware shop. Shelves ran up into the dark in every direction, holding helmets, ropes, saddles, kettles, a stuffed owl, three harps, a great many buckets and one small cannon. Nothing was labeled. Everything, Fred noticed, had a price tag.

Behind the counter stood Quartermaster Bilge Reprobate. He was a broad, soft, comfortable man, as wide as the counter he leaned on, and he gave off the warm, slightly damp friendliness of a fresh loaf. He wore a ring on every finger, thumbs included, and an apron with so many pockets that Fred lost count at forty. Every pocket was full, and every pocket clinked.

"Welcome, welcome, lads and lasses," he said. "Line up nice. Standard issue coming right up."

Standard issue was a gray tunic, a belt, a rusty sword and a bucket. Fred took his and said thank you, because it was free, and free things deserved thanks.

"And those," said Bilge, pointing a ringed finger at Fred's feet.

Fred looked down. He was still wearing his work sneakers, the black ones with non-slip soles that he'd bought for the Quik-Stop, now gray with mud.

"Non-regulation," said Bilge sadly. "Can't have it. Off they come, lad."

Fred took them off and handed them over. Bilge put them carefully in a sack behind the counter, and Fred went to the back of the line in his socks. It was not a short line, and it moved slowly. When Fred reached the counter again, about ten minutes later, to ask about boots, there was a new display on a shelf behind Bilge's head, lit by its own candle. On it sat a pair of black sneakers with non-slip soles, recently wiped. A little card in front of them read:

EARTH-PATTERN BOOTS. RARE IMPORT. ONE MONTH'S PAY.

"Those are my shoes," said Fred.

"Those are Earth-pattern boots," said Bilge warmly. "Very rare. Very comfortable, I'm told. Non-slip." He leaned across the counter, and his pockets clinked. "Now, standard issue's free, lad. Boots that fit are extra."

Fred thought about the mud of the parade ground, and about however many weeks of marching in his socks. He signed the chit. A month's pay went into one of Bilge's pockets, which clinked, and his shoes came back to him. They had been warmed by the candle. He was oddly grateful for that, and then annoyed with himself for being grateful.

Hut 9 held twenty bunks, twenty footlockers, one stove that didn't light and a roof that leaked in four places, each with a bucket under it. Fred's bunk was the bottom one by the third bucket. The bunk next to his already had someone sitting on it.

He was a slight man, older than most of the batch, with thinning hair and round glasses. He wore his issued tunic over a brown knitted cardigan, as if he'd been told to change and had decided to compromise. On his knees lay a ledger, and he was writing in it with a pencil, very small and very neat, moving his lips as he went.

"...and nineteen," he murmured. "And twenty. Moreover, that makes..."

Fred knew the voice. He had heard it once before, in the departures lounge, counting heads.

"Excuse me," he said. "Were you upstairs? Counting?"

The man looked up and blinked. Then his whole face crinkled into a smile. "You heard that? Oh, how embarrassing. Habit. I count things. I used to be an accountant." He held out a hand. "Mook. Mook Moreover."

"Fred." Fred shook it. "Mai-Captain. But you can say Hyphen. Everybody else does."

"I'll say Fred," said Mook, "if it's all the same to you. Moreover, I think Hyphen is undignified." He closed the ledger on his pencil. "What did you get? For a class?"

"Latrine Paladin."

Mook nodded seriously, as if this were a perfectly good class with a pension. "Tally Clerk," he said. "Casualties."

"What does that mean?"

"I count the dead." Mook said it brightly, the way another man might say he did the books for a garden center. "After battles. It's quite important, apparently. Somebody has to keep the numbers straight, or nobody knows how many more to order."

Fred didn't know what to say to that, so he looked around the hut. Somebody had dropped their boots at the end of the row, and somebody else had kicked them, and now eleven pairs lay about the floor at various angles, like the aftermath of a small explosion in a shoe shop. Without really thinking about it, Fred got up and began to put them in a line. Heels against the wall. Toes to the edge of the floorboard. Pairs together. Left on the left.

When he finished and turned around, Mook was watching him with his pencil stopped in midair. Fred had seen that expression once before, on a man in a gold suit looking at a shelf of soup.

"Oh," said Mook softly. "Oh, that's lovely."

It was, Fred thought later, the moment they became friends.

The rest of the day was training. In the Holy Kingdom of Fekfosio, training turned out to involve a great deal of standing in lines, which Fred was good at, and a great deal of shouting, which he was not.

They marched. They marched up the parade ground and down it and up it again, while Gruk walked alongside them shouting the time, which never seemed to be the same time twice. They were given wooden swords and taught three moves: Thrust, Parry and Look Brave. They were sent over the obstacle course. Recruit Thirty Minutes went over it faster than anyone in the history of the camp. Gruk stood at the finish with his stopwatch and said nothing for a long time. Then he said, "THE FRONT'S GOING TO LOVE YOU," in a voice that made it sound like a threat.

After lunch, which was gray, came a class called Heroic Posing, taught by the schoolteacher who had become a Drill Assistant. She was quite good at it. She stood them in rows and taught them to put one foot on a rock and gaze into the middle distance with the wind in their hair. There was no rock and no wind, but they were told to imagine both. Recruit Spreadsheet asked what the posing was for. The Drill Assistant said it was for the paintings. Recruit Spreadsheet asked which paintings. The Drill Assistant said the paintings of the Chosen Ones, in case the Chosen Ones were busy and somebody had to stand in.

Fred missed most of Heroic Posing. After lunch Gruk had stopped in front of him, looked up at him with deep and personal contempt, and said, "LATRINE PALADIN. LATRINE DUTY. UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE."

"Yes, Sergeant," said Fred, and went.

The latrine block was everything a latrine block in a training camp can be, and it had been that for a very long time. Fred stood in the doorway with his bucket and his brush and looked at it the way he had once looked at aisle 4.

Then he rolled up his sleeves.

He did it properly. He didn't know how to do it any other way. He scrubbed and rinsed and scraped. Under a loose board he found a scrubbing brush that had been lost so long it had grown moss, and he cleaned the brush too. He also found, without looking, four socks, which he washed and hung on a nail by the door in case anyone came back for them. At some point a small glowing window opened in front of his eyes with a cheerful chime:

SKILL PROGRESS: LATRINE 1 → 2 (PENDING APPROVAL)

Fred waved it away like a fly and kept going.

At six o'clock, Gruk came to inspect. He stopped in the doorway. He looked at the floor, which was the color of a floor. He looked at the walls, which had turned out to be white. He sniffed, and something passed over his scarred green face that Fred thought, for a moment, might be awe. Then it hardened into suspicion.

"WHAT DID YOU DO?" he said.

"I cleaned it, Sergeant."

Gruk walked all the way around the latrine block, slowly, looking for the trick. He did not find one. At last he stopped in front of Fred again.

"NOBODY," he said, "CLEANS IT."

"Sorry, Sergeant."

Gruk looked at him a while longer. Then he turned and walked away without another word, and Fred heard him muttering all the way across the parade ground.

By dinner, the story of the gangrene had changed. It had been his own leg, the farmer said, and he had cut it off himself, with a spoon. By lights-out, it had been somebody else's leg. Nobody would say whose.

Night came down on the camp early and wet. The rain found all four holes in the roof of Hut 9 and played on the four buckets in four different notes, like a small, sad band. Somebody at the far end was snoring. Somebody else was crying very quietly into a blanket, and everyone was kind enough to pretend not to hear. The stove didn't light. The only light in the hut was a candle stub on the footlocker between Fred's bunk and Mook's, and by it Mook was doing his sums.

Fred lay on his back and listened to the rain. His shoulders ached from scrubbing, and his feet, in their rare imported boots, were warm. In his mind, without meaning to, he lined up the hut's boots again: heels to the wall, toes to the line, left on the left. It helped.

Mook's pencil stopped scratching.

"Moreover," Mook whispered in the dark, "by my figures, we've got about six weeks."

"Until graduation?" said Fred.

There was a pause while Mook checked his ledger.

"Until something."
