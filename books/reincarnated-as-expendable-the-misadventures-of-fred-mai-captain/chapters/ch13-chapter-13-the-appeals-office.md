# Chapter 13: The Appeals Office

In four hundred years, the Appeals Office of the Bureau of Destiny had never upheld an appeal, though it had, on several occasions, come close.

Fred knew this because Spreadsheet told him on the first of the seven days. Spreadsheet also told him what time the Appeals Office opened, which forms he would need, and which of those forms would need other forms. Then he cleared a corner of his desk at Central Supply, and the two of them got to work.

They spent the week getting Fred's paperwork in order. It was the happiest week Fred had spent in Fekfosio. Everything went into one folder, a good stiff gray one that Spreadsheet found in a cabinet nobody had opened since the last century. In went Mook's ledger, with its two hundred names and its eleven pages of map. In went the original roster of the Forlorn Hope, still damp at one corner. In went Kaito's note, *Hold on. I'm trying.* In went Clause 412 in Patrice's tiny handwriting, the rejected Form 9-D, and Fred's own death certificate: CAUSE OF DEATH: GALLANTRY. Spreadsheet labeled the folder in his best clerk's hand: APPEAL OF MAI-CAPTAIN, F. (ALL MATERIALS). Fred squared the corners.

On the seventh morning, he went up.

Every sign to the Appeals Office pointed down. Fred had expected that. The clerk at the Holding Office had warned him, and he had his own map now. He ignored the signs, followed the map, and climbed. He climbed for most of the morning, up stairs and ramps and ladders and one rope, past departments that grew quieter and dustier the higher he went, until at last, at the very top of the building, he came out into a long, high room full of light.

It took him a moment to understand what was different. Then he saw: there were windows.

Real ones. Tall, arched windows ran down one whole side of the room, and through them, for the first time since he had entered the capital, Fred could see the sky from indoors. It was blue and enormous. Clouds moved across it. The light came in long gold slants across the floor.

Nobody was looking at it. The clerks who worked in the anteroom, at a row of high desks along the opposite wall, all wore small leather blinkers fixed to the sides of their spectacles, like carriage horses, so that they could only see straight ahead. Over the windows hung a neat painted sign: PLEASE DO NOT LOOK OUTSIDE. IT ENCOURAGES APPEALS.

Fred looked outside.

He couldn't help it. From up here he could see the whole of the Holy Kingdom spread out below: the hill of roofs and spires, the green and brown patchwork of farms, the long gray scar of the road down to Grimhollow. And far away, on the very edge of the world, ran a low black ridge the color of a bruise. The Demon Realm. Fred had never seen it before, and he looked at it for a long time. Smoke was rising from the chimneys of the capital below him, gray and slow, into the clear sky. Far away, smoke was rising from the black ridge too. It was the same gray, and it rose the same slow way. If you hadn't known which side was which, you couldn't have told them apart.

"Excuse me," said one of the blinkered clerks, without turning his head. "You're looking."

"Sorry," said Fred, and joined the queue.

It was a long queue, and almost everyone in it was appealing a class. There was an Arrow Retriever who wanted to be an Archer. There was a Field Surgeon (Probationary) who wanted to be just a barber again. And halfway along, sitting on a bench with her hands in her lap and soot still in the creases of her knuckles, was the Boiler Stoker.

She smiled when she saw him. She looked tired.

"Fire magic," she said, before he could ask. "I'm appealing for fire magic. Still." She looked down at her hands. "It's silly."

"It's not silly," said Fred.

She was quiet for a while. "The thing is," she said at last, "I've started to like the boilers. Isn't that strange? They're warm. And when they go out, the whole building gets cold, and people come and find me." She turned her hands over. "Nobody ever needed me before. Not on Earth. Not anywhere."

When her turn came, her appeal was denied. Fred watched her come out of the Appeals Officer's room, and she didn't look disappointed. She looked relieved. She waved to him on her way to the stairs, back down to her boilers, and he waved back.

The Appeals Officer was the oldest man Fred had ever seen.

He sat at a large desk in a small, warm room: a tiny, dry, bright-eyed figure in a robe so old that its gray had gone almost silver. He had a beard down to his belt and spectacles on the end of his nose, and he looked at Fred over them with enormous, delighted interest, as if Fred were the first visitor he'd had in a century. For all Fred knew, he was.

The walls were lined with small brass plaques, polished until they shone. Fred, who always read labels, read them. NEARLY UPHELD, 1714. NEARLY UPHELD, 1802. NEARLY UPHELD, 1846. And in the place of honor over the desk, slightly larger than the rest: VERY NEARLY UPHELD, 1893.

"A difficult case," said the Appeals Officer, following his gaze, with quiet pride. "A woman who wanted to be a bard. Very nearly. I had the stamp in my hand." He sighed happily. "Sit, sit. Let's see what you've got."

Fred sat and handed him the gray folder. The old man opened it with real reverence and read every page, slowly, all the way through, nodding and murmuring, now and then saying "Ah," or "Hm," or once, at the death certificate, "Oh, very good." It took almost an hour. Fred didn't mind. Nobody had ever read anything of his so carefully.

At last the old man closed the folder and folded his hands on top of it.

"Your status," he said, "is a very interesting knot. I shall enjoy it. But your class, Mr. Mai-Captain..." He shook his head gently. "I'm afraid I can tell you about your class right now. It wasn't a mistake."

"It wasn't?"

"Oh, no. Classes are never mistakes. They're assigned by need." He smiled kindly over his spectacles. "Sanitation was short that week."

Fred thought about that. He thought about the orb, and the window in the air, and the whole hall reading LATRINE PALADIN.

"So it was never going to be anything else," he said.

"Not that week," said the old man. "Now. To be thorough, I really ought to consult your full file." He looked up at the ceiling, and his eyes twinkled. "It's kept upstairs. Nobody ever goes upstairs. We'll take a recess for lunch." He rang a little bell on his desk, and a blinkered clerk came and showed Fred out.

Fred sat on the bench in the corridor with Spreadsheet's folder on his knees and waited, because waiting was a thing he was good at. After a while he noticed that he was not waiting anymore. He was looking at a door.

It was a plain door at the end of the corridor, painted the same tired green as the walls, with STAFF ONLY on it in white letters. Nothing about it was interesting. That was what was interesting about it. Fred had the feeling he used to get at the Quik-Stop at three in the morning, when a customer said they had lost a glove, and he knew before he even looked that it was under the second shelf of the cereal aisle.

His skill had changed. It did not only find socks anymore. It found the other copy. And somewhere behind that door, something was missing its other half.

The arm was already reaching for the handle. Fred let it.

Behind the door was a small dark room with a row of chairs and a long window. Through the window was a brighter room, where eight townspeople sat around a table with cups of tea and a plate of biscuits. A clerk with a clipboard stood at the head of the table, holding up painted portraits one at a time. The townspeople could not see Fred. Fred could see them very well. On their side, he realized, the window was a mirror.

"Number six," said the clerk, and held up a portrait of a young man with a square jaw and a sword.

"Too honest-looking," said a woman in a bonnet.

"This one looks like he'd ask questions," said a man with a pipe.

"Can we see him with a dragon?" said someone at the end.

The clerk made a note. She held up number seven: the same jaw, the same sword, a slightly braver nose. The man with the pipe nodded slowly. The woman in the bonnet took a biscuit. Fred's left arm reached toward the plate, glass or no glass, and Fred held it down.

On an easel behind the clerk stood a board headed SLOGAN TESTING. It had three lines on it, with a number after each:

DESTINY CHOSE ME. I CHOSE YOU. — 81

YOUR STORY STARTS WITH A TRUCK! — 60

GET IN THE CHUTE! — 12

Fred looked at the last one for a while. He had got in the chute. Nobody had asked him how he felt about it. Somebody, it turned out, had asked eight people with biscuits.

Along the back wall of the dark room ran a row of filing drawers. Fred's skill pulled at him like a dog on a lead, and he went along the drawers and opened the fifth one without quite deciding to. Inside lay a fat folder tied with ribbon: BATCH 4712 — SELECTION.

He untied the ribbon.

The first card had a sketch of Kaito pinned to it, drawn in confident ink from slightly below, the way people draw statues.

CANDIDATE: REKLAMO, KAITO. RATING: 94%. CHIN: HEROIC. HAIR: ASPIRATIONAL. VOICE: TOO SAD — FIX WITH SCRIPT.

Fred read the last part twice. He thought of the young man with the tired eyes walking toward his golden arch, and of how Kaito had never once, in all those speeches, sounded like himself. Somebody had fixed it.

He leafed on. The courier: RATING: 41%. LEGS: GOOD. Spreadsheet: RATING: 22%. POSTURE: CLERICAL. Then, near the back, a card with a pencil sketch that looked like nobody in particular. It took Fred a moment to recognize the nose.

CANDIDATE 4712-0381 (SANITATION). RATING: 3%. FACE: FORGETTABLE. RECOMMEND: BACKGROUND.

Fred studied it with interest. He had never had a rating before. Three percent seemed fair. And somebody had, after all, recommended him for something.

"It's a good likeness," he told the arm. The arm did not comment.

He tied the ribbon back exactly as he had found it, because that was polite, and put the folder back in its drawer. On the other side of the glass, the woman in the bonnet said, "Could he have a cape?" and everybody wrote it down.

Behind a curtain at the back of the dark room, a narrow iron stair went up in a tight spiral. A sign at the bottom said NARRATIVE OFFICE. AUTHORIZED STORYTELLERS ONLY. Fred was not an authorized storyteller. But he was carrying a folder, and he had learned in the capital that a man carrying a folder can go nearly anywhere, as long as he looks slightly worried. Fred looked slightly worried. It came naturally.

He climbed. Round and round, up and up, until his legs ached and the noise of the building fell away below him. At the top was a round room at the very peak of a tower, with windows on every side and the gray sky pressing against all of them. It was full of bards.

They sat in rows at high desks, scratching away with quill pens, and every one of them looked tired in the same particular way, like people who have been told to be inspired by Friday. Lutes hung on pegs along the walls. Nobody was playing them. A small fire in the grate was being fed, slowly, with crossed-out verses.

Around the curved wall ran an enormous painted calendar. It was ten years long. Every month had a battle on it in neat gold capitals, with a little note in brackets underneath:

THE SIEGE OF PALLID WELLS (GLORIOUS, HEAVY LOSSES).

THE RIVER CROSSING (BITTERSWEET, WITH ONE DEATH OF NOTE).

THE BATTLE OF GRIMHOLLOW (TRIUMPHANT).

Fred found Grimhollow. Then, further round, he found it again, three years on. And again, four years after that. TRIUMPHANT both times. The fireworks were probably already ordered.

He thought about Mook, and about the word VICTORY on a form, and then decided not to think about it in front of strangers.

The bards did not look up. At the desk nearest him, a thin bard with ink on his nose was chewing his quill and glaring at a page.

"Nothing rhymes with 'survived,'" he said, to nobody.

"Arrived," Fred offered.

The bard did not turn round. "Too cheerful."

"Contrived?"

"Too honest." The bard crossed out a line. "Don't bother. Nobody survives in a good ballad."

Fred thought about the hundred and ninety-one people walking around the kingdom with their names back, eating breakfast and complaining about the weather, all of them very badly suited to a good ballad. For the first time since he had reached the capital, he felt quite cheerful.

The arm had wandered off without him. Fred found it, and the rest of himself, standing in front of a tall oak cabinet at the back of the room. The cabinet was locked. The arm did not seem to think this was important. It took hold of the handle and gave it a short, tired twist, the kind you give a jar for your grandmother, and the door swung open.

Inside, on a shelf by itself, lay a single thick manuscript bound in red leather, with gold on the edges. Its title was written across the cover in beautiful letters:

THE FALL OF THE DEMON LORD

Across the beautiful letters, in red ink, somebody had stamped DO NOT USE.

The dust on it was very thick. It was the dust of a story that had been finished a long time ago and then carefully, on purpose, never needed.

Fred closed the cabinet. The arm let him.

On the desk beside the cabinet sat a wire basket marked OUTGOING. There was one sheet of paper in it. It was a very ordinary sheet of paper, and the arm went still beside it, the way a dog goes still beside a smell.

Fred picked it up.

TO: NARRATIVE OFFICE (DEMON REALM).

FROM: NARRATIVE OFFICE (HOLY KINGDOM).

RE: GRIMHOLLOW (NEXT CYCLE).

PLEASE CONFIRM DATES. SUGGEST WE ROTATE THE VICTORY THIS TIME, AS AGREED. USUAL TERMS.

At the bottom were two seals. One was gold, with a sword and a sunrise. The other was black, with something on it that had horns.

Fred's left arm rose all by itself and saluted the black seal. Smartly. It was the best salute that arm had ever done.

Fred read the memo twice. The first time, he read the words. The second time, he read what they meant, which took longer and was colder. Then he folded it once, and again, and again, until it was as small as a stamp, and slipped it into his top pocket.

Behind him, the bards scratched on. The fire ate another verse. Nobody looked up as he went back down the stairs. Nobody ever looked at the background.

The Appeals Officer came back from lunch with crumbs on his robe. He found Fred sitting where he had left him, on the bench, with the folder on his knees and his hands folded on top of it, looking like a man who had been nowhere at all.

"Ah," said the old man. "Your appeal. Yes." He settled behind his desk, beneath the plaques. "Do you have anything new to submit?"

Fred took the memo out of his pocket, unfolded it, and laid it on the desk. He smoothed it flat with the hand he was still learning to write with.

The Appeals Officer read it. He read it the way Fred had, twice, and the second time slower. Then he took off his spectacles, folded them, and set them down very gently, as if they had done something wrong and he did not want to upset them.

"Oh, dear," he said.

For a long while he did not say anything else. Outside the window, the smoke went on rising.

"Can you uphold it?" Fred asked.

"I could," said the Appeals Officer. "Once. And then there would be a new Appeals Officer by Thursday, and he would be very young and very keen, and he would deny everything." He looked up at his plaques. "I have been doing this a long time, young man. I have learned that the most useful thing an Appeals Office can do is stay open."

He picked up a stamp. He looked at it. Then he put it down and picked up a different one, older, its handle worn pale by thumbs, and pressed it onto Fred's appeal with great care.

APPEAL DENIED (PENDING).

"It's the closest I've ever come," he said.

Fred understood that it was meant as a kindness. He decided to take it as one.

The old man pushed the memo back across the desk with one finger, as if it were hot.

"Destiny isn't chosen, young man," he said. "It's assigned. Somebody fills in the form. Somebody signs it. Somebody tests the slogan." He was quiet a moment. "You should not have found that."

"I'm sorry," said Fred.

"No, you're not. Good." He opened a drawer and took out a spectacles case. It was old brown leather, cracked at the corners, with the clasp held shut by a twist of wire. He tipped out a pair of spectacles even older than the ones on his desk, and held the case out to Fred. "Keep it somewhere safe. This case has survived three hundred years of appeals. It will keep."

Fred folded the memo small again and fitted it inside. The case shut with a soft click, like a door in a house where people are sleeping. He put it in his top pocket, next to Mook's glasses. One pair of spectacles and one case for spectacles. That seemed right, somehow.

As Fred stood to go, he saw the old man's eyes go up to the last plaque in the row: VERY NEARLY UPHELD, 1893. Beside it was an empty space on the wall, with a nail already in it. From the look of the nail, it had been waiting a long time. Fred did not say anything about it. Some things you leave a man to look at by himself.

Fred went down through the building without his map.

He did not need it. The stairs only went one way now, which was down. He passed the clerks in their blinkers, and the sign that said PLEASE DO NOT LOOK OUTSIDE. IT ENCOURAGES APPEALS. He passed the bench where the Boiler Stoker had sat, and he thought about her saying that nobody had ever needed her before. Two kingdoms needed her now. On a schedule. He thought about VOICE: TOO SAD, and about a tired young man who had sounded like himself exactly once, on the way to his golden arch.

At the bottom, the big front doors let him out into a gray afternoon that smelled of coal. Fred stood on the steps and looked up. Over the capital rose a fat black chimney, puffing away. Far off, beyond the walls and the hills, at the very edge of the sky, rose another. Their smoke went up, and leaned toward each other, and met in the middle, and became one cloud.

In Fred's pocket, folded small, was a memo saying that two kingdoms had been fighting the same scheduled war for four hundred years. It was the most dangerous form he had ever found.

It was also, he noticed, missing its copy.
