# Chapter 10: The Medal

In the official painting of the Battle of Grimhollow, Fred was nine-tenths of an inch tall and holding a mop.

It was unveiled on the fifth day after the battle, on the ridge, in front of the whole Field Army. It had to be carried up the slope by eight men on poles, like a sail. When the cloth came off, the army gasped, because the handlers had asked them to, and then went on gasping a little longer, because it really was very large.

It was called *The Chosen One Parts the Waters*. It showed the valley of Grimhollow at the moment of victory, under a sky of heroic gold. In the center, on a rock, stood Kaito Reklamo in white armor, his holy sword raised and his hair streaming in a wind that had not, in fact, been blowing. Before him the flood rose in two great curling walls of water, which bowed to him as if he had told them to. Between the walls, black demons tumbled away into the deep. In the foreground, nine noble soldiers of the Forlorn Hope lay dying in graceful positions, each one gazing up at the Chosen One with an expression of perfect gratitude. Above them all, Brenda's sparkles had been painted in real gold leaf. Overhead, a painted dove carried a small banner reading VICTORY, in case anyone was unclear.

The army applauded. Fred applauded too. Then, because he always read the labels, he went closer and looked at the corners.

In the bottom left, almost lost in the brown of the painted mud, was the old stone arch of the culvert. In its mouth, if you knew to look, stood a very small figure, nine-tenths of an inch tall, Fred guessed. It had one green arm, one ordinary one, and a mop.

"For accuracy," murmured a voice beside him.

It was the painter, a small, paint-spattered woman with a pencil behind each ear. She didn't look at Fred. She looked straight ahead at her painting with an expression of great professional calm.

"They'll never see it," she said. "Nobody looks at the corners." She sniffed. "But I'll know."

Then she walked away, and Fred stood a while longer looking at himself, very small, in the only honest brushstroke in Fekfosio.

The ceremony was held the next morning, and it went exactly to schedule.

The viewing stand had been taken apart and rebuilt as a stage, with steps, a gold rail and a red carpet that forty men with brooms had beaten free of mud overnight. Bunting hung from every tent pole on the ridge. The band had polished its instruments until they hurt to look at. And on a table at the front of the stage, under a velvet cloth that was whisked away with a flourish, lay the medals for the Battle of Grimhollow. They were the ones Fred had seen in the general's tent before the battle, already engraved with the date. Not a single letter had needed changing.

General Bombast Bellweather presented them himself. He stood at the front of the stage with his mustache waxed into two gleaming points and his chest jingling, reading from a scroll. As each name was called, an aide handed him a medal, and he pinned it on.

The Order of the Golden Sunrise, First Class, for parting the waters, would go to the Chosen One, Kaito Reklamo. It was saved for last.

The Bellweather Cross, a new medal struck for the occasion, went to General Bombast Bellweather, for planning. He pinned it on himself, with some difficulty, because there was nowhere left on his chest, and in the end settled for his hat. His hat now outranked most of the officers present.

The Silver Lyre, for composition, went to the Bards of the Narrative Office for the Ballad of Grimhollow, all nineteen verses. The senior bard wept.

A Royal Commendation, for services to morale, went to Radiance Tonics, official sponsor of the Chosen One. A representative in a blue suit accepted it and handed out free samples.

Then the general cleared his throat, and his voice dropped to the low, grave tone generals keep for the dead.

"And now," he said, "we remember the Forlorn Hope. The gallant few. Who charged, and fell, and in falling made victory possible."

An aide whisked the cloth off a second table. Under it lay a bronze plaque, polished bright and mounted on a slab of stone. Across the top it said TO THE GALLANT FEW WHO FELL AT GRIMHOLLOW. Underneath, in small, neat letters, ran their names.

There were two hundred of them.

Fred read down the list from where he stood in the crowd. The farmer was on it. The Boiler Stoker. The Arrow Retriever. All of them. And at the very top, because he had been their captain, was his own name, MAI-CAPTAIN, F., with the hyphen engraved very small.

Around him, one hundred and ninety-one members of the gallant few stood in their mended uniforms, very much alive, reading their own names on a memorial. The Boiler Stoker made a small sound. The farmer took off his cap, then didn't seem to know what to do with it, and put it back on. Then he stepped forward and ran one finger along his own engraved name. "They spelled it right," he said at last, and sounded almost disappointed.

The band played a slow and beautiful tune, and everyone bowed their heads. It was, Fred thought, a very moving ceremony. It was just that most of the people it was about were standing right there.

The last medal of the morning was Kaito's.

Kaito climbed the steps to the front of the stage in his white armor, with his four companions a step behind and their handlers a step behind them. He stood very straight. The general held up the Order of the Golden Sunrise, a great gold star on a white ribbon, and the crowd went quiet. An aide handed Kaito a card. Fred, near the front, could see the card trembling slightly in Kaito's hand.

"I accept this honor," Kaito read, "on behalf of Destiny, which chose me, and the Holy Kingdom, which..."

He stopped.

He looked up from the card, out over the crowd: the mended uniforms, the bunting, the bronze plaque with two hundred names on it. When his voice came again, it wasn't the strong, clear voice from the speeches. It was the quiet, tired one from the Gilded Privy.

"This medal," said Kaito Reklamo, "belongs to..."

At the side of the stage, a handler moved so fast that Fred didn't see her move at all. One moment she was standing still; the next, the APPLAUSE sign was high above her head. The band, which had clearly been told what to do in this situation, burst into the national anthem at full volume, all brass and drums. The crowd, which had clearly been trained, burst into applause. Whatever Kaito said next went up into the noise and was lost.

Kaito stood at the front of the stage with his mouth still open. The general, beaming, pinned the star to his chest. The painters sketched furiously. And over the general's shoulder, across all that noise, Kaito's eyes went searching through the crowd until they found Fred.

Fred looked back at him. Then, very slightly, so that only Kaito would see it, he shook his head.

*It's all right*, the small shake said. *Don't. They'll only hurt you for it.*

Kaito's face did something Fred would think about for a long time afterward. Then the smile came back, bright as brass, and he turned to wave at the crowd. But he wore the medal crooked for the rest of the day, and nobody could get him to straighten it.

The summons came that afternoon, on a printed card delivered by a runner: PRIVATE (SEE NAME) MAI-CAPTAIN, F. REPORT TO QUARTERMASTER, FIELD ARMY. RE: OUTSTANDING ACCOUNT.

The Field Army's quartermaster had a tent at the bottom of the ridge that had clearly been set up by someone who knew what he was doing. It was the only tent in Grimhollow with a wooden floor. Shelves lined its walls, crowded with boots, saddles, sacks and crates, and every item, Fred noticed, had a price tag. At the back, behind a long counter polished by years of leaning elbows, sat a broad, soft, comfortable man with a ring on every finger and an apron full of clinking pockets.

"Hello again, lad," said Bilge Reprobate.

"You're here," said Fred.

"Followed the army," said Bilge, without shame. "The army's where the money is." He leaned forward, and his pockets clinked. "Now. I'm afraid there's a small matter of an account."

He laid a sheet of paper on the counter, turned it around, and pushed it across to Fred with one ringed finger. It was a bill, written in a fine, sloping hand, and it was itemized.

ONE (1) MOP, ARMY PROPERTY, DAMAGED IN ACTION.

SOCKS, FORTY-ONE (41), LOCATED WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION.

UNAUTHORIZED USE OF MILITARY EQUIPMENT FOR DRAINAGE.

WATER DAMAGE TO ENEMY PROPERTY (PROPORTIONAL SHARE).

LOSS OF ONE (1) ARM, LEFT, ARMY ISSUE.

RENTAL OF ONE (1) ARM, LEFT, ORC, PROPERTY OF THE HEALERS' GUILD (WEEKLY).

The numbers in the right-hand column got bigger as they went down the page. The total at the bottom was underlined twice.

Fred read it all the way through. Then he read it again, because there was one item he wanted to be sure of.

"Water damage," he said, "to enemy property."

"Proportional share," said Bilge kindly. "The demons have put in a claim. It's all done through the proper channels. Can't go flooding people without paperwork, lad. Where would we be?"

"And I'm renting the arm."

"Healers' Guild property." Bilge spread his ringed hands. "Weekly. Very reasonable rates."

The green arm, which had been lying quietly on the counter, made a small fist.

"I don't have this much money," said Fred. "I don't think anyone has this much money. I gave you my first month's pay for my shoes."

"Oh, don't you worry about that." Bilge took the bill back and turned it so Fred could see the bottom of the page, where someone had stamped a single word in red ink. "It's not going to you."

The word was ESTATE.

"Bill's been sent to your estate," said Bilge comfortably. "Seeing as you're dead."

It took Fred the rest of the afternoon to find out what Bilge meant, and in the end he found it out from a pay clerk.

The pay clerk sat in a small tent near the general's pavilion, behind a desk piled high with ledgers. He looked at Fred over the top of them with the patient sorrow of a man who has explained this before.

"Grimhollow," he said, running his finger down a page. "Forlorn Hope. Here we are. Two hundred, all killed in action."

"There are a hundred and ninety-one of us outside," said Fred. "You can hear them. That's the farmer, laughing."

The clerk listened politely. Then he tapped the page. "The official account states that the gallant few charged and were overwhelmed. The Narrative Office has approved the official account. The Narrative Office has approved the ballad. The ballad says they fell. The plaque says they fell." He spread his hands. "So they fell." He found Fred's death certificate and turned it around so Fred could read it. CAUSE OF DEATH: GALLANTRY. "Would you like a copy?" said the clerk. "There's a small fee."

Their pay had been stopped. Their rations had been reassigned. Their families had been sent letters, very nicely written, along with medals for gallantry, posthumous. The farmer's mother, the clerk said, consulting another ledger, had already received hers and written back to say thank you.

Fred stood very still in the pay tent. He thought about the farmer's mother, somewhere in the Holy Kingdom, holding a medal for a son who was outside right now, laughing. He thought about two hundred names on a bronze plaque. He thought about the bill, stamped ESTATE.

Then he took Mook's ledger from inside his tunic.

It fell open where it always did, at the pages Mook had ruled the night before the battle: two hundred names in Mook's tiny, careful hand, with the CAUSE column empty for all but nine. Those nine Fred had filled in himself over the last three days, in his slow, wrong-handed writing. And at the bottom: MOREOVER, M. — CASUALTY OFFICE — CAUSE: VICTORY.

It was, Fred realized, the only accurate record of the Battle of Grimhollow in existence.

"How would someone correct the official account?" he said.

The clerk looked at him as if he had asked how someone might move a mountain with a spoon. "In the capital," he said at last. "At the Records Office. With the right forms." He paused. "A great many of the right forms."

Fred thought about that. He thought about forms. He had always been good with forms.

"Then I'd like to apply for leave," he said, "to go to the capital."

He expected to be refused. He was not. The clerk checked a list, then another list, and then, with a slightly puzzled frown, stamped a form and handed it over. "Granted," he said. "Captain's privilege. A captain is entitled to leave after an engagement." He peered at the form. "It says here you're a captain. Pending correction."

The companions saw him off that night at the trench behind the caravan, which was the only place it could have been.

They didn't say much. It wasn't that kind of goodbye. Patrice gave him a sheet of paper she had copied out in very small writing. It was a clause from the army code, she said, number four hundred and twelve, the one about correcting records, and he'd want it, because nobody at the Records Office would ever have read it. Marge gave him a thick bundle of the right forms, tied with string. She had already filled in half of each in her neat priestess's hand, so all he had to do was sign. Brenda gave him a cigarette. He didn't smoke. He put it in his pocket anyway, because she meant it.

Gwen didn't give him anything. She just hugged him, hard, for a long time, and then went and stood by the trench with her back to everyone, and nobody said anything about it.

Kaito didn't come. He couldn't; the handlers were watching the Gilded Privy now. But as Fred passed it on his way to the wagon, he saw that the door stood open a crack, and the little brass lock had no key in it, and he understood.

Thirty Minutes was driving the supply wagon. He had volunteered. He said it was the fastest way to the capital, and he wanted to see whether a wagon could do it in under three days. He sat on the driver's bench, grinning, with the reins in his hands, and the horses stamped.

Behind the supply wagon, eight men were lashing a second wagon to it. On the second wagon, wrapped in canvas for its tour of the capital, was *The Chosen One Parts the Waters*. Fred would ride all the way there beside a twelve-foot painting of somebody else winning his battle.

Fred climbed onto the supply wagon with his mop, his ledger, his green arm, and a bill for more than he would ever earn, and set off for the capital to correct the paperwork. It was, he realized, the first thing in Fekfosio that he had chosen to do.
