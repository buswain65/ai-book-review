# Chapter 3: The Status Screen

Fred arrived in the Holy Kingdom of Fekfosio the way most things did: from above, and with a splash.

The chute ended in a pool. It was not a deep pool, but it was a wide one, and it was not water, or not only water. Fred came up spluttering, found his feet, and stood waist-deep in something cold and brown while the rest of Batch #4712 came down the chute behind him, one after another, like laundry. Plop. Plop. Plop. The pizza courier. The salaryman, holding his laptop above his head. A great many people Fred did not know, all of whom had been expecting an arch.

The smell from the top of the chute was explained. Fred decided not to think about it in any more detail than that. It seemed the polite thing to do.

The pool lay at the bottom of a vast stone yard with high walls, and above the walls was a sky the color of dishwater. On the wall facing the chute, someone had painted WELCOME TO FEKFOSIO, THE HOLY KINGDOM in gold letters taller than a man. Underneath, in smaller letters, someone else had painted MIND THE STEP.

Fred minded the step. It was slippery. He climbed out onto the flagstones and turned, dripping, to look.

From the top of the steps he could see the city. It climbed a long hill in terraces, roof above roof, and every roof bristled with spires. There were hundreds of them, gilded and sharp, as if the city were trying to stab heaven to get its attention. Between the spires rose smokestacks, black and busy, and from every spire and every smokestack hung a gold banner showing a sword and a sunrise. It was magnificent. It was also, Fred noticed, slightly damp all over, the way a magnificent thing gets when it has been left out in the rain for several hundred years and nobody has thought to bring it in.

Far up the hill, beyond the highest spire, fireworks went up: gold and white, bursting against the gray, with a faint pop that arrived long after the light. Somewhere, faintly, people were cheering.

"That's for the Chosen," said a voice. A guard had appeared at the top of the steps, a short, bored man in a dented breastplate, holding a hose. "Arrival ceremony. Very nice. Big crowds." He looked at Batch #4712, dripping on his flagstones. "This is yours."

He turned on the hose.

Afterward, wet and slightly cleaner, they were made to form a line, and Fred felt better at once. Whatever else was true of the Holy Kingdom of Fekfosio, it had queues.

The line led into the Appraisal Hall. It was a long, cold room with a ceiling painted to look like the sky, except that the painter had run out of blue two thirds of the way across. The last third was bare plaster with ETC. written on it in pencil. Tall windows let in tall stripes of gray light. Under the last of the painted sky stood a lectern, and on the lectern sat a crystal orb the size of a melon, glowing faintly, like a lamp left on in another room.

Behind the lectern stood the Appraiser. He wore robes covered in stars and an expression covered in nothing at all. Beside him, a clerk sat at a small desk with a very large book.

"Hand on the orb," said the Appraiser. "Wait for the light. Move along. Next."

The pizza courier was third in line. He rubbed his hands together, muttered "Come on, come on, come on," and slapped his palm on the orb like a man ringing for service. The orb flashed, and a glowing window opened in the air above it, big enough for the whole hall to read:

CLASS: DISPATCH RUNNER (FRONT LINE)

There was a pause.

"That's speed," said the courier slowly. "Right? That's technically speed."

"You run messages to the front," said the Appraiser. "Very fast, ideally. Next."

The courier walked away looking at his hands, as if somebody had swapped them for a pair he didn't like.

The salaryman put down his laptop to touch the orb, which Fred thought was brave of him.

CLASS: INVENTORY CLERK, GRADE 4

"Is that Item Box?" said the salaryman. "Is that a kind of Item Box?"

"It's a kind of box," the Appraiser agreed. "Lots of them. In a warehouse. Next."

The woman who had wanted fire magic was a few places further on. She touched the orb with both hands, and the light came up red, and for a moment her whole face lit up with it.

CLASS: BOILER STOKER

She did get fire, in a way. Fred thought about that for a long time afterward.

It went on like that. Each person stepped up glowing with hope, and each stepped away a little dimmer, holding their new class like a parking ticket. The hall grew quieter with every flash, the way a party does when people start checking the time. A barber became a Field Surgeon (Probationary). A schoolteacher became a Drill Assistant. A professional gamer became an Arrow Retriever. Watching, Fred began to see a pattern. The orb took whatever you had been on Earth and turned it very slightly the wrong way round, like a sock pulled on inside out.

Nobody got a dragon.

"Next," said the Appraiser, and it was Fred.

He stepped up to the lectern, wiped his palm on his damp polo shirt, and put his hand on the orb. It was cool and smooth, like a can from the bottom of the drinks cooler.

The orb did not flash. It flickered. Then it made a small, unhappy noise, a sort of hiccup, and stopped glowing altogether. Fred took his hand away, in case he had broken it.

The Appraiser sighed through his nose. He drew back his hand and slapped the orb smartly on its side.

Fred had seen that before, he thought. In a cubicle, a long way up.

The orb lit. The window opened in the air above it, and the whole hall read:

NAME: MAI-CAPTAIN, FRED

RANK: CAPTAIN

CLASS: LATRINE PALADIN

LEVEL: 1

SKILL: ALWAYS FINDS SOCKS (COMMON)

For a moment nobody moved. Then the Appraiser's eyes went to the second line, and something happened to his spine. He straightened. His heels came together with a small click. His hand rose to his brow.

"Sir," he said.

Two guards at the door, who had been leaning on their spears, stopped leaning and saluted as well. The clerk with the big book stood up so fast that his chair fell over. Down the line, Batch #4712 stared at Fred with expressions ranging from amazement to deep personal betrayal. The salaryman's mouth hung open.

"Oh," said Fred. "No. Sorry. That's my name."

"Sir?"

"It says captain, but it's in my name. I'm not a captain. I'm not anything." He looked up at the window again. "Well. I'm a Latrine Paladin, apparently."

Nobody lowered their hands. From the far end of the hall came the sound of running boots, and a sergeant burst in, red-faced, buttoning his jacket. "Where's the captain? They said there's a captain in the intake. We've never had a captain in the intake!"

Everyone pointed at Fred.

The sergeant looked at Fred: at his wet polo shirt, his name badge, his face. He looked up at the window. He looked at Fred again. And then, being a sergeant, and having been taught since boyhood that the paperwork was always right, he saluted too.

Fred did not know what else to do, so he saluted back. He did it badly. Several people winced.

The clerk from Corrections arrived about twenty minutes later, which is a long time to stand in a hall full of people saluting you. She was a small, brisk woman with ink on her fingers and a rubber stamp in a holster on her hip. She took in the scene with one glance, the window, the salutes and the sergeant's trembling arm, the way a plumber takes in a flooded kitchen.

"Hands down," she said. "All of you. It's a typo."

Every hand in the hall came down with relief.

She looked up at Fred's window, clicked her tongue and drew her stamp. Then she reached up into the glowing window itself, as if it were a sheet of paper pinned to a board, and stamped it. *Thunk.* The second line shivered and rewrote itself:

RANK: PRIVATE (SEE NAME)

"There," she said. "Sorted. It's the hyphen. We get one of you every few centuries."

"I'm sorry," said Fred.

"Not your fault." She considered. "Well. Partly your fault."

Fred looked up at the third line, which had not changed. "Excuse me," he said. "What does a Latrine Paladin do?"

The clerk from Corrections exchanged a look with the Appraiser. It was the look of two people deciding which of them had to say it.

"Holy duties," said the Appraiser.

"Oh," said Fred, encouraged.

"In the latrines."

"Oh," said Fred.

He considered it. It was not, he told himself, so very different from the Quik-Stop. Somebody had to clean the restrooms there too, and quite often it had been him. He had always done it properly, because a clean restroom was a small kindness to strangers. There was a kind of holiness in it, if you squinted. Nobody wrote ballads about clean restrooms, but then, nobody wrote ballads about soup either, and Fred had never held that against the soup. Still.

"Is it possible," he said carefully, "that there's been a mistake? With the class? Since there was one with the rank?"

"Class assignment is final," said the clerk from Corrections. The Appraiser said it at the same moment, so it came out in stereo.

"Next," said the Appraiser.

Fred was walking away from the lectern, with the window fading above his head like a bruise, when he remembered the form.

It was still in his shirt pocket, folded into quarters. It had been through a chute, a pool and a hose, and it had gone soft as cloth and faintly brown at the edges. But when he unfolded it, the printing was still there. COMPLAINT. And under it, the first box: HERO REGISTRATION NUMBER (REQUIRED).

He had one of those now.

He asked a guard where complaints went. The guard looked at him for a long moment, then pointed down a side corridor without a word, the way you might point a lost tourist toward a cliff.

The Complaints Office was the warmest room Fred had yet seen in Fekfosio. Someone had made an effort. There were potted plants, a little dusty, and a rug. Over the single window hung a hand-lettered sign, decorated with painted flowers and little smiling suns, that read WE VALUE YOUR FEEDBACK! Behind the window sat a round, cheerful clerk with spectacles on a chain, who looked delighted to see him, as if visitors were rare. A small framed certificate on the wall announced that the office had achieved ONE HUNDRED PERCENT CUSTOMER SATISFACTION, a figure it had arrived at by never closing a single case. Set into the wall beside him was a brass hatch about the size of a letterbox, with one word engraved above it: PENDING.

Fred filled out the form at a little counter by the plants, with a pen on a chain. Hero Registration Number: 4712-0381. Nature of complaint: There has been a mistake. He thought for a while about what else to put. Then he added, in his neatest block capitals, I SAID NO. He wrote the hyphen in his name extra small, out of habit.

"Lovely," said the clerk, taking it. "Lovely handwriting. You don't see that anymore." He read it all the way through, nodding, with every sign of interest, and Fred felt a small, warm hope begin to rise in him. Then the clerk took out a stamp, stamped the form RECEIVED in green ink, opened the brass hatch and dropped it in.

Fred listened. Somewhere below, a very long way below, he heard the soft sound of paper landing on paper. A moment later he heard it again, further down. Then again. It went on for some time.

"How long," Fred asked, "does it usually take?"

The clerk beamed at him. "We value your feedback," he said.

They marched Batch #4712 out of the Appraisal Hall in a column of twos, with the sergeant at the front. He kept glancing back at Fred, as if he was not entirely convinced. The column went down a long ramp toward a gate. Beyond it, at the bottom of the hill, Fred could see rows of low gray huts behind a high fence, a parade ground of mud churned by generations of heroes, every one of whom had arrived expecting a dragon, and a flag that said, quite plainly, BOOT CAMP.

The pizza courier fell into step beside him. "Latrine Paladin," he said. "Man. That's rough."

"It's honest work," said Fred.

"I've got to run messages to the front. The front, man." The courier was quiet for a few steps. "At least you're not going to the front."

Fred didn't say anything. He was thinking about the young man with the tired eyes going through his golden arch, and about Facilmora's chart, and about a brass hatch, and paper falling and falling.

Then, very close, so close it seemed to be inside his own head, came a cheerful chime.

A small glowing window opened in front of his eyes. It hung in the air a hand's width from his nose and moved when he moved his head, so there was no looking away from it. It had a thin gold border and a cheerful font, the kind used on birthday cards and eviction notices.

QUEST ACCEPTED: SURVIVE BOOT CAMP. REWARD: NONE.

Fred had not, as far as he could remember, accepted anything.
