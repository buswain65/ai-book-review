# Chapter 7: Mistaken Salute

The Battle of Grimhollow had a timetable, and the timetable had a gap in it shaped exactly like Fred.

He didn't know that yet. When the Hero's Caravan rolled into the valley on the tenth day, banners up and bards tuning, all Fred knew was the mud.

Grimhollow was a long, shallow valley between two ridges, and every inch of it had been churned. Trenches zigzagged across its floor like stitches in a wound. Siege engines stood along the near ridge, tall and spindly, their arms cocked back like the legs of enormous grasshoppers. Smoke from a hundred cookfires hung low over everything, so the far side of the valley came and went. When it came, Fred could see the other ridge, and along it a line of black banners, hanging still in the windless air. Nobody looked at them for long.

The caravan did not go down into the mud. It stopped on the near ridge, at a spot with a fine view, and the grooms began at once to build a viewing stand. It had three tiers and a gold rail. The painters set up their easels along the front row, and the bards tuned in the second. The third tier, Fred was told, was for sponsors.

He found the Casualty Office before he found anything else. It was a long brown tent at the bottom of the ridge, set a little apart, with a neat hand-lettered sign over its flap: CASUALTY OFFICE. Underneath, someone had added in chalk: OPEN EARLY.

Inside, at a folding table, with his black ledger open in front of him and his cardigan on under his armor, sat Mook.

"Fred!" He stood up so fast that he knocked the inkpot over, caught it, set it right, and then shook Fred's hand with both of his. "Fred. Oh, it's good to see you. Moreover, it's very good to see you. Sit, sit. How's the caravan? How's the Chosen One? Is the privy really gold?"

"It's really gold," said Fred, and sat.

They talked for an hour. Fred told him about the trench, the companions and the bard. Mook told him about the Field Army, about the cook who served the same soup three times under three names, and about the colonel who had read Mook's ledger over his shoulder and said "Very neat" and nothing else. It was the best hour Fred had had since Hut 9.

When he got up to go, he glanced down at the open ledger. Mook had ruled fresh columns across two new pages: DATE. UNIT. NAME. CAUSE. The columns ran down the page and onto the next, and the next. They were all empty. Beside the ledger lay a row of freshly sharpened pencils, far more than any one man could need for one morning.

"Preparing," said Mook, and closed the book. He didn't meet Fred's eye. "It's just good practice."

General Bombast Bellweather's headquarters was the largest tent in the valley, and the only one with a carpet.

It stood at the top of the near ridge, a great striped pavilion with a pennant on every pole. Inside, it was warm and close and smelled of cigar smoke and saddle soap. There were maps on every table, officers bent over the maps, and orderlies carrying tea to the officers. But the real heart of the tent was the board.

The board ran along the back wall, as tall as two men and as wide as a wagon, painted white and lettered in black and gold with the care of a temple sign. Across the top it said THE BATTLE OF GRIMHOLLOW (ORDER OF EVENTS), and below that, minute by minute, ran the battle:

06:00 — REVEILLE

07:30 — BREAKFAST (OFFICERS)

08:00 — BREAKFAST (OTHER)

09:00 — ARTILLERY (DECORATIVE)

09:40 — DIVERSIONARY CHARGE (EXPENDABLES), LED BY A CAPTAIN

09:52 — ARRIVAL OF THE CHOSEN ONE

10:15 — VICTORY (GLORIOUS)

10:30 — PORTRAITS

In front of the board, rocking on his heels with his hands behind his back, stood the general.

He was a magnificent man, in the way a wardrobe is magnificent: broad and tall and polished. He had an enormous white mustache that swept out on both sides of his face like the wings of a gull. His uniform was so covered in medals that there was no chest left to see, only a jingling, gleaming breastplate of honors. In one hand he held a riding crop, and as he rocked, he tapped it against the board: 09:40, 09:52, 10:15, VICTORY. He seemed to find this soothing. On a side table, under a velvet cloth, lay the medals for the Battle of Grimhollow, already engraved with tomorrow's date.

An aide hurried in, saluted and cleared his throat. "Sir. Captain Hollis has reported sick, sir."

The tapping stopped.

"Sick," said General Bellweather.

"Yes, sir. A note from his healer, sir. Bad back."

"He's leading the nine-forty." The general tapped the board, rather harder. "DIVERSIONARY CHARGE, LED BY A CAPTAIN. It says so. It's on the board. The ballad requires a captain, man. A gallant captain, leading a gallant few. If there's no captain, there's no gallant few. And if there's no gallant few, what is the Chosen One rescuing at nine fifty-two? Air?"

"Yes, sir. No, sir." The aide had a roster, and he was running his finger down it in growing desperation. "All other captains are assigned, sir. All of them. Unless... hm." He stopped and looked closer. "Here's one, sir. Attached to the Hero's Caravan. Sanitation."

"A captain? In sanitation?"

"It says Mai-Captain, sir."

The general's mustache rose like a pair of wings about to take flight.

"Does it," he said. "Fetch him."

They found Fred at the trench behind the viewing stand, with his sleeves rolled up and his mop in his hand.

Two orderlies marched him up the ridge, one at each elbow, so fast that he didn't have time to put the mop down. He came into the headquarters tent still holding it, dripping slightly onto the only carpet in Grimhollow.

General Bellweather turned. He looked at Fred: at the damp polo shirt under his tunic, at the name badge, at the mop. Then his eyes went to the roster in his aide's hand and stayed there a moment, and something happened to his spine. He straightened. His heels came together. His medals jingled. He saluted.

"Captain," he said. "Welcome to Grimhollow."

Every officer in the tent stood and saluted too. An orderly dropped a teacup.

"Oh," said Fred. "No, sorry. It's my name, sir. It's a hyphen. I'm a private. It says so. Private, see name."

"Splendid," said the general. "Splendid. You'll be leading the nine-forty."

"Sir, I'm Sanitation Support. I clean the..."

"Every man has his specialty," said the general warmly. "Mine is victory. Yours, evidently, is hygiene. Very good. A clean charge is a good charge." He turned and tapped 09:40 with his crop. "Two hundred of the best, Captain. The Forlorn Hope. You'll take them down the valley at nine-forty sharp and engage the enemy until relieved."

"Sir, I really think there's been a mistake on the..."

"Is there a form?" said the general.

Fred stopped. "Sir?"

"Is there a form," said the general, more slowly, as if to a child, "that says you are a captain?"

Fred thought about the roster, and the Appraisal Hall, and the glowing window in the air. NAME: MAI-CAPTAIN, FRED.

"In a way, sir," he said.

"Then you're a captain." General Bellweather beamed under his mustache. "It's on the form. If it's on the form, it's true. Dismissed, Captain. Nine-forty. Don't be late."

Fred was outside the tent, holding his mop, before he had quite understood what had happened. With a cheerful chime, a small glowing window opened in front of his eyes:

TEMPORARY RANK: CAPTAIN (PENDING CORRECTION)

He looked at it for a long time. For once, he hoped the correction would be quick.

The Forlorn Hope was billeted in a trench at the bottom of the valley, the closest trench to the black banners. It was not a good trench. The water in it had the gray, patient look of water that had watched several Forlorn Hopes come and go. It was half full of water and entirely full of people, two hundred of them, sitting on the firestep with their knees pulled up, not talking.

Fred climbed down into it with his mop. Two hundred faces turned toward him.

He knew some of them. There was the farmer from boot camp, the one who had told him about Gruk's leg, looking thinner. There was the gamer who had become an Arrow Retriever, with an empty quiver. There was the woman who had wanted fire magic, the Boiler Stoker, with soot on her face and her hands clasped tight in her lap. The rest were strangers. Most of them were young. All of them, Fred saw at once, were frightened, the way people are frightened when they have read the board.

"Are you the captain?" said the farmer.

Fred opened his mouth to explain about the hyphen. Then he looked at their faces and closed it again.

"I'm Fred," he said.

He didn't know how to lead a charge. He didn't know how to lead anything. He had never even been Shift Supervisor. So he did the only thing he knew how to do. He walked down the trench, slowly, from one end to the other, and he looked at everyone, and he tidied.

He got them up off the wet firestep and onto the dry part. He lined up their packs. He checked their boots, every pair, and showed three of them how to lace boots so the water stayed out. He found the farmer's lost canteen under a duckboard. Then, because it was the thing he was best at, he found their socks. He found them without looking: socks in the mud, socks at the bottom of packs, socks rolled inside helmets and forgotten. By sunset he had found forty-one missing socks and returned every one to its owner, and two hundred people in the worst trench in Grimhollow had dry feet for the first time in a week. The Boiler Stoker, who had wanted fire magic, held her dry socks in both hands for a long time before she put them on.

It didn't make them less frightened. But Fred noticed that they had started to talk again, quietly, and that when he passed, they nodded.

At dusk the general's aide came down with a clipboard to check their numbers. Fred caught him by the sleeve.

"Excuse me," he said. "What's the charge for? Exactly?"

The aide looked surprised to be asked. "To be rescued," he said. "By the Chosen One. At nine fifty-two. It's in the ballad."

"How many get rescued?"

The aide checked his clipboard. "The ballad says 'a gallant few.'"

"How few is a few?"

The aide looked at the clipboard for a long moment. Then he looked at the two hundred people in the trench with dry feet. Then he tucked the clipboard under his arm and went back up the ridge without answering, and Fred watched him go.

That night the whole valley seemed to hold its breath. Campfires burned along the near ridge, hundreds of small orange lights, and around each one sat a ring of soldiers, not singing. On the far ridge there were no fires at all, only the dark line of the banners against the darker sky. From the far ridge came no sound at all, which was worse than drums. Somewhere up at the caravan, a bard was practicing a lute part, the same four bars over and over: the bit where the gallant few were saved.

Fred went up to the trench behind the viewing stand one last time. He didn't know why. Habit, maybe. The companions were already there.

Patrice had a copy of the battle script. She'd lifted it from a handler's satchel, she said, and nobody would miss it till morning. She held it close to the lantern and read it out flatly, while Brenda smoked, Gwen sat with her bow across her knees, and Marge, for once, put down her paperwork.

"'Act One: The Gathering Storm.' That's the artillery. 'Act Two: The Sacrifice.'" Patrice stopped. She looked up at Fred, then back down, and read on more quietly. "'A gallant company, led by a gallant captain, charges the enemy line and is overwhelmed. Their sacrifice is not in vain. Act Three: The Chosen One Arrives.'"

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Not in vain," said Brenda at last, to the stars. "That's nice for them."

Later, when the others had gone, Fred cleaned the Gilded Privy. The door handle turned, as he'd known it would. Kaito came in without his armor, locked the door, and sat down on the step.

This time Fred spoke first. He told him about the board, and the nine-forty, and the hyphen, and the two hundred people in the trench with dry feet.

Kaito listened without moving. When Fred had finished, he was silent for so long that Fred thought he might not answer at all. Then he said, very quietly, looking at nothing:

"I'm scheduled for nine fifty-two."

Twelve minutes. Fred did the arithmetic. He was good at arithmetic.

Kaito stood up, unlocked the door and went out. He didn't look back. But Fred saw his hands as he went, and they were shaking.

Mook found him last, on his way down to the trench. He was standing at the foot of the ridge in the dark with his ledger under his arm, as if he had been waiting a while.

"I heard," he said. "The whole camp's heard. Captain Mai-Captain." He tried to smile. "Moreover, you'll be the first captain I've ever known personally."

"I'm not a captain," said Fred.

"No." Mook looked down at the ledger, at CASUALTIES on the front in gold. When he spoke again, his voice was very small. "Fred. Moreover. I'd rather not count you."

Fred didn't know what to say to that, so he did the next best thing. He reached out and straightened Mook's collar, which had been folded under on one side all day. Then he went down into the trench.

Dawn came gray and cold, and right on schedule.

At 09:00 the artillery began. It was decorative, as advertised: great colored bursts high over the valley, gold and white and blue, that boomed beautifully and hit nothing. The nobles applauded each burst, the way you would at a summer fair. Up on the viewing stand, the painters began to sketch. At 09:30 the bugles sounded. At 09:38 the Forlorn Hope stood up in its trench.

Fred stood at the front. He had a rusty sword in one hand. In the other, because he had carried it every day for two months and his hand had forgotten how not to, he had his mop.

Two hundred faces looked at him. They were waiting for a speech. Captains, they had been told, gave speeches.

Fred had never given a speech in his life. He tried to think of one, and all he could think of was the wall at the bottom of the chute on his first morning in Fekfosio: the gold letters, the smaller ones underneath, the slippery stone.

"Right," he said. "Mind the step."

Somebody laughed. Then somebody else did. It wasn't much of a laugh, but it was real, and it went down the trench and back, and two hundred people climbed out into the mud a little steadier than they'd been.

Across the valley, the demon drums began. On the viewing stand, a painter quietly mixed a new color.
