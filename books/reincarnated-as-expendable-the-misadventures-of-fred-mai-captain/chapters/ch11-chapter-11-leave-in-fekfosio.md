# Chapter 11: Leave in Fekfosio

The first thing Fred saw in the capital was a sign that said YOU ARE HERE, and the second was a sign that said FORM 3-A REQUIRED TO CONFIRM. It was, he thought, the first place he had ever been that had more forms than soup.

Thirty Minutes had got them there in two days, twenty-three hours and fifty-one minutes, and he was disappointed. He had been aiming for two days and twenty-two hours, and he blamed the painting, which swayed on the corners.

Fred had seen the capital once before, from the bottom of the sump on his first morning: a city of spires and smokestacks climbing a hill, every roof stabbing at heaven. Up close, he could see what he had missed. It wasn't a city. It was a building.

It had started, he learned later, as a single office, four hundred years ago. Then it had needed an annex. Then the annex had needed an annex. Then someone had built a corridor between the annexes and roofed it and put a door at each end, and the door had needed a doorkeeper, and the doorkeeper had needed an office. Four hundred years of that had produced what now covered the whole hill: one vast, continuous, many-roofed, many-spired building, every part joined to every other part, so that you could walk from one side of the capital to the other without once going outdoors. Most people did. Most people, Fred gathered, hadn't been outdoors in years. The streets were corridors. The districts were departments. The sky, for the most part, was ceiling.

The painting was unloaded at the Great Door to the sound of trumpets. A crowd had gathered to see it, pale people in gray robes blinking in the daylight, and they gasped on cue when the canvas came off, then stood gazing up at the Chosen One on his rock with tears in their eyes. Nobody looked at the corners.

Thirty Minutes turned the wagon around at once.

"Back to the Front," he said. "Dispatches." He grinned down at Fred from the bench. "Good luck with the paperwork, Hyphen."

"Thank you," said Fred.

"Try not to die again," said Thirty Minutes, and was gone in a spray of gravel.

Fred asked the doorkeeper for the Records Office. The doorkeeper gave him directions involving four left turns, which brought him back to the doorkeeper. The doorkeeper looked at him, sighed, and drew him a map involving four right turns, which also brought him back to the doorkeeper.

"It moves," said the doorkeeper, as if this explained everything.

It took Fred most of the first day, but he found it in the end by following a stream of clerks carrying armfuls of paper, the way you'd follow ants to a picnic. The Records Office was a long, low, gloomy hall, lit by lamps that never went out, and it was full of queues. There was a queue for births, a queue for marriages, a very short queue for name changes, and, longest by far, a queue under a sign that read CORRECTIONS (DEATH).

Fred joined it.

It was an odd queue. Everyone in it had the same slightly faded look, like a photograph left in a sunny window. The man in front of Fred had been declared dead by a clerical error eleven years ago and had been in this queue, on and off, ever since. The woman in front of him had been declared dead in a war she hadn't been in. A little further up, a very old man in a nightshirt explained cheerfully that he had been wrongly declared dead in his thirties, had waited so long that he had since actually died, about a fortnight ago, and was now waiting to find out which form applied.

"Probably the other one," he said. "It's always the other one."

After three hours, Fred reached the front and met the clerk. She was a thin, tidy woman behind a brass grille, and she listened to the whole story, the battle, the ballad, the plaque and the 191, with her hands folded. Then she reached under the counter and brought out a form.

FORM 9-D. CORRECTION OF DEATH. TO BE SUBMITTED IN PERSON BY THE DECEASED.

"So I just fill this in," said Fred.

"In person," said the clerk. "By the deceased. You are the deceased?"

"Yes."

"Then you're dead." She folded her hands again. "The dead cannot submit forms."

Fred looked at the form, and then at her. "But it says it has to be submitted by the deceased."

"Yes."

"And the deceased can't submit forms."

"No."

"Then how does anyone ever get it corrected?"

The clerk thought about this, and seemed genuinely interested by the question. "Have you tried," she said at last, "being alive?"

"I'm trying now," said Fred.

He showed her Patrice's copy of Clause 412. The clerk read it twice. Then she said she had never seen it before, that she would have to send for the original, that it might take some time, and that in the meantime she would need him to fill out a request to have it sent for. She handed him the form. It was blue. It referred him to another form, which was green.

That was how Fred came to be lost in the capital for five weeks.

He didn't mean to be. He went looking for the green form, which was kept in a different department, which had moved. In following it he passed through the Department of Departments, the Bureau of Bureaus, and a door marked OFFICE OF OFFICES (CLOSED FOR REORGANIZATION). At some point on the second evening, he realized that he had no idea where he was, and neither did anyone he asked.

The corridors of the capital went on forever. They were lined on both sides with filing cabinets, gray and green and brown, floor to ceiling, so many that they made a second wall. Above them the lamps burned steadily and never went out, so it was always the same pale, patient afternoon. Signs hung at every crossing. THIS WAY TO THE OTHER WAY, said one. YOU HAVE PASSED THIS SIGN BEFORE, said another, and Fred, checking his notes, found that it was right.

So he did what he always did when he didn't know where something was. He made a list.

In the back pages of Mook's ledger, in his slow, wrong-handed writing, Fred began to draw a map. He noted every corridor, every crossing and every sign. He marked the departments that moved and the ones that didn't. He numbered the doors. It took him weeks.

The first week, he slept in an archive alcove behind a cabinet marked DECEASED, A–F, which he felt was appropriate. He ate in a canteen two floors down, where the soup was always the same soup. It came out of tall gray cans in a back kitchen, and on the second night, waiting for his bowl, Fred noticed that the cans were facing every which way. Before he knew it, he was in the kitchen, facing them. The cook came in and found him there, forty cans in perfect rows, labels out. She looked at the shelves for a long time. Then she offered him a job.

He nearly took it. It would have been easy: a kitchen, a shelf, cans to face, and soup that never changed. He stood with his hand on a can of cream of something and thought about it for longer than he had thought about anything since the truck. Then he thought about the farmer's mother, and the 191, and said no, thank you. The cook said the offer stood.

The second week, he found the PENDING shaft.

It ran right through the building, top to bottom, in a square stone well behind a grille in the wall. Fred heard it before he saw it: the soft sound of paper falling, landing, falling again, never stopping. He pressed his ear to the grille. Somewhere down there, he thought, a very long way down, was a soft brown form with HERO REGISTRATION NUMBER 4712-0381 on it, and I SAID NO in his neatest capitals, still going. To his surprise, he found this comforting. At least something he had done was still in motion.

The third week, he made the discovery.

He had been looking for a particular pink form and couldn't find it. Then, without thinking, he reached behind a cabinet and pulled out its copy. He looked at it. Then he looked at the cabinet. Then, slowly, he understood.

Every form in Fekfosio had a copy. That was how the building worked. Every form was filled in twice, with a sheet of thin black carbon between the two. The original went one way and the copy went another, and sooner or later they were supposed to meet again and be filed together. Mostly they never did. The building was full of lonely forms whose copies were lost: a whole city of odd socks.

And Fred always found socks.

He tested it. He thought of a form, any form, and walked, and his feet took him to its copy every time, through four corridors, a stairwell and a door marked AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL. He found the copy of a two-hundred-year-old marriage certificate. He found the other half of a treaty. With a cheerful chime, a small glowing window opened in front of his eyes:

SKILL EVOLVED: ALWAYS FINDS SOCKS (AND THE OTHER COPY)

He looked at it for a long time. For the first time since he had come to Fekfosio, a window had told him something good.

By the fifth week he had filled eleven pages of Mook's ledger with his map, and it was, as far as anyone could tell, the first accurate map of the capital in four hundred years. Clerks began stopping him in corridors to ask the way. He always knew.

Everywhere Fred went, the green arm left exactly one filing drawer open, never more, as agreed. Before long, clerks who needed him simply followed the trail of open drawers.

It was in the fourth week that Fred, following a map of his own making, turned a corner into a corridor marked CENTRAL SUPPLY and walked straight into Recruit Spreadsheet.

He almost didn't recognize him. The salaryman from the waiting hall, the one who had clutched his laptop through a chute, a pool and a hose, was gone. In his place sat a senior clerk in a gray robe with three silver stripes on the sleeve, at a large desk with a lamp, a stamp, an abacus and an expression of perfect peace. His laptop was there too, dark and dead on a little stand of its own, like a shrine.

"Hyphen!" Spreadsheet jumped up and shook Fred's hand. Then he saw the green arm and shook that too, carefully. "Hyphen. I heard about Grimhollow. I heard you were dead."

"I am," said Fred. "That's why I'm here."

Spreadsheet listened to the whole story. Then he sat back and smiled the smile of a man who has found his place in the universe.

"I love it here," he said simply. "Do you know how many things are in this building? Nobody knows. That's the beauty of it. I've been counting. I'm at nine million." He spread his arms to take in the corridors, the cabinets and the endless lamps. "It's an Item Box, Hyphen. The whole building. I got what I wanted after all."

He meant it. Fred could see that he meant it. He looked happier than anyone Fred had met since the truck, and Fred didn't know what to do with that. It seemed to him that the building was a trap, and Spreadsheet was sitting in the middle of it, delighted. But then, Fred had nearly taken a job facing soup. He wondered what he would have settled for, if someone had offered him the right shelf.

Spreadsheet explained how things worked. Everything ran on carbon copies, he said. Nothing in Fekfosio was true until it was written down, and nothing written down could be changed except by more writing. And all the writing flowed downhill. At the top of the building, in a tower nobody went into, was the Narrative Office, where the official accounts were written. From there they went down to Records, and from Records down to Pay, and from Pay down to the families and the plaques and the ballads.

"So if you want to change what happened," said Spreadsheet, "you have to start at the bottom and work up. Or..." He lowered his voice. "Find the copy they forgot."

He gave Fred a desk in the corner of Central Supply, with its own lamp, a stamp pad and a fresh pen. Fred sat down, opened Mook's ledger, and went to work.

It took him a week.

First, he found the copy. Somewhere in the building, Fred knew, there had to be an original roster of the Forlorn Hope: the list the general's aide had checked in the trench at dusk the night before the battle, before the Narrative Office got to it. He thought about it, and walked, and found it in the bottom drawer of a cabinet in a stairwell, folded in quarters and still damp at one corner with Grimhollow mud. Two hundred names. Not one of them marked dead.

He laid it beside Mook's ledger. They matched, name for name. Two records of the same battle, both true, and neither one official.

Then he used Clause 412.

Patrice had been right: nobody had ever read it. It was two short sentences on a yellowed page at the back of the army code, and it said that a commanding officer could certify, in writing, that the members of his company were alive. It didn't say anything about the commanding officer having to be alive himself. Nobody, Fred supposed, had thought to put that in.

And on every form in the capital, Fred was still a captain. Pending correction.

So he filled in Form 9-D, Correction of Death, one hundred and ninety-one times. For each name, in the box marked CERTIFIED BY, he wrote in his neatest wrong-handed capitals: MAI-CAPTAIN, F., CAPTAIN (PENDING CORRECTION), COMMANDING. Spreadsheet carried them up to Records in armfuls. One by one, the forms came back stamped APPROVED.

The stamping was done at Spreadsheet's desk, and after the first dozen, the green arm took over. It turned out to be brilliant at it. It took the big APPROVED stamp in its huge green fist, raised it high, and brought it down on each form like a war drum, *boom*, so hard that the ink went right through to the desk. Clerks two corridors away flinched at every one. By the hundredth, people were coming to watch.

The pay was restored and the rations were reassigned. The families were sent letters, nicely written, explaining that there had been an error, that their sons and daughters were alive after all, and asking them, please, to return the posthumous medals by return of post.

There were still nine names in Mook's ledger that no form could bring back. Fred couldn't change what had happened to them. But he could change what the record said. He filled in nine Forms 7-C, Amendment of Cause, and in the box marked CAUSE he crossed out GALLANTRY and wrote, in his neatest capitals, A SCHEDULE. Nobody at Records read them. All nine came back APPROVED.

The farmer's mother wrote back within the week. Spreadsheet brought the letter to Fred's desk, grinning. It was short.

*Dear Sirs,* it said. *I have had the medal framed, and he has been far less trouble since he died. I would like to keep it, if it is all the same to you.*

Fred filed a form letting her keep it.

Then, last of all, he filled in a Form 9-D for himself.

He did it carefully. Name: MAI-CAPTAIN, F. Status: alive. In the box marked CERTIFIED BY, he wrote MAI-CAPTAIN, F., CAPTAIN (PENDING CORRECTION), COMMANDING. He carried it up to Records himself. It came back within the hour, stamped in red.

REJECTED. AN OFFICER MAY NOT CERTIFY HIMSELF.

Fred read it twice. Then he put it in his pocket with the others.

The next morning, at the far end of the Records Office, behind the queue for CORRECTIONS (DEATH), he found a door that nobody used, because it led outside. He opened it. The sky was enormous and gray and smelled of rain and woodsmoke and horses, and he stood on the step for a long time with his face turned up to it, blinking, like one of the pale clerks who had come out to see the painting.

Fred walked out of the Records Office into daylight for the first time in five weeks, knowing more about the forms of Fekfosio than anyone alive. Which, according to the forms, he wasn't.
