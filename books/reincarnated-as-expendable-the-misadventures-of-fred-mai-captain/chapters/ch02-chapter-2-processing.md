# Chapter 2: Processing

Fred woke up in a queue, which was a great comfort to him, because he understood queues.

He was sitting in a plastic chair. It was bolted to the chair on his left, which was bolted to the chair on its left, and so on, in a long curving row that ran off into a white haze where the far end should have been. There were more rows in front of him and more behind, thousands of them, tens of thousands, all full. Overhead there was no ceiling, only a soft bright cloud, lit so evenly that nothing cast a shadow.

In his hand was a paper ticket. It said 4712-0381.

Fred looked at the ticket for a long time. Then he looked up and found, hanging in the air above the haze, a vast red sign: NOW SERVING 4712-0094.

He did the arithmetic, and then he settled back. A queue was a queue. Queues had rules, the rules were fair, and if you waited your turn, your turn came. It was one of the few things Fred knew for certain about the world, and it was a relief to find it held for the next one too.

Somewhere a choir was singing. It took Fred a while to place the tune. It was the song that had played in the elevator at his dentist's for as long as he could remember, sung very slowly, in harmony, by what sounded like several hundred angels, all of whom had apparently smoked two packs a day.

He looked around at his neighbors.

They were all dead too, he supposed, and they had all died in whatever they'd been wearing. To his right sat a young man in a pizza courier's jacket, with an insulated bag still on his knees. To his left sat a man in a business suit, tie loose, clutching a laptop to his chest like a life preserver. In the row ahead, a young man in gaming headphones held a controller, his thumbs still twitching. Truck, Fred thought. Truck, truck, truck. A great many people seemed to have been hit by trucks.

What surprised him was their faces. He would have expected people who had just died to look upset. These people looked thrilled. The pizza courier was bouncing his knee. The salaryman kept checking his reflection in the laptop's dark screen and smoothing his hair. A woman two seats along was whispering to herself, "Fire magic, fire magic, please, fire magic."

Posters hung in the aisles, hanging from nothing. They showed a young man with impossible hair on a clifftop, sword raised, cape blowing, with three beautiful women gazing at him and a small dragon on his shoulder. YOUR DESTINY AWAITS!\* said the posters. The asterisk was very small and led nowhere Fred could find.

A cherub floated by. It was a real cherub, chubby and pink with small white wings, and it wore a lanyard and carried a clipboard. Fred raised a hand.

"Excuse me. I think there's been a mistake."

The cherub didn't stop, exactly, but it slowed. "Everybody thinks that, hon."

"I said no," said Fred. "To the man in the suit. I said I liked it where I was."

"Then you'll want to file a complaint." The cherub produced a form from nowhere and handed it over. "Fill that out and hand it in at the counter."

Fred looked at the form. The first box said HERO REGISTRATION NUMBER (REQUIRED).

"I don't have a Hero Registration Number."

"You get that at the counter."

"So I have to go to the counter to get the number I need to complain that I shouldn't be going to the counter."

The cherub beamed at him as if he'd said something clever. "Now you've got it." And it drifted off, humming along with the angels.

Fred folded the form neatly and put it in his shirt pocket. You never knew.

He had been waiting for what felt like an hour, or possibly a week, when the trumpets sounded. There were no clocks, only the red sign, which had crept forward from 0094 to 0102.

Everybody sat up. Down the center aisle ran a velvet rope Fred had not noticed before, and two cherubs in little gold waistcoats were unhooking it. A gold carpet unrolled itself with a sound like a sigh. Along it, past ten thousand people with tickets in their hands, walked a young man.

He wore a dark uniform jacket with a high collar, and he was the most handsome person Fred had ever seen. His black hair swept in a way hair should not be able to sweep, and it did not move at all when he walked. He had the kind of face that looked as if it was about to be very brave about something. Cherubs fluttered around him like moths. One sprayed a sparkling mist over him, and another fussed with his collar.

"Who's that?" whispered the pizza courier.

"That's him," breathed the salaryman. "That's a Chosen One."

The gold carpet led to a door at the side of the hall, which Fred hadn't noticed either. It was made of frosted glass, with light blazing through it. The young man went in, and the door didn't quite close. Everyone else was craning to look, so Fred looked too.

Through the gap and the frosted glass, he saw a tall golden shape lift a sword out of the air, a long white blade that rang like a bell. He saw a ribbon of blue light pour into the young man's chest, and a voice like a game-show host said, "...Infinite Mana..." Four shapes in flowing dresses filed in through another door and lined up behind the young man, and the same voice said, "...and the Harem, Deluxe Package!" A camera flashed. Then another, then a great many.

"Kaito Reklamo!" a cherub announced at the door. "Your Chosen One for Batch 4712! Be inspired, everybody! This could be YOU!"

The hall erupted. People leapt to their feet, cheering, whistling and stamping. The gamer was in tears. The woman who wanted fire magic was hugging a stranger.

Fred clapped politely, because everyone else was. Then he noticed something, and his hands slowed.

Floating near the ceiling at the front of the hall, where everyone could see it and nobody was looking, a cherub held up a large sign. The sign said APPLAUSE. As Fred watched, the cherub flipped it over. The other side said CHEER LOUDER.

The hall cheered louder.

Kaito Reklamo came out of the frosted door with his new sword on his back and four women behind him. He smiled at the crowd and waved. For a moment, just as he passed Fred's row, his smile slipped a fraction, and Fred saw that his eyes were very tired. Then the smile came back, bright as the trumpets, and he was gone through a door at the far end. The velvet rope was hooked back up, and the sign clicked forward to 0103.

"That's going to be me," said the salaryman, to nobody. "Next time. I can feel it."

Fred looked down at his ticket and said nothing.

It was a long time before the sign said 4712-0381, and when it did, Fred almost missed it, because he had been dozing in a way that wasn't quite sleep. A voice said, "Four seven one two, oh three eight one, to cubicle seven," so warmly and personally that it seemed to have waited all its life to say his number. He stood up. His knees didn't ache. Being dead had some advantages.

Cubicle seven was one of a row of thousands. The gray fabric partitions were exactly like the ones at the Quik-Stop's regional office, except that these went up and up into the clouds. Inside were a desk, a terminal, a chair for visitors, and a goddess.

She was radiant; there was no other word for it. She glowed gold at the edges, her hair floated as if underwater, and she wore something long and gauzy that moved without wind. She also wore a telephone headset. At her elbow sat a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST DEITY. Pinned to the partition behind her was a chart. Long ago its line had gone up. Lately it went down, steeply, toward a dotted red line marked QUOTA.

"Welcome, welcome, sit down, congratulations," said the goddess, all in one breath, typing. "I am Facilmora, Goddess of Reincarnation and Second Chances. On behalf of the heavens, may I extend my deepest apologies for your untimely death. It was an accident, a terrible accident, and as compensation you have been chosen for a new life in a world of magic and adventure. Congratulations! You've been chosen!" She did not look up from the screen. "Name?"

"Fred Mai-Captain," said Fred. "But I said no."

"Mm-hm. Spell that?"

He spelled it. At the hyphen she stopped typing and frowned at the screen.

"I said no," Fred repeated gently, because she seemed busy. "To the man in the suit, with the clipboard. He offered me a free trial, and I said no thank you."

"Everybody says no," said Facilmora, typing again. "Everybody signs. Date of death?"

"Tonight, I suppose. This morning. About half past three."

"Cause?"

"A truck."

"Of course it was." She hit a key harder than she needed to. "Any questions before we proceed?"

Fred thought about it. He had a great many questions, but most of them looked as if they would take a long time, and the queue behind him was very long. So he asked the one that seemed most important.

"You said it was an accident. Me dying."

"A terrible accident," said Facilmora automatically. "On behalf of the heavens, may I extend my deepest—"

"It's all right," said Fred. "I mean, I forgive you. Whoever did it. I'm sure it's a hard job. I know what it's like when the numbers are bad."

Facilmora stopped typing.

For a moment the cubicle was very quiet. The angels hummed far away. The goddess turned in her chair and looked at him, properly, for the first time. Fred saw that under the glow her face was tired in the same way Kaito's had been, as if she were holding up something heavy just out of sight. Her mouth opened a little. Nobody, Fred thought, had said that to her in a very long time. Possibly ever.

Then something on her desk went *ding*, and a small red light began to flash. She blinked, the glow came back up to full, and she turned back to the screen.

"Thank you, that's very... that's lovely. Cheat skill!"

The cheat skills lived in a machine in the corner of the cubicle. It looked like the capsule-toy machine at the front of the Quik-Stop, where you put in two quarters and got a plastic egg with a sticky hand inside, except that this one was the size of a refrigerator and glowed faintly. Through the glass Fred could see rows of little slots, each with a label underneath.

Most of the labels said SOLD OUT. INFINITE MANA: SOLD OUT. SWORD SAINT: SOLD OUT. MONSTER TAMER: SOLD OUT. A few said RESERVED, with a little gold crown beside them. One said DRAGON (GIRL): RESERVED (VIP).

"It's random," said Facilmora, coming around the desk. "Totally fair. Totally random. Everyone gets the same chance." She put her hand on the crank. "Feeling lucky?"

"Not especially," said Fred.

She turned the crank. The machine groaned. Somewhere deep inside it, something rattled down a long way and stopped. Nothing came out.

Facilmora sighed, drew back one gauzy foot, and kicked the machine hard in the side.

A capsule rolled down the chute and dropped into the tray. It was gray. Facilmora picked it up, twisted it open and read the slip of paper inside with the face of someone who has learned not to react.

"Always Finds Socks," she said. "Common."

Fred thought of Hank, and of the sock behind the reading glasses, and felt a strange little click inside him, as if something had been put away in the right place.

"Is that good?" he asked.

"It's..." said Facilmora, and paused. "It's a skill."

"Could I swap it? Not for a dragon or anything. Just for something that might help."

"Exchanges require a Form 12-R." She was already back at her desk. "Available after death."

"I am dead," said Fred.

"After your next one."

The contract came out of a slot in the terminal and kept coming. It unrolled across the desk, over the edge, across the floor and out under the partition into the next cubicle, where somebody said, "Hey." It was printed on heavy cream paper with gold edges, and it was almost entirely small print.

"Sign here," said Facilmora, tapping a box with a gold pen, "and here, and here." She flicked the scroll so that a long stretch of it slid past. "Don't read that bit."

Fred read that bit.

It wasn't easy. The print was tiny, and much of it was in the same curling, wriggling script he had seen on the recruiter's clipboard. But some of it was in English, and Fred read slowly, following with his finger, the way he read the backs of lottery tickets.

THE HERO (HEREINAFTER "THE EXPENDABLE") ACKNOWLEDGES...

There it was again. He read on. The Expendable acknowledged that destiny was not guaranteed. The Expendable acknowledged that cheat skills were assigned at random and were non-refundable. The Expendable acknowledged that results might vary, that past heroes were no guarantee of future heroes, and that harems were subject to availability. Near the bottom, in letters a little bolder than the rest, it said:

CLASS ASSIGNMENT IS FINAL.

"What's my class?" Fred asked.

"Assigned downstairs." Facilmora held out the pen. "Here, here and here."

Fred looked for a box marked NO. He looked for quite a long time, down the whole length of the scroll and back again. There wasn't one. There was a box marked YES, a box marked YES (ENTHUSIASTICALLY), and a box marked I HAVE READ AND UNDERSTOOD THE TERMS, which Fred felt he couldn't honestly tick, so he didn't.

He signed in the three places. When he came to his surname, he wrote the hyphen extra small, so as not to upset anyone.

The terminal beeped. It was not a happy beep. A small window opened on the screen, and a line of red text began to flash in it: ERROR. ERROR. NAME FIELD CONTAINS RANK.

Facilmora looked at it for a moment. Then she reached over and smacked the side of the monitor with the flat of her hand, the way you'd hit a vending machine, and the window vanished.

"There," she said. "It'll sort itself out downstairs."

"Are you sure?"

"Everything sorts itself out downstairs." She stamped the contract with a gold stamp that said PROCESSED, rolled it up with one practiced twist and dropped it into a slot in the wall. "Congratulations, Mr. Mai-Captain. You've been chosen. Departures is through the door on your left. Next!"

Fred stood up. At the entrance to the cubicle he turned back, because it seemed rude not to.

"Thank you," he said. "I hope your numbers get better."

Facilmora was already typing. But as he went out, Fred thought her hand paused on the keys for just a second before it went on.

The departures lounge was even bigger than the waiting hall, and it was full of people from his batch, all holding stamped tickets and all talking at once. Above them hung a banner in gold letters: BATCH #4712, DEPARTING TO THE HOLY KINGDOM OF FEKFOSIO. Underneath, in smaller letters, it said WE HOPE YOU ENJOY YOUR DESTINY.

"Fekfosio," said the pizza courier beside him, trying it out. "That sounds cool. That sounds like an elf name."

Fred didn't know what it sounded like. He looked around for the woman who had wanted fire magic, to ask what she had got, but he couldn't see her.

Along the edge of the crowd, a slight man in a cardigan and glasses was walking slowly with a ledger open on his forearm, moving his lips. As he passed, Fred heard him murmur to no one in particular, "...three thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight, three thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine... Moreover, that's four thousand, and they said it would be twelve hundred." Then the crowd shifted, and he was gone.

Across the hall, the trumpets sounded again.

On a raised platform at the far end stood a great golden arch, full of warm light and birdsong. Through it, alone, walked Kaito Reklamo, with his sword on his back and his four companions behind him in a neat line, as if for a photo shoot. Rose petals fell from nowhere. The batch cheered; somewhere, a cherub must have been holding up a sign. Kaito did not look back.

"Where's our arch?" asked the pizza courier.

A bell rang. The warm voice that had called Fred's number said, "Batch four seven one two, please make your way to the departure point. Mind the gap."

The floor of the lounge opened.

It did not open dramatically. A wide square of it in the middle of the room simply wasn't there anymore, and in its place was a smooth dark chute, tilted down at a steep and businesslike angle into nothing. A faint smell rose out of it. Fred couldn't identify the smell, and later he would wish he hadn't tried.

The crowd surged forward, though it was never clear whether anyone pushed. Fred had always been good at queues, and he found himself near the front of this one, then at the edge, then over it.

The chute went down. Of course it did. Destiny, Fred was beginning to understand, was mostly downhill.
