# Chapter 12: Impersonating an Officer

Fred was arrested on the steps of the Records Office at a quarter past nine, for being dead in a public place.

It was a beautiful morning for it. The rain had cleared in the night, and the sky over the capital was a washed, pale blue, the color of a clean bucket. Fred had slept on the step, wrapped in his tunic with the ledger for a pillow, and woke to find the Great Square below him filling with people.

It was the Victory Tour. He had forgotten. The Chosen One was coming to the capital.

The pale clerks of Fekfosio came out of the building in their thousands, blinking and shading their eyes, in gray robes and inky cuffs, and lined the square ten deep. Bunting went up between the spires. Somewhere a band was tuning. Then the trumpets sounded, and the parade came round the corner.

First came the trumpeters on white horses. Then came the floats. The first was a great wooden wave, painted blue and white and frothing with cotton, and on top of it, on a painted rock, stood Kaito Reklamo in white armor with his holy sword raised. Behind him came the companions, each on her own float. Brenda glittered on a crescent moon, scattering sparkles into the crowd with the face of a woman doing her fourth show of the day. Gwen stood on a painted tree, holding her bow the way she had obviously been told to hold it. Patrice crouched on a giant cushion with her knives out and, when she thought no one was looking, scratched discreetly at her ears. Marge stood on a float shaped like a cloud, blessing the crowd with one hand and holding a sheaf of papers behind her back with the other. Behind them all, carried on poles by twelve men like the sail of a ship, came the painting.

The crowd went wild. Fred clapped, because everyone else was.

Kaito's float came level with the Records Office steps. He was waving, as scheduled: left, right, left, a smooth turn of the wrist, the same wave at every corner. Then, for one moment, from the top of his wave, his eyes found Fred on the steps.

His wave faltered, for only a fraction. Then it went on.

The Wardens came out of the crowd so smoothly that Fred didn't see where they came from.

There were two of them. They wore gray, like everyone else in the capital, but their gray was harder and shinier, with silver buttons, and on their helmets, where a soldier might wear a plume, each wore a single gold quill. One took Fred's right arm. The other reached for his left, found it was green and the size of a ham, and thought better of it.

"Mai-Captain, F.?" said the first Warden.

"Yes," said Fred. "Sorry. Yes."

"You are under arrest," said the Warden, "on the authority of the Bureau of Destiny, Office of Continuity." He unrolled a scroll and began to read, loudly, in the special voice of a man who enjoys reading scrolls. "Charges as follows. Impersonating an officer."

Below them, a few people in the crowd turned to look. One of them, a Bureau official with a clipboard, saw the Wardens, the scroll and the man between them, and evidently decided that this must be part of the parade. He had a folded board under his arm. He unfolded it and held it up. APPLAUSE.

The crowd applauded.

"Being deceased," read the Warden, a little louder, "in a public place."

The crowd cheered. Someone whistled.

"Unauthorized resurrection of one hundred and ninety-one persons."

A great roar went up. People were stamping now. A child on someone's shoulders waved a little flag.

"Damage," read the Warden, sweating slightly, "to an approved narrative."

The green arm, which had been hanging quietly at Fred's side all this time, raised itself, turned toward the Warden, and made the gesture: the three fingers and the thumb. The Warden had evidently seen it before, because he went a deep, mottled red, took a pencil from behind his ear, and added a line to the bottom of the scroll in furious capitals.

"And insulting an officer," he read. "Orcish."

It got the biggest cheer of the morning.

Fred was led down the steps through the applauding crowd. Behind him the parade went on: the trumpets, the wave, the moon, the cushion, the cloud, the painting. Kaito's float was already past. Fred looked back once over his shoulder and saw Kaito at the far end of the square, on his painted rock, sword raised, turning at the corner where he was scheduled to wave.

He didn't wave.

It was, the bards later agreed, the only corner on the entire Victory Tour at which the Chosen One failed to wave. A handler running beside the float noticed, and wrote something down.

The Holding Office of the Wardens of Continuity was a low stone building at the bottom of the Great Square. It had two heavy doors, one behind the other, with a short, dim corridor between them. Behind the second door, Fred could hear the clank of keys and the shuffle of prisoners. In front of it, at a high desk with a large book, sat the intake clerk.

"Name," said the intake clerk.

Fred told him. The clerk wrote it down, frowned at the hyphen, and turned the book around so Fred could see the first line of the admission form: PRISONER IS LIVING (TICK TO CONFIRM).

"Are you living?" said the clerk.

"I am," said Fred. "But it says here..." He showed the clerk his own death certificate, which he happened to have in his pocket, with CAUSE OF DEATH: GALLANTRY on it in a neat hand.

The clerk read it twice. Then he looked at the empty box on the admission form, at his pen, and back at the certificate, and Fred watched him slowly understand that he was trapped.

"I can't admit you," said the clerk. "You're dead. The form says living."

"Then can I go?"

"Of course not. You're under arrest." The clerk tapped his book. "I can't release you until you've been admitted. Release is on page two." He looked at the two doors, the outer one behind Fred and the inner one behind himself, and something resigned came over his face. "You'll have to wait in the Pending Cell."

The Pending Cell, it turned out, was the corridor between the two doors.

As cells went, it was not so bad. It was about the size of a pantry, with a stone floor, a high barred window, and a long wooden bench along one wall. The bench was already occupied by three people, and Fred recognized one of them: the old man in the nightshirt from the Records Office queue, who had waited so long to be declared alive that he had died in the meantime.

"Hello again," said the old man cheerfully. "They got you for it too?"

The second occupant was a middle-aged woman with her knitting, who had been pending, she said, for six years. She had made the most of it. There were cushions on her end of the bench, a potted geranium on the windowsill, a small rag rug on the floor, and, pinned to the wall with a hairpin, a hand-ruled sign-up sheet for who got the end seat on which days. The third occupant was asleep.

Fred looked around the Pending Cell for a long moment. Then, because he couldn't help it, he tidied it.

He straightened the rug. He turned the geranium so its best side faced the room. He plumped the cushions, squared the sign-up sheet, and swept the floor with his mop, which the Wardens had forgotten to take off him. When he had finished, the woman with the knitting looked at him over her needles with an expression of deep and growing affection.

"Six years," she said. "Six years I've been waiting for somebody like you. Would you like to be adopted?"

"Thank you," said Fred. "I'm not sure I'm allowed."

"Nobody here is allowed anything," said the woman serenely. "That's what's so restful about it."

They came for him at noon. A Warden opened the inner door and beckoned, and Fred followed him down a passage, up a stair, and into a room that was nothing like a cell at all.

It was small and warm. There was a soft red carpet on the floor and a fire in a little grate, with a pair of good armchairs drawn up to it. Between them, on a low table, sat a tea tray: a silver pot, two china cups, and a plate of biscuits arranged in a neat spiral. Over the fire hung a portrait of Kaito Reklamo, gazing into the middle distance. It was the first comfortable room Fred had been in since the Quik-Stop, and something about that frightened him more than the Wardens had.

"Sit, please," said a voice behind him. "Anywhere. Make yourself at home."

Fred hadn't heard the door. He never had.

He turned. The man from the soup aisle stood just inside the room. He wore the same suit, white or gold or white catching a gold light that wasn't there, fitting the way suits fit in advertisements and nowhere else. He held the same gold clipboard. His smile was exactly as it had been at twenty past three on a Tuesday morning, a lifetime and a world ago.

"Wonderful facing," said the man, with a nod toward the Pending Cell below. "Truly. I heard about the geranium."

He sat down in one of the armchairs, crossed his legs, and poured two cups of tea. After a moment, Fred sat in the other.

On the man's lapel was a small brass badge. Fred, who always read labels, read it. It said SENNOMA, and under that, smaller, (NAME WITHHELD). Fred looked at it for a long time. He had spent five weeks in a building where every piece of paper had a copy, and he could find the copy of anything. He realized now that in all that time, in all those corridors, among all those millions of forms, he had never once seen this man's name written down. It was the only thing in Fekfosio he had ever failed to find.

"Mr. Sennoma," said Fred.

"Senior Acquisitions Officer," said Mr. Sennoma pleasantly, "Bureau of Destiny. We've met." He held out the plate. "Biscuit?"

The green arm took the plate.

Not a biscuit; the plate. It lifted the whole plate out of Mr. Sennoma's hand, brought it round to its own side of the table, and began, steadily and without hurry, to eat the biscuits one by one.

Mr. Sennoma did not blink. "I see," he said, "you've been upgraded."

He let the arm finish. Then, while it licked its thumb, he talked.

He was very good at talking. He explained the system the way a kindly teacher explains sums. Heroes, he said, were the Holy Kingdom's most valuable product. They came from Earth in their thousands, and they came because they wanted to. And they wanted to because of the ballads, the paintings and the Chosen Ones: the dream. "Every hero who comes down the chute," said Mr. Sennoma, "comes because of a story. We sell the story. The story fills the quota. The quota fills the war." He spread his hands. "It's really very simple."

"And the war?" said Fred.

"Keeps the Kingdom running. Armorers, priests, bards, potions, tonics." He smiled. "Everybody needs a war, Mr. Mai-Captain. You'd be amazed how many jobs it supports."

Then his smile cooled, very slightly.

"And then," he said, "a hundred and ninety-one people came back to life."

He turned his clipboard around. On it was a chart, very like the one behind Facilmora's desk, with a line sloping down toward a dotted red line marked QUOTA.

"The Ballad of Grimhollow is our best seller this season," said Mr. Sennoma. "The gallant few. The noble sacrifice. The Chosen One parts the waters. Very moving. Very effective. And now every bard in the Kingdom is being asked, in every tavern, why the gallant few are drinking at the bar." He tapped the chart. "Recruitment in the eastern provinces is down eight percent. Do you know what eight percent is, in heroes?"

Fred thought about it. He was good at arithmetic. "Yes," he said.

"So you see my problem."

Then Mr. Sennoma reached into his clipboard and took out something Fred recognized at once. It was a soft, brown, water-stained form, curled at the corners, as if it had fallen a very long way and lain at the bottom of something for a long time. In the top box it said HERO REGISTRATION NUMBER 4712-0381, and at the bottom, in neat block capitals, it said I SAID NO.

"Fished it up myself," said Mr. Sennoma. "From the bottom of the shaft. I like to read the complaints. It's important to know your customers." He laid it gently on the table between them, beside the empty biscuit plate. "Everybody says no, you know," he said, very kindly. "Everybody signs."

Fred looked at his complaint. It had taken him a long time to write. It was the only thing he had ever sent anywhere, and someone had read it. They just hadn't cared.

"Now," said Mr. Sennoma, brightening. "Let's talk about what I can do for you."

He made his offer, and it was a good one. Fred had to admit that. Reverse the corrections, said Mr. Sennoma. Let the 191 go back to being gallant and dead, on paper only. Nobody would actually come for them. They could go on drinking at the bar for all the Bureau cared, as long as the records said what the ballad said. In return, Fred would be declared alive. Not pending: alive. He would be made a captain, a real one, with a pension and a pair of good boots. He would have a stockroom of his own in the capital, with shelves that never moved. And the day shift, if he wanted it. Or the night shift, if he preferred.

Fred said nothing. Mr. Sennoma watched him saying nothing with professional interest.

"Or," he said softly, "there is something else."

He touched his clipboard, and above it, in the warm air of the little room, a small glowing window opened. It was like the windows that had been opening in front of Fred's eyes ever since the waiting hall, only bigger and clearer, and it didn't show words.

It showed the Quik-Stop on Route 9.

It was two in the morning. Fred knew it at once by the light: the buzzing fluorescent tubes, the slushie machine turning its two slow colors, cherry and blue. Someone else was behind the counter, a young man in headphones, scrolling on his phone. And in aisle 3, the soup was unfaced. Cans stood every which way. A can of tomato lay on its side. Somebody had put chicken noodle in with the cream of mushroom.

Fred made a small sound. He didn't mean to.

"We do send people back, occasionally," said Mr. Sennoma gently. "It isn't advertised. Returns." He smiled. "There's a form."

He turned the clipboard so Fred could see the top sheet. It was a plain white form, very short, with a single box for a signature. Across the top, in plain black capitals, it said RETURNED TO SENDER.

Fred looked at the form for a long time. He looked at the soup. He thought about aisle 4, which he had never finished, and the creamed corn among the green beans, and Dolores saying someday, and his application in the drawer marked FORMS. He thought about it for longer than he had ever thought about anything.

Then he thought about the farmer's mother with her framed medal, and the Boiler Stoker holding her dry socks in both hands, and a hundred and ninety-one signatures crammed into every corner of a sheet of paper, a great many of them X's.

He picked up the offer from the table. He folded it in half, then in half again, and ran his thumb along each fold so the edges were crisp. He squared the corners. Then he held it out to Mr. Sennoma across the empty biscuit plate.

"No, thank you," said Fred. "I said no."

Mr. Sennoma looked at the folded paper for a moment. Then he took it and put it back in his clipboard, the little window over the table winked out, and the Quik-Stop was gone.

He sighed. It was the same sigh Fred had heard in the soup aisle: the deep, personal sigh of a man two hundred souls behind on his numbers in the last week of the month.

"Pity," said Mr. Sennoma. "Quota's quota."

As he was led back down to the Pending Cell, Fred said the name of the form over to himself, quietly, so as not to forget it. *Returned to Sender.* He had always been good with forms.

They let him go at four.

They didn't want to. The intake clerk explained it to Fred at length, and seemed personally pained by it. Fred had never been admitted. Therefore, technically, he had never been held. Therefore he could not continue to be held, because you could not continue something you had never started. Therefore he must be released, pending determination of status. The clerk filled in a form to that effect, Fred signed it, the clerk stamped it, and then the clerk handed him a second form.

It was a summons. It ordered MAI-CAPTAIN, F. (STATUS PENDING) to present himself at the Appeals Office of the Bureau of Destiny in seven days, to appeal the determination of his status and to answer the charges against him.

"The Appeals Office," said Fred.

"Top of the building," said the clerk. "Follow the signs. They're wrong, but follow them anyway."

At the door of the Pending Cell, the woman with the knitting hugged him and pressed a cushion on him. The old man in the nightshirt shook his hand. The third occupant was still asleep. Two Wardens walked him to the outer door, and there, with some ceremony, one of them handed him back his mop.

The square was empty now. The parade had moved on hours ago into the depths of the building, and all that was left of it were the tracks of the floats on the cobbles and a great many pink and white petals lying in the gutters, going brown at the edges. A sweeper was pushing them into heaps. Somewhere far off, faint through the walls, a band was still playing the Ballad of Grimhollow.

Fred stood on the steps of the Holding Office with his summons, his mop, his cushion and the ledger under his arm, and his pockets full of forms: the complaint form, the pink ticket, Kaito's note, Patrice's clause, the rejected Form 9-D, his own death certificate. He thought about the Quik-Stop at two in the morning, and the soup, unfaced. He made himself stop thinking about it.

A cheerful chime, and a small glowing window opened in front of his eyes:

STATUS: ALIVE (PENDING). ARRESTED (PENDING). CAPTAIN (PENDING).

Fred had never been so many things at once, and he wasn't sure he was any of them.
