# Chapter 1: Truck-kun

Fred Mai-Captain was, at two o'clock on a Tuesday morning, the happiest man in the world, and he had the soup to prove it.

Forty-two cans of cream of mushroom stood in three perfect rows, every label turned to the front, every seam tucked out of sight. Beside them stood the chicken noodle, and beside the chicken noodle the tomato in its cheerful red jackets, like a small and well-disciplined army. Fred stepped back, wiped his hands on his polo shirt, and admired them the way other men admired sunsets.

Facing, it was called. You pulled every can to the front edge of the shelf and turned it so the label faced out, so that a customer coming down the aisle saw a wall of order instead of a jumble. Nobody had ever thanked Fred for facing. Nobody, as far as he could tell, had ever noticed. That was fine. Fred noticed.

The Quik-Stop on Route 9 was open twenty-four hours a day, three hundred and sixty-five days a year, and between midnight and six it belonged to Fred. The fluorescent tubes buzzed. The slushie machine turned its two slow colors, around and around, like a tiny weather system. The road outside was empty in both directions. Beyond the parking lot there was nothing but dark and a billboard for a personal-injury lawyer.

Fred had worked the night shift for eleven years. He had been offered the day shift twice and turned it down both times. During the day there were people, and people moved the soup.

He straightened his name badge. It read FRED MAI-CAPTAIN in capital letters, with the hyphen squeezed in at the end where the label maker had nearly run out of room.

He moved on to aisle 3. Aisle 4 could wait until after his break. He liked to have something to look forward to.

At twenty past two the phone behind the counter rang. Only one person ever called the store at night.

"Fred, honey, it's Dolores." Behind her voice was the hiss of a highway. "I'm on my way to the airport, so I'll be quick. It's about your review."

Fred's heart lifted. The Shift Supervisor review. His application had been in for, he counted, four years.

"It's been postponed," said Dolores. "Corporate lost the paperwork again."

"Again," said Fred.

"Third time. I don't know what it is about your file, Fred. Every time it goes up the line it comes back all—" She made a vague noise. "The computer doesn't like the hyphen. It thinks you're two people. Last time it thought you were a rank."

"I could fill it in again," Fred offered.

"You could fill it in again," Dolores agreed. "Someday, Fred. Someday. You'll make supervisor, I promise."

"Thank you, Dolores."

He hung up and found that he was, if anything, happier than before. Someday was a perfectly good day. It was the day most good things were scheduled for. He took a fresh application from the drawer under the register, the one marked FORMS, and filled it out in his neatest block capitals. When he reached the box marked NAME, he wrote the hyphen extra small, so as not to upset anyone.

The door chimed at 2:41, and Hank came in.

Hank came in most nights, usually around this time, usually from the direction of the Rusty Anchor, which closed at two-thirty except when it didn't. He was a large, gentle man with a beard like a hedge, and tonight he wore one boot and one bare foot.

He squinted at Fred's badge, drew himself up and threw a salute that nearly took out his own eye. "Aye aye, Captain!"

"It's Mai-Captain, Hank," said Fred. "It's my name. I'm not a captain."

"Course you're not," said Hank. "Course you're not." He winked enormously. "My lips are sealed, Cap."

They had this conversation about twice a week. Fred did not mind. It was nice to be part of somebody's routine.

Hank bought a bag of pork rinds and two sports drinks, then stood at the counter frowning at his bare foot as if it belonged to somebody else. "Lost a sock," he said. "Lost a whole sock, Fred. Lost the boot too, but I know where the boot is. Boot's in the river. The sock, though." He shook his head sadly. "Sock's just gone."

Fred never knew why he did what he did next. He walked around the counter and down aisle 2, past the motor oil and the phone chargers, and stopped at the rack of reading glasses. He reached behind it without looking and pulled out a gray wool sock, slightly damp.

"That's my sock," said Hank, in wonder.

"I suppose it is," said Fred, who was just as surprised. Hank had not been anywhere near the reading glasses. Fred was fairly sure Hank had never needed reading glasses in his life.

Hank put the sock on with enormous dignity, saluted again, and went out into the night still one boot short. Fred washed his hands and tried not to think about it. It was the kind of thing that happened on the night shift.

At ten past three the only other person in the store was the college kid by the magazine rack. He had been there since one, reading a paperback with a cartoon girl on the cover, and he had bought one energy drink in two hours, which Fred considered within his rights.

Fred was restocking the gum when the kid looked up. "Can I ask you something weird?"

"Sure."

"If you got hit by a truck, like, right now, what cheat skill would you want?"

Fred thought about it. He liked to give questions their due. "What's a cheat skill?"

The kid stared at him. Then he explained, with the patience of the very young explaining something to the very old. Fred was thirty-six.

It was a whole genre, the kid said. Isekai. It meant "another world." You were some loser (no offense) and you got hit by a truck. A goddess appeared and apologized for killing you, and she reincarnated you in a fantasy world with a special power nobody else had, and you became a hero. "You level up. You've got stats. Everyone underestimates you, and then you destroy them. And you get, like, a harem."

"A harem," said Fred.

"Of elf girls. And a cat girl, usually. And maybe a dragon who's also a girl."

"And then what?"

"Then you defeat the Demon Lord."

"And then what?"

The kid looked at him. It was clear nobody had ever asked him that before. "Then it's the end. You're the hero."

Fred considered all of it: the truck, the goddess, the dragon who was also a girl. "That sounds," he said at last, "like an awful lot of responsibility."

"It's not about responsibility. It's about being special. Like, finally being special." The kid's voice did something small and private at the end, and he looked back down at his book. "Everyone wants that."

Fred did not, particularly. But he could see that the kid did, very much, so he said, "I hope you get a good one," and meant it. The kid looked embarrassed and pleased, and bought a second energy drink.
