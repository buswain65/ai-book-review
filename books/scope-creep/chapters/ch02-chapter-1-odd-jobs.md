## Chapter 1: Odd Jobs

The first thing Mike learned in his new life was that babies cannot file complaints.

He was born in the back room of a cobbler's shop in the river town of Brindlemoor, to a broad, cheerful cobbler named Hob Dobbins and a sharp-eyed laundress named Greta, who held him up to the lamplight, looked him over, and named him Mikel.

*Of course,* Mike thought. *Even the universe reuses old variable names.*

Mikel Dobbins. It had reused the surname too. Somewhere, Mike suspected, a deity was copying and pasting.

The second thing he learned was that \[Accurate Estimation\] did not switch off. It ran constantly, politely, in glowing letters only he could see.

**Estimated time until someone notices you are hungry: 2 hours, 14 minutes.**

**Estimated time until you can hold your own head up: 3 months, 6 days.**

**Estimated time until Father fixes the leaking roof he promised to fix "this weekend": 4 years, 2 months.**

That last one was correct to the day. Mike had to watch the bucket in the corner fill and empty for four years, two months, knowing exactly when it would end and powerless to speed it up. In a way, it was the most familiar thing that had happened to him since he died.

---

By age six, Mike had established three facts about the new world.

First, it had magic, monsters, and dungeons, all of which sounded dangerous and none of which he wanted any part of.

Second, his family was poor. Not tragically poor, just cobbler poor, the kind where boots got resoled four times and dinner was "stew" in the same loose sense that Brittany's routing feature had been "basically" built.

Third, there was an Adventurers' Guild in the center of town, a big stone hall with a quest board out front and a steady flow of coin moving through it. And it hired clerks.

"Clerks?" his father said, when Mike asked over dinner. "Indoor work. Good pay. Steady. They take you at twelve, same as the adventurers."

Twelve. Mike did the math, and the skill did it for him anyway: **6 years, 1 month, 9 days.**

A desk job. A steady paycheck. Paperwork, filing, a quill instead of a sword. After the life he'd had, it sounded like paradise.

*Indoor work at a stable institution,* he thought. *What could possibly go wrong.*

The skill considered the question for a long moment.

**Estimate unavailable. Requirements unclear.**

He chose not to take that as a sign.

In the meantime, the family needed coin, and in Brindlemoor nobody thought twice about a child running errands for pay. So Mike went to work.

### The Miller (age 7)

Oswin the miller was a big, flour-dusted man who believed in two things: the value of hard work, and the idea that saying something loudly enough made it true.

Mike's job was to run sacks to customers and messages back. It was simple, it paid in coppers and day-old bread, and for the first three weeks it was fine.

Then the baker, Mistress Pell, came by on a Monday afternoon and asked whether she could have a full cart of flour by morning. The harvest festival was coming. She needed to bake for half the town.

"Morning? Easy!" Oswin boomed. "Consider it done!"

Mike, who had been sweeping the mill floor, looked over at the great millstone. There was a crack running across it, thin as a hair, which he had pointed out to Oswin twice. The skill hovered over it helpfully.

**Estimated time to grind a full cart of flour with a cracked millstone: 4 days, 3 hours.**

**Estimated time to replace millstone first, then grind: 2 days, 11 hours.**

"Master Oswin," Mike said carefully, "the stone's cracked. It'll take four days at least. If we replace the stone first, it's faster. Two and a half."

Oswin laughed and ruffled his hair. "Four days! Listen to him. It's flour, boy. You put grain in, flour comes out."

Mike knew that tone. He had heard it in conference rooms. *It's a button, Mike. You just move it.*

They ground all night. The stone groaned and shed grit into the flour. By morning they had a third of a cart, and at noon the crack finally split the stone in half with a sound like a church bell falling down a well.

Mistress Pell got her full cart four days later, as predicted, and had to buy the rest from a mill two towns over at twice the price.

Oswin was furious. Not with himself, and not with the promise. He spent most of an afternoon shouting at the broken millstone, calling it lazy and ungrateful, and at one point hit it with a hammer. By evening, he was telling the whole market that the stone had underperformed.

Then he turned to Mike and said, "And *you* should have warned me."

Mike, aged seven, looked up at him with the hollow eyes of a man who had sat through forty-one post-incident reviews, and said nothing at all.

That evening, Oswin told his wife the boy had gone pale and quiet and "looked about a hundred years old for a moment there." She said he was imagining things. He was not.

### The Scribe (ages 8–9)

Mike lasted at the mill until Oswin "restructured," which meant hiring his nephew. His mother found him a place with Master Quillon, a stooped, ink-stained scribe who copied contracts and letters for the merchants along the river.

This was, Mike quickly realized, the most valuable job he could possibly have. He learned to read and write the local script, and to keep a neat hand, and to understand the long, tangled language of trade agreements. A guild clerk would need all of it.

It also introduced him to Tobias Venn.

Venn was a wool merchant with soft hands and a hard smile, and he could not leave a finished contract alone. He would approve a copy, watch Quillon seal it, and then return the next morning with "one small change." Then another. Then a clause that contradicted the first small change. Then a request to restore the clause he had removed the week before, which he did not remember removing.

His masterpiece was a wool contract in which he changed "sheep" to "sheep (wool-bearing)," then back to "sheep," then returned a week later demanding to know who had removed "wool-bearing" and whether Quillon was trying to defraud him.

By the third revision of a single shipping agreement, Mike had developed a system. Without really thinking about it, he started marking each draft in the corner: *Final.* Then *Final 2.* Then *Final (Venn approved).* Then *FINAL — ACTUALLY.*

Master Quillon squinted at the stack. "What is all this?"

"So we know which one is current," Mike said. "And which one he signed. And which one he says he signed."

Quillon studied the stack for a long moment. Then he went to his cabinet, pulled out every contract from the last ten years, and began re-sorting them with trembling hands, muttering about the Hendricks dispute of four winters back and how *this* would have settled it in an afternoon.

Mike got a raise of two coppers a week. It was the first time in either life that anyone had rewarded him for building process instead of putting out fires. He was nearly overcome.

Unfortunately, Venn noticed too, and started demanding to know why his old drafts were "still lying around," and could they please be burned, and also could Mike bring back the clause from *Final 2*.

### The Tavern (age 10)

Quillon retired at the end of Mike's ninth year, and Mike, now able to read, write, and do sums faster than most grown men in town, was hired to scrub dishes at the Drowned Goose. Literacy, it turned out, was not in high demand among tavern keepers.

The Goose was loud, warm, and smelled of spilled ale and onions. It closed at midnight. In theory.

In practice, there was Lord Ashcombe's steward.

Lord Ashcombe lived in the manor on the hill, and the Goose's owner, a nervous woman named Hilde, owed him rent. So every night, sometimes a quarter hour before closing, sometimes an hour after, the steward would sweep in and announce that his lordship required something. Pheasant. Roast lamb with mint. A specific pie that Lord Ashcombe had eaten once as a boy in a different town and could only partially describe.

"Right away," Hilde would say, and the kitchen would light back up, and Mike would be back at the washtub at one in the morning while the cook swore in three languages.

The cook, a one-eyed woman named Marta, took a while to warm to Mike. "The boy said the soup would boil over in forty-one seconds," she told Hilde, his first week. "It did. I don't like him."

"Couldn't he send the order in the afternoon?" Mike asked once. "Then we could plan."

Hilde looked at him as if he had suggested negotiating with a thunderstorm. "He's *Lord Ashcombe*."

The regular customers started drifting to the tavern across the square, where the food came out on time because nobody's kitchen was being held hostage by a pie. Hilde's response was to hire a second cook just for the steward. The steward responded by coming twice a night.

It was at the Goose, scrubbing pots at midnight, that Mike first heard the name Brannoc.

A group of adventurers sat in the corner booth most nights, nursing ale and wounds. Their complaints drifted back to the kitchen.

"He signed us up for the lich *and* the widow's lost cat. Same Thursday."

"Priscilla told the lumber guild we'd clear the whole Thornwood by the new moon. There's eight of us."

"Brannoc says if we'd just *worked harder* in the crypt, we'd have been done in two weeks."

"Brannoc killed a dragon once. He thinks that means he knows how long things take."

Mike stood very still at the washtub, a pot dripping in his hands.

It was the tone he recognized. Not angry, exactly. Tired. The sound of competent people who had stopped expecting to be listened to.

For the first time, a cold thought crept up the back of his neck: *What if the guild is just another Synergex?*

He pushed it away. The adventurers worked in the dungeons. He would be at a desk. It would be different.

The skill, still sulking, declined to comment.

### The Forge (age 11)

The Goose closed in Mike's eleventh spring, when Hilde finally sold it to Lord Ashcombe, who turned it into a private kitchen for himself and his midnight pies. Mike found work at the forge on Anvil Lane, pumping the bellows for Brunhild Ashgrove, a blacksmith with forearms like hams and the patience of a saint who had been pushed past it long ago.

Brunhild was good. Genuinely good. She made one sword a week, and every one of them would hold an edge for a lifetime.

Then Fennick arrived.

Fennick was a traveling arms dealer in a velvet hat, and he swept into the forge on a gray afternoon with the bright, beaming confidence of a man who had already spent money that did not belong to him.

"Brunhild, my dear, wonderful news! I've secured you an order from the Baron of Westmarch. Twenty swords! Fine steel, silver inlay on the hilts, ready in ten days."

Brunhild set down her hammer. "I make one sword a week."

"I told him you were the finest smith in the province."

"I am. That's *why* I make one sword a week."

"Well, he's already paid me the deposit," Fennick said, "so."

Mike looked at the cold steel bars stacked by the wall, and the skill obliged.

**Estimated time for 20 quality swords: 20 weeks.**

**Estimated time for 20 swords in 10 days: 10 days (quality not included).**

**Estimated time until those swords break: 3 weeks, 2 days.**

The forge did not go dark for ten days. Brunhild hammered through the nights while Mike pumped the bellows until his arms shook. There was no time to fold the steel properly, no time to temper it slowly, no time for a second look at anything. The silver inlay was done by Fennick's cousin, who was a jeweler, and who was drunk.

The swords shipped on day ten. Fennick collected his fee and left town in the velvet hat.

Three weeks and two days later, a rider in Westmarch colors arrived with a sack of broken blades and a letter from the Baron, which described Brunhild's work as "shameful," "brittle," and "exactly what one expects from small-town smithing." It did not mention Fennick, the ten days, or the drunk cousin.

Brunhild read the letter twice, then folded it and fed it to the forge.

"That's how it goes," she said quietly. "Man sells what you haven't made, sets a date you didn't pick, and when it breaks, it's your steel that's bad."

Mike sat down hard on an upturned bucket.

It wasn't just Synergex. It wasn't just the miller, or Venn, or the steward. It was the shape of the thing. The same shape, in every workshop and every world, as if it were a law of nature like gravity, or rust.

"Brunhild," he said, "does it ever get better?"

She considered this seriously, which was one of the reasons he liked her.

"Sometimes," she said. "When someone in charge finally listens to the person who does the work. It doesn't happen often." She shrugged. "But it happens."

The skill flickered, as if it wanted to say something about the odds. Mike, for once, waved it away.

### The Guild (age 12)

His mother had packed him bread and cheese. His father saw him to the door, beaming, and clapped him on the shoulder. "A guild clerk! We'll build you a proper writing desk at home to celebrate. This weekend."

**Estimated time: 6 years, 3 months.**

Mike hugged him anyway.

On the morning of his twelfth birthday, Mikel Dobbins, son of Hob the cobbler, scrubbed his face, put on the one clean shirt he owned, and walked to the center of Brindlemoor.

The Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor rose over the square: gray stone, iron-banded doors, and a quest board out front so crowded with notices that several were pinned on top of each other, and one appeared to be pinned to a smaller, older notice reading *URGENT*.

He knew how to read. He knew how to write a clean contract. He knew how long everything would take, down to the hour. He had a reference from a scribe, a miller who said he was "spooky but reliable," and a blacksmith who had simply written: *He listens.*

Inside, the hall was chaos. Adventurers argued at tables. A clerk sat buried behind a stack of paper taller than he was. At the front counter, a bright-eyed young woman in a ribboned blouse was beaming at a nervous farmer and saying, "Oh, absolutely, we can have the wyvern removed by Friday, and we'll throw in a full survey of your well, no charge."

And at the far end of the hall, standing on a table so that everyone could see him, was a huge man with a gray-shot beard, a dragon-tooth necklace, and a voice like a collapsing barn.

"LISTEN UP!" he roared. "The Duke of Varrenhold needs an escort tonight. Drop whatever you're doing! I don't care what it is! And whoever told the lumber guild we'd need six weeks for the Thornwood, you're on report. I've already told them three."

Guildmaster Brannoc. It had to be.

Mike stood in the doorway in his one clean shirt and felt every hour of his first life settle on his shoulders like a wet coat.

The skill chimed softly. A line of text he had nearly forgotten, from a beige waiting room a lifetime ago, glowed into view, its countdown almost run out.

**Estimated time until you regret this: 0 years, 0 months, 3 days.**

Mike took a deep breath, stepped up to the counter, and asked about the clerk position.
