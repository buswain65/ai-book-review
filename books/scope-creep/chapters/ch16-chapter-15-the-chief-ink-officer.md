## Chapter 15: The Chief Ink Officer

Brannoc introduced him at the stand-up the next morning, between the motto and the three questions, with his arm around the young man's shoulders like a proud uncle at a wedding.

"Everyone," boomed the Guildmaster, "I'd like you to meet our new *Chief Ink Officer*!"

The circle by the window looked at the young man. The young man looked back at the circle, beaming.

He was exactly as Mike remembered him from the Rusty Flagon: slim, neat, eager, with glossy dark hair combed back from a smooth, shining face. He had put on a new blue doublet for the occasion, with brass buttons. He was also still wearing his white waiter's towel, draped over one forearm. Mike suspected he had forgotten it was there. It had become, after four years, part of his arm.

"Rupert Spindlewick," said Brannoc. "He'll be overseeing all the clerks from today. Every one of them!"

Mike, behind his pillar, felt the floor move slightly under his feet. It was not the demon this time.

Rupert stepped forward into the middle of the circle, over the dog, and raised both hands as if blessing a congregation.

"Friends," he said warmly. "Colleagues. *Partners*. I'm so excited to be here. I've been listening to Guildmaster Brannoc's vision, and I have to tell you, it's *transformational*. And I believe that together, we can synergize the quest pipeline, move to a parchment-first strategy, and really *leverage* our core ear competencies going forward."

There was a pause.

Mike replayed the sentence in his head twice, carefully, looking for anything in it that meant something. He did not find anything. It was, he thought with a kind of professional admiration, a perfect sentence: every word familiar, and the whole thing completely empty, like a beautifully made box with nothing inside.

"*Ear* competencies?" whispered Velka, beside him.

"I don't know," Mike whispered back. "I don't think he does either."

The Duke of Varrenhold, by the window, raised the dragon tooth. "IS IT DONE YET?"

Rupert turned to him without missing a beat. "Your Grace," he said, "I *love* that question. That's exactly the kind of outcome-focused thinking we'll be leaning into."

The Duke considered this. "ADEQUATE," he said.

Brannoc rang the gong.

Afterward, as the circle broke up, Mike made his way through the crowd to the new Chief Ink Officer. It seemed polite. It also seemed necessary. He had worked for a great many bosses, and he had learned that it was always best to find out on the first day what they thought the job was.

"Mr. Spindlewick," he said. "I'm Mike. The senior clerk."

"*Mike!*" Rupert seized his hand in both of his own, towel and all. "I've heard *so* much about you. Love what you've built. Love it."

"Thank you. May I ask about your background? Before the guild?"

"Four years at the Rusty Flagon," said Rupert proudly. "Front-of-house leadership."

"I see." Mike waited. Nothing more came. "And what does CIO stand for, exactly? Chief Ink Officer?"

Rupert beamed. "Chief Ink Officer. Exactly. You've got it." He clapped Mike on the shoulder. "We're going to do *amazing* things together."

Mike asked the skill, because he had to.

**Estimated time until Rupert understands what a clerk does: unavailable.**

It wasn't *requirements unclear*. It wasn't *requirements contradictory*. For the first time in twenty years, the skill had looked at a question and simply declined to put a number on it.

---

Rupert's first act as Chief Ink Officer was to hire three new clerks.

They arrived that afternoon, all together, in a cheerful, noisy bundle. Mike watched them come through the new front door and felt his heart sink in a very particular way. They were young men in their early twenties, in brand-new doublets that matched Rupert's, and they walked into the guild hall the way people walk into a party they've been told will have free food.

"Team!" said Rupert, opening his arms. "These are my people. Dex. Tolliver. Bramble."

Dex was tall and amiable and vague, with a perpetual faint smile, as if he had just remembered a joke. Tolliver was short and sharp and very confident, and talked the most. Bramble was round and quiet and carried a lute, for reasons that were never explained.

"How do you know them?" Mike asked.

"We live together," said Rupert. "Above the chandler's. Best team I've ever worked with."

"Have they done clerking before?"

Rupert looked at him with gentle, pitying patience, the way you might look at someone who had asked whether a bird had ever flown before.

"Mike," he said, "it's about *culture fit*."

None of them could file. Mike found this out over the course of the afternoon, one by one. Tolliver filed a lumber guild contract under *L* for *Lumber*, then under *W* for *Wood*, then lost it. Bramble did not file anything; he sat at his new desk and tuned his lute. And Dex, when Mike handed him a quill to sign his new employment ledger, held it up to the light, turned it over curiously, and asked: "What's this for?"

"It's a quill."

"Oh!" said Dex, delighted. "Like a feather."

"It *is* a feather."

Dex looked at the quill with new respect, and put it carefully behind his ear.

They were given the three desks nearest the Guildmaster's office: the best desks in the hall, by the windows, with good light and new blotters. Those had been Mike's desk and the two beside it. Mike came back from the Registrar's room at the end of the day to find his desk gone. After some searching, he found it in the far back corner of the clerk's alcove, wedged against the wall behind Osric's stack, in the dim, papery shadow where no window reached.

He stood and looked at it for a while. Then he sat down.

The stack beside him rustled. It was the same stack, the same towering, shifting mass of paper that had been there since his first day, eight years ago. He had never seen the man behind it. He had heard only his voice, saying *Edwin knew*, and then, for the past several years, *Mike knows*.

Now, from somewhere deep inside the paper, a dry, quiet, papery voice spoke. And for the first time in eight years, it spoke directly to him.

"Welcome," said Osric.

Mike looked at the stack.

"Thank you," he said.

The stack did not say anything else. But after a moment, a single, slightly crumpled cookie emerged from between two folders, on a small, gray, wrinkled hand, and was set down on the corner of Mike's desk.

Mike ate it. It was a very old cookie. It was, under the circumstances, extremely kind.

---

The Ink Strategy was unveiled at the stand-up three days later.

Rupert had been building up to it all week. He had also ordered new nameplates for his desk and the roommates', and his own, Mike noticed, now read *Rupert Spindlewick, Chief Innovation Officer*. When Mike asked, Rupert said that was what CIO had always stood for. He had spoken several times of "something big" from "the team," and "a real paradigm shift," and "the kind of fresh thinking this guild has been crying out for." Brannoc was so excited about it that he skipped his usual eighty-minute speech and handed the dragon tooth straight to Rupert. Rupert handed it, with a gracious bow, to Tolliver.

Tolliver stepped into the center of the circle. He was holding a single sheet of paper. It was old and soft from handling, and its edges had gone slightly brown.

"The new Ink Strategy for the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor," Tolliver announced, in a ringing voice. "Two pillars."

Mike, behind the stack in the back of the alcove, could not see the paper from where he was sitting. But something about it made him put down his quill.

"*Pillar one*," read Tolliver. "*Write down how the Registrar works, in a book that stays with the guild, so that the next clerk doesn't have to learn it all by guessing.*"

Mike stood up.

"*Pillar two*. *Put all the quests in one list, ranked by what matters most, instead of a pile of URGENTs, so the adventurers can work from the top down, and we'll know what's being done and what isn't.*"

Mike came out from behind the stack. He walked to the edge of the circle. And now, finally, he could see the page in Tolliver's hand.

It was his.

Not a copy, not a paraphrase. *His.* The actual sheet of paper he had written on his very first day at the guild, at twelve years old, on the back of a rejected blue-ink contract, in his best scribe's hand. He could see his own handwriting from here: the round, careful, slightly too-large letters of a child who had only recently learned the local script. He could see the Registrar's great round stamp in the corner, **FILED**, dated eight years ago. Someone, presumably one of the roommates, had gone into the Registrar's archive and pulled out the first document Mike had ever filed.

And at the bottom of the page, under the two pillars, was a signature.

Tolliver read that too. He read everything on the page, in the same ringing, confident voice, all the way to the end.

"*Respectfully submitted*," read Tolliver, "*M. Dobbins, Junior Clerk*."

There was a tiny pause.

Tolliver glanced down at the page. Then up at the circle. Then he said, smoothly, without the slightest change of expression:

"...which is the working title."

Nobody noticed.

Mike looked around the circle, slowly, from face to face. Sixty people. Nobody looked at him. Nobody frowned. Nobody said *wait*. Sixty people had just heard his name read aloud, at the bottom of his own idea, and not one of them had noticed.

Except Velka. She was staring at him across the circle with her mouth slightly open.

And Brannoc was clapping.

"*Visionary*," said the Guildmaster. He was genuinely moved. Mike could see it. His eyes were actually shining. "*Visionary*. Write things down! Rank the quests! Why didn't anyone ever think of this *before*?"

Eight years ago, in the Guildmaster's office, over a roast chicken leg, Mike had read him those exact words. Brannoc had thrown back his head and laughed. *Write things DOWN? Boy, we haven't got time to write things down.*

The roommates received a medal. One medal, for the three of them, which Rupert pinned on Tolliver, who was the tallest. The gong was rung. Gareth the Bold shook each of them by the hand and told them it was the bravest strategy he had ever seen.

When the cheering died down, Mike cleared his throat.

"Mr. Spindlewick," he said mildly. "I'd just like to mention, for pillar one, I've already written a guide to the Registrar. It's three hundred and twelve pages. It's been in use for eight years."

Rupert turned to him, and his face lit up with warm, delighted encouragement, as if Mike had just said something adorable.

"Mike," he said. "I *love* the energy. I really do." He put a hand on Mike's shoulder. "But going forward? We really need you to show more *initiative*."

Gareth, beside him, nodded gravely. "Initiative," he said.

Mike did not say anything else. He went back behind the stack, and sat down at his desk in the dim corner, and looked at the wall for a while.

After a minute, a second old cookie appeared on the corner of his desk.

---

The second pillar of the Ink Strategy, the ranked list of quests, was never implemented. Rupert announced it, the roommates were congratulated for it, and then nobody did anything about it at all. The URGENT pile went on growing exactly as before.

Rupert, by then, had moved on to the thing he was really excited about.

He had found it, he told the guild, in the Rusty Flagon, left behind on a table by a traveling merchant, and he had read it four times. He held it up at the stand-up like a holy relic: a slim, pamphlet-sized booklet, bound in faded green card, slightly stained with ale.

Its title, in gold letters, was: *LEAN SIX SIGIL for Wagon-Wheel Manufactories*.

"This," said Rupert, "is going to change *everything*."

Mike, who had worked at a software company that had once paid an outside firm a great deal of money to apply a car-factory process to a web application, felt something inside him quietly close its eyes.

Lean Six Sigil, Rupert explained, was a *methodology*. It had been developed by the great wheelwrights of the southern provinces to eliminate waste, reduce defects, and achieve perfect efficiency in the manufacture of wagon wheels. And if it worked for wheels, he said, his eyes shining, then surely it would work for *anything*.

"Like goblins," said Rupert. "Like wolves. Like *dragons*."

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time to apply a wheel-factory process to a dragon: not applicable.**

The first thing, said Rupert, was the belts.

In Lean Six Sigil, he explained, every practitioner wore a colored sigil belt to show their rank. White was a beginner. Yellow had some training. Green was a practitioner. Black was a master. He had commissioned them already, from a tailor on Needle Street. They arrived that afternoon in a large wicker basket, and he handed them out himself, one by one, in front of the whole guild.

He did not hand them out by skill. He did not hand them out by training, since nobody, including Rupert, had any. He handed them out, as far as Mike could tell, by enthusiasm.

He gave himself the black belt first. He tied it around his blue doublet with a flourish, and posed.

"Have you done the training?" asked Mike.

"I've read the pamphlet," said Rupert. "Four times."

Dex, Tolliver, and Bramble got green belts. Practitioners. Dex tied his around his head, then was gently corrected by Tolliver, then tied it around his waist, then took it off and looked at it and seemed to forget what it was.

Gareth the Bold got a yellow belt. He looked at it with deep suspicion. Then he tied it around his forehead like a headband, at a jaunty angle, and refused to take it off. It went rather well with the medal. Nobody corrected him.

And Durgan Flintmantle, who had been called upstairs for the occasion, who had kept the wards of the guild hall running for nineteen years, who had put out a burning chandelier at a gesture and patched a demon's seal overnight with a chisel, was handed a white belt.

Beginner.

Durgan looked at the white belt in his broad, square hand for a long moment. Then he looked at Rupert. Rupert beamed at him with enormous goodwill.

"Everyone starts somewhere!" said Rupert.

Durgan said nothing. He tied the white belt around his leather apron, in a neat, square, dwarvish knot, and went back down the ladder. Pip, who had been given a yellow belt, went after him, looking stricken. Mike heard him, faintly, from the trapdoor: "Footnote: *nineteen years*—"

Then the trapdoor closed.

---

With the belts came the method.

Rupert drew it out on a slate at the next morning's stand-up, under the heading *VALUE STREAM MAP: DUNGEON*. It was a long row of boxes, joined by arrows. Mike read them from left to right: *Arrive. Open Door. Enter. Find Monster. Slay Monster. Loot. Exit.*

"*Seven* steps," said Rupert proudly. "Any dungeon in the world. Seven steps."

"What if there isn't a door?" asked Mike.

"Every dungeon has a door, Mike."

"What if there's more than one monster?"

"Then you *repeat* step five." Rupert drew a small curly arrow from *Slay Monster* back to itself, and looked at it with satisfaction. "It's called a *loop*."

Under the map, he wrote a number.

*Takt time: 40 minutes.*

"*Takt time*," Rupert explained, "is how long each step should take. It's how they keep the wheel line moving. One wheel every forty minutes, steady, like a heartbeat." He tapped the slate. "So from now on, every slaying in this guild takes forty minutes."

"Every slaying," said Mike.

"Every one."

"A rat and a troll both take forty minutes?"

"*Standardization*, Mike."

And then, because he had saved the best for last, Rupert turned to a fresh page of the green pamphlet and read aloud, with reverence, the sentence that gave the method its name.

"*A Six Sigil process*," he read, "*shall produce no more than three point four defects per million units.*" He looked up, eyes shining. "So that's our new target. From today, this guild will tolerate no more than three point four defects per million *goblins*."

There was a pause.

At the edge of the circle, by the coat rack, Pip raised his hand. He was wearing his yellow belt, and an expression of deep, quiet suffering.

"Footnote," said Pip faintly. "There are six hundred goblins."

Rupert considered this. Then his face broke into a huge, delighted smile.

"Then we're already *ahead of target*!" he said. "Great work, team!"

He rang the gong.

---

The first Lean Six Sigil quest was a wolf den.

It was a simple job, the kind the guild did every few weeks: a pack of wolves had settled in a hollow in the hills above the eastern pastures and had been taking sheep. Ordinarily, the crypt party would have gone up, chased them out, and been home by supper. Instead, Rupert assigned them a process.

He called it the *assembly line*.

"Specialization," he told the party, at the edge of the woods, with his black belt on. "That's the heart of Lean. On the wheel line, nobody does everything. Each worker does *one* step, does it perfectly, and passes it on. One person bends the rim. One person fits the spokes. One person paints." He spread his hands. "So: Sir Roderick, you only open doors. Velka, you only cast. Gareth, you only swing. Bertram, you only loot. Nobody does anyone else's step. That's how we eliminate *waste*."

"And me?" asked Sister Agatha.

Rupert consulted his pamphlet. He looked troubled.

"Your healing," he said. "Well. Let's be honest. When you heal someone, you're fixing something that already went wrong. You're doing the work *again*." He turned a page. "On the wheel line, that's called *rework*. Rework is waste." He closed the pamphlet sadly. "I'm afraid we'll have to eliminate you."

There was a silence.

"*Eliminate* her?" said Velka.

"From the process, I mean," said Rupert quickly. "She can watch."

That was not the end of it. Rupert had also found, on the last page of the pamphlet, something called *5S*: five words, each starting with S, for keeping a workshop tidy. *Sort. Set in order. Shine. Standardize. Sustain.* He had decided to apply all five to the wolf den.

Which was why, before anyone was allowed to enter the wolf den, the party had to *shine* it.

They stood at the mouth of a muddy hollow in the hills, full of bones and old fur and the overpowering smell of wolf, holding the polishing cloths Rupert had given them. Somewhere inside, something growled.

"Shine," said Rupert encouragingly, from a safe distance.

Bertram, who had carried things into seven dungeons and asked nothing about any of them, stepped forward with his cloth. He walked into the hollow, past the bones. He selected a wolf, a large gray one, who was so astonished that it did not move, and slowly, carefully, silently, polished it.

The wolf looked down at itself. It was very shiny.

Then the rest of the pack woke up.

It did not go well after that.

The wolves did not follow the process. They did not arrive in an orderly line, one per forty minutes, to be slain at a standard rate. They came all at once, out of three different holes, from three different directions. The assembly line broke down almost immediately, mostly because it could not start.

It could not start because the first step, after *Arrive*, was *Open Door*, and it was Sir Roderick's job, and only Sir Roderick's job, to open the door.

The den did not have a door.

Sir Roderick, who took his assignments very seriously, stood at the mouth of the hollow for an hour, sword drawn, scanning the rocks, looking for one. He looked behind boulders. He looked under bushes. He tapped the hillside with his gauntlet, listening for a hollow sound. He would not let anyone go in until he had found it, because nobody was allowed to do his step, and the process said the door came first.

Meanwhile, the wolves were coming out.

Velka, who was only allowed to cast, cast. She cast quickly and well, with tested spells, and drove most of them back. But one wolf, a scruffy, opportunistic brown one, got past her on the left. Velka was not allowed to swing a sword, because swinging was Gareth's step, and Gareth was standing on a rock at the back, waiting for the wolves to be found, so that he could slay them, in turn, in forty minutes each.

So Velka could only watch as the scruffy brown wolf trotted past her, stood up on its hind legs, took her hat neatly off her head in its jaws, and ate it.

It was a new hat. She had bought it after the crypt, to replace the one that had melted in the antechamber. It had a little silver pin on the brim.

The wolf swallowed the pin too.

Velka stared at the wolf. The wolf stared back, chewing, and then burped.

And Sister Agatha, eliminated from the process, sat on a tree stump at the edge of the clearing with her hands folded in her lap and watched all of it, and was not allowed to help anyone.

They came home at dusk. The wolves were still in the den, very shiny. The party was scratched, bruised, and missing a hat. They had done nothing out of order.

---

Rupert read out his report at the stand-up the next morning.

He had prepared it carefully. He had signed it, too, Mike saw, as *R. Spindlewick, Chief Inspiration Officer*. Nobody asked. Mike had stopped asking. The report was written out neatly on three sheets of paper, in Tolliver's hand. It had a title: *Wolf Quest: Root Cause Analysis*. Rupert read it to the circle by the window with the grave, serious air of a man who has looked deep into a failure and found the truth at the bottom of it.

"The wolf quest," he said, "did not achieve its outcomes. And I want to be transparent about that. I want to *own* that. So we've done a full root-cause analysis, and we've found three root causes."

He held up one finger.

"*First*. A failure of documentation from the clerk's office." He looked across the circle, with great sorrow, at Mike. "The process documentation for the wolf den was simply not there."

Mike, from behind his pillar, raised his hand. "Which documentation?"

Rupert gave him a long, sad, compassionate look.

"That's the problem, Mike," he said gently. "*You* tell *me*."

Mike lowered his hand.

Rupert held up a second finger.

"*Second*. The wolves themselves." He shook his head. "Frankly, the wolves were *non-compliant stakeholders*. They did not engage with the process in good faith."

The Duke, by the window, nodded thoughtfully, as if this matched his experience of wolves.

Rupert held up a third finger.

"And *third*," he said, and his voice dropped into something deeper and more sorrowful, "I'm afraid I have to point to the systemic legacy issues left behind by the *previous Chief Ink Officer*."

There was a pause.

Mike looked around the circle. Several people were nodding. Brannoc was nodding. Gareth, in his yellow headband, was nodding very seriously.

There had never been a previous Chief Ink Officer. The position had been invented, Mike was nearly certain, eleven days ago, at about midnight, at the bar of the Rusty Flagon.

He looked over at Velka. She looked back at him and very slightly shook her head, as if to say: *don't*.

He didn't.

"On a positive note," Rupert went on brightly, turning the page, "I want to give a *huge* shoutout to Dex, Tolliver, and Bramble for their outstanding *process leadership*."

The circle applauded. Bramble played a short, cheerful chord on his lute.

And that afternoon, a junior clerk came halfway down the Basement ladder, threw a scroll tied with a red ribbon, and fled. Durgan, without a word, opened the drawer marked *MIKE*, and put it in, beside the first one.

"Two," said Durgan.

"Two," said Mike.

Durgan was still wearing the white belt. He had not taken it off.

---

There was one more thing at the end of that stand-up. Mike almost missed it. Rupert said it so quickly, so cheerfully, as an afterthought, while the circle was already breaking up and people were drifting toward the doors.

"Oh! And one more exciting update, team." Rupert clapped his hands. "Next on the roadmap, we're going to be modernizing our core infrastructure. Starting with the Registrar." He smiled around at everyone. "Let's be honest. It's old. It's slow. Nobody knows how it works. It's time we replaced it with something more *modern*."

The guild hall went on emptying. People went back to their desks. Nobody stopped. Nobody said anything.

But Mike stopped.

Because from down the hall, from behind the closed door of the round back room, there had been a sound, the same sound there had been every day for eight years, every hour, so constant that he had stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat: the low, steady, contented hum of the Registrar, working.

And as Rupert finished his sentence, it stopped.

Mike stood in the emptying hall and listened.

The silence went on.

He walked down the hall to the back room and opened the door. The Registrar sat where it always sat, in the middle of the round room, under the dusty window, beside its great ledger. Its quill arm was still. Its stamp arm was still. Its two blue eyes were open, and glowing, and turned toward the door.

They were turned toward the hall. Toward the stand-up. Toward the place where Rupert had been standing when he said *replaced*.

"Esteemed Registrar," Mike said quietly.

The blue eyes swung slowly toward him. They dimmed, once, the way they did at Edwin's name.

Then, very faintly, very slowly, the hum came back. But it was different now. It was lower. Quieter. Like something listening very hard for footsteps.
