## Chapter 14: Goblin Ears

Brannoc had not slept. Mike could tell. The Guildmaster arrived at the stand-up the next morning with red eyes, a creased tunic, and the bright, fevered energy of a man who had spent all night staring at a number and had finally decided what to do about it.

"*Productivity!*" he announced, before the motto, before the three questions, before the Duke had even reached the window.

The circle blinked at him.

"We have a debt," said Brannoc. He said it the way a man might say *we have a small fire in the kitchen*, if the kitchen was entirely on fire. "A *manageable* debt. Which we will manage. By being more *productive*." He held up his empty mug as if it were a torch. "And to be more productive, we need to *measure* our productivity. So we can see it going *up*!"

Mike, behind his pillar, felt the familiar cold drop in his stomach that always came just before a manager discovered a new number.

"Now," said Brannoc, "*quests completed*. That's what we've been counting. And it's too *slow*. A quest takes days. Weeks! You can't see it go up! What we need," and here his eyes lit with an inspiration that had clearly come to him at about four in the morning, "is something we can *count*. Every *day*. Something *small*."

He let the pause build.

"Goblins," said Brannoc.

The circle murmured. Goblins were a familiar nuisance in the hills above Brindlemoor: small, green, quarrelsome creatures who stole sheep and chickens and the occasional boot, and who had been a low and constant entry on the URGENT pile for as long as anyone could remember.

"From today," said Brannoc, "every adventurer in this guild will be paid a bounty of one silver for every goblin ear they bring in. Proof of work! Something you can hold in your *hand*!" He swept his arm toward the wall beside the purple quest board, where two junior clerks were already hammering up a long wooden board painted with columns. "And *that* is the leaderboard!"

The hall burst into excited chatter. Gareth the Bold was already rolling up his sleeves.

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time until this metric is gamed: 2 days.**

He read it twice. *Two whole days?* He was almost impressed. At Synergex, the sales team had gamed the new dashboard before the meeting announcing it was over. In his head, quietly, a sentence surfaced from another life, printed in blue letters on the side of a coffee mug that a coworker named Priya had carried to every meeting for three years, and set down in front of every manager who ever proposed a new dashboard.

*When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.*

He had always liked that mug. He wished, very much, that he had it now.

---

It took exactly two days. The skill, as always, was right.

The first ears came in that afternoon: three of them, genuine, a little grubby, from a party that had chased off a goblin raid on a turnip farm. Mike counted them, stamped them, paid three silver, and wrote *Brenna's party: 3* on the leaderboard. Brannoc came out of his office, looked at the leaderboard, and rang the gong.

By the morning of the third day, there were forty.

Mike counted them. Then he counted them again. Then he laid them out on his desk in rows and looked at them for a long time, and noticed that a great many of them seemed to be exactly half the size of the others.

"These are halves," he said to the adventurer who had brought them, a cheerful young man named Fitch.

"Two halves," said Fitch, "is two ears."

"Two halves is one ear."

"Says who?"

Mike looked at the bounty notice. It said *one silver per goblin ear*. It did not, he realized, say anything about size.

That afternoon a new notice went up under the bounty notice, in Brannoc's huge slanting hand.

*WHOLE EARS ONLY.*

The next morning, Mike found ears that were whole, plump, faintly shiny, and very slightly warm. When he pressed one with his thumb, it dented and didn't spring back.

"Wax," he said.

"*Goblin-shaped* wax," said the adventurer, with dignity.

The notice grew.

*WHOLE EARS ONLY. NO WAX.*

The next morning, a tall, earnest ranger laid a single long, slender, delicately pointed ear on Mike's desk. It was very clearly not a goblin's.

"That," said Mike, "is an elf's ear."

"It's pointy," said the ranger.

"It's an *elf's*."

The ranger shrugged. "Pointy is pointy."

Mike decided he did not want to know where it had come from. He found out anyway, two days later, when an elf from the tanning district came to the counter with a bandaged head to complain. The elf, it turned out, had sold it. Voluntarily. For two silver. He'd heard there was a market.

The notice grew again.

*GOBLIN EARS ONLY. (No elves.) (No wax.) (Whole.)*

And so it went, day after day. Every morning, the adventurers found a new way to satisfy the rules without doing the job. Every afternoon, Brannoc added a new rule. By the end of the first week, the notice ran to the bottom of the board and had begun creeping down the wall. It had a section on minimum ear length, a section on acceptable shades of green, a section forbidding ears "previously submitted" (after an incident involving the same ear being brought in four times by four different adventurers), and a short final line, added after a particularly bad Thursday, that simply read: *NO ARGUING WITH THE CLERK.*

Mike read the whole notice every morning. It was, he thought, the most perfect piece of documentation he had ever seen. It recorded, in order, every single way the metric had been broken. Nobody upstairs had read it as anything but a list of rules.

And all the while, the leaderboard climbed.

Forty ears a day. Then eighty. Then two hundred. Gareth the Bold took first place on day three and never gave it up. Brannoc rang the gong every afternoon at five, and stood beneath the leaderboard with his hands on his hips, gazing up at the rising columns the way a farmer gazes at a field of ripe wheat.

"*Productivity!*" he would say to whoever was nearest. "Look at it *go*!"

Mike looked at it go. And every afternoon, he took the day's ears down to the Basement, because they had to go somewhere, and he had run out of drawers.

---

It was the cleanliness that caught him in the end.

Goblins in the wild were not clean. They lived in caves and holes and the undersides of bridges, and they washed, as far as anyone knew, only when it rained. The first three ears, from the turnip farm, had been grubby, scratched, and nicked at the edges. They looked like they had been attached to something that had lived a hard life in a hole.

The ears coming in now were spotless. They were plump and smooth and evenly green. Some of them smelled faintly of soap. One of them, Mike could have sworn, had been moisturized.

He took a basket of them down the ladder and set it in front of Pip.

"How many goblins," he asked, "are there in the hills?"

Pip looked at the basket. He looked at Mike. Then he went to a shelf at the back of the Basement and took down a thick, dusty folder labeled *CENSUS (VARIOUS)*, in which, it turned out, he had been keeping track for nineteen years of the population of every creature within a day's ride of Brindlemoor. Footnote: because nobody else was.

He ran his finger down a column. He did some arithmetic on his slate. Then he did it again.

"About six hundred," he said. "Footnote: give or take. Footnote: goblins are hard to count. They hide. And bite."

Mike opened his ledger to the bounty pages and turned it around so Pip could see the total.

Pip looked at it for a long time.

"Mike," he said slowly. "The guild has bought two thousand four hundred goblin ears."

"Yes."

"From six hundred goblins."

"Yes."

"That's four ears per goblin." Pip took off his spectacles. "Footnote: goblins have *two*."

From his desk, without lowering his pamphlet, Durgan said: "Someone's growing them."

---

They went up into the hills the next morning: Mike, Velka, Roderick, Agatha, and Bertram, who carried everything and asked nothing. It took them half a day to find the first farm. After that, finding the others was easy. You just followed the smell of soap.

The first farm was in a sheltered valley above a stream. It had a neat wooden fence, a gate with a latch, and a row of small, clean, whitewashed pens, each with a little straw roof. Inside the pens, on cushions, sat goblins.

There were perhaps forty of them. They did not look like the scrawny, snarling goblins of the hills. They looked well fed. Some were plump. They were sitting on embroidered cushions in the shade of their straw roofs, eating porridge from wooden bowls, and every single one of them had two small, bright green, freshly grown ears.

At the end of the row of pens, nailed to a post, was a slate. On it, in chalk, was a schedule.

*HARVEST: Mondays.*

*FEEDING: 3x daily.*

*CUSHIONS: fluff Wednesdays.*

Roderick read it aloud, slowly. Then he read it again.

"They're *farming* them," he said.

"Goblin ears grow back," said a voice behind them.

They turned. A broad, ruddy farmer in a leather apron was leaning on the gate, chewing a stalk of grass. He was, Mike realized after a moment, one of the guild's own adventurers. A man named Corran. Third place on the leaderboard.

"Takes about a week," said Corran, friendly and completely unashamed. "Like fingernails. You don't hurt 'em, you just trim 'em, like a hedge. Every Monday." He gestured proudly at the pens. "Best-kept goblins in the hills. Clean water. Three meals. Cushions. They've never had it so good."

"Why," said Velka, "are you doing this?"

Corran looked genuinely puzzled by the question. "Bounty's one silver an ear," he said. "Rules say whole ears, no wax, no elves, goblin only." He pointed at the pens. "These are whole, real, goblin ears. Every one of 'em follows every rule on that board." He grinned. "I read it all. Took me an hour."

Mike stood at the gate and looked at the pens, and thought about the notice in the guild hall that ran to the bottom of the board and halfway down the wall. Every rule had been written to stop someone gaming the metric. And here was the place where all of those rules ended up: a way to satisfy every one of them, exactly as written, without ever doing the job they were meant to measure. Not a single goblin in these hills had stopped stealing chickens. They had simply been given cushions, and a schedule.

Then Corran said something that made Sister Agatha go very still.

"'Course, some farms do faster," he said. "Got a fellow over the ridge who hired a cleric. Real healer. Comes up twice a week to see to his goblins, and every time she does, *poof*." He made a little gesture by his ear. "Back on in an instant. He's harvesting twice a week, now."

There was a long silence.

Mike turned slowly to look at Agatha.

She was standing very still beside the gate, in her white robes with the circle-and-arrow of Saint Redundia on the breast. Her round, kind face had gone pale.

"A man came to me," she said quietly. "A fortnight ago. He said he ran a clinic in the hills. For *patients*. He said they had... ear trouble. He paid well." Her voice wavered. "I go up on Tuesdays and Fridays. I lay hands on them, and Saint Redundia restores them to how they were three days ago." She swallowed. "Before their... before their ear trouble."

She looked at the plump, cushioned goblins in their pens.

"They said it was for *patients*," said Sister Agatha.

Nobody said anything. Velka put a hand on her arm.

In the nearest pen, a small goblin with a bowl of porridge looked up at Agatha with bright, interested yellow eyes, and waved.

---

It was the cushions, in the end, that did it.

You cannot keep forty goblins in neat whitewashed pens, side by side, with three meals a day and nothing to do and a very clear understanding of exactly who was profiting from their ears, without them starting to talk to one another. And there were a great many farms. And the goblins, it turned out, talked across the fences.

The delegation arrived at the guild hall on the fifteenth day of the bounty.

There were six of them. They came in through the new front door in single file, in clean, plain clothes, each with two neat green ears, and they walked across the hall in perfect silence, past the leaderboard, past the purple quest board, past the gong, and stopped in front of the counter.

The goblin at the front was small, even for a goblin. He wore a neat brown waistcoat, a pair of small round spectacles, and an expression of complete and patient calm. Under one arm he carried a folder.

"Good morning," he said to Mike. His voice was quiet and precise. "My name is Nettle. I am the shop steward of Local 47 of the Amalgamated Goblins of the Brindlemoor Hills." He laid a sheet of paper on the counter. "We represent the goblins of the eleven farms. We would like to discuss terms."

Mike looked at the paper. It was a list of demands. It was very neatly written. It was, he noticed, numbered, with sub-points, and the sub-points were lettered.

> *1. A share of the bounty: one copper in every silver, paid to the Local.*
>
> *2. Humane harvesting conditions, as follows: (a) no Mondays; (b) warm hands; (c) advance notice.*
>
> *3. A pension.*
>
> *4. A dental plan.*
>
> *5. The return of all previously harvested ears.*

Mike read it twice. Then he looked up at Nettle, who was waiting with his hands folded.

"Can I ask," said Mike, "why a dental plan?"

Nettle smiled for the first time. It showed a great many small sharp teeth, several of which were clearly in need of a dental plan.

"It's the porridge," he said.

---

The negotiation took place in the council room. Brannoc sat at the head of the table, with Priscilla on his left and Gareth on his right. Nettle sat alone at the other end, with his folder, his spectacles, and five silent goblins standing behind him in a row. Mike stood at the side of the room with the ledger, because someone had to know the numbers.

It did not go well.

It did not go well from the start, because Nettle had read everything. He had read the bounty notice. He had read every rule on the notice, all the way down the wall. He had read the guild's charter, and two old quest contracts, and, somehow, a copy of the stand-up schedule. Every time Brannoc made a claim, Nettle quietly opened his folder, turned to the relevant page, and read it back to him.

"The bounty," said Brannoc, "is a reward for *heroism*—"

"The bounty notice," said Nettle, turning a page, "says *one silver per goblin ear*. It makes no mention of heroism." He looked up. "Our members produce the ears. We are requesting a share of the proceeds."

"You don't *produce* them, you *grow* them!"

"In my understanding," said Nettle gently, "that is what producing means."

Priscilla tried. Priscilla was wonderful at this sort of thing. She leaned forward with her brightest smile and promised Nettle a pension, a dental plan, a lovely new set of cushions, and "a real *partnership* going forward." Nettle wrote every promise down, carefully, then looked up and asked her when each one would be delivered, and how, and with what money, and Priscilla's smile developed a small crack in the corner.

Gareth said "out of scope" four times.

And then, about an hour in, in the middle of one of Brannoc's speeches about the importance of productivity, Nettle did something nobody expected.

He closed his folder. He took off his small round spectacles, folded them, and laid them on top of the folder. And he looked down the long table at the Guildmaster with an expression of honest, mild curiosity.

"Forgive me," said Nettle. "I have a question. It may be a foolish one."

Brannoc waved a hand. "Ask."

"What is the ear *for*?"

The room went quiet.

Brannoc blinked. "What do you mean, *for*?"

"The ear," said Nettle patiently. "The bounty. Two thousand four hundred ears. What were they *for*? What were you trying to find out by counting them?"

"*Productivity*," said Brannoc, as if it were obvious. "To measure productivity."

Nettle nodded slowly. He seemed to consider this with great care.

"Productivity," he said, "at what?"

The silence went on.

Brannoc opened his mouth. He closed it. He looked at Priscilla. Priscilla looked at her notes. Mike watched the question travel down the table like a slow, cold draft, and watched each of them reach for the answer and find that there was nothing there.

It was Gareth who answered, in the end. He had been thinking hard. Mike could see him doing it.

"...Getting ears," said Gareth.

Nettle looked at him for a long moment with something like pity.

Then he put his spectacles back on and opened his folder.

"Before we continue," he said, "I'd like to ask your clerk a question." He turned to Mike. "Honestly, please. How long will the guild actually need goblin ears?"

Everyone looked at Mike. Brannoc's face was a silent, urgent plea. *Say forever. Say the program is working.*

Mike asked the skill. Then he told the truth.

"The guild doesn't need any ears at all," he said. "It never did. The bounty was supposed to reduce goblin raids. There have been the same number of raids every week since it started. Nothing has changed except the number on the board."

There was a pause. Brannoc made a small strangled sound.

Nettle studied Mike over his spectacles for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way a man nods when someone has told him the truth for the first time in a very long meeting.

"Thank you," he said. "That's very helpful." He closed his folder and stood up. Behind him, the five goblins straightened. "Local 47 is now on strike. Effective immediately, no member of the Local will grow, provide, or permit the harvesting of a single ear, until our demands are met."

He turned to go. Then he paused, and turned back.

"And we'll be wanting the ears back," he said. "Item five."

---

The ears were in the storeroom.

Mike hadn't realized how many until he went to find them. He opened the storeroom door at the back of the guild hall, behind the kitchens, and stood there in the doorway, holding his lantern up, for some time.

There were barrels. Big ones. Wine barrels, salted-fish barrels, and two large rain barrels borrowed from the stables, all lined up along the walls of the little stone room, all with their lids off. Every one of them was full to the top with small, green, perfectly clean, faintly soap-scented goblin ears.

Brannoc came and stood beside him in the doorway. The Guildmaster looked at the barrels for a long time. Then he let out a long, heavy breath through his nose, and Mike braced himself.

"Well," said Brannoc at last. He nodded slowly at the barrels, as if they were some sort of comfort. "At least we've still *got* the ears."

That evening, Mike went to the Registrar and opened Malphus's ledger to the newest page, where a fresh entry had appeared in neat red ink that afternoon, all by itself.

*Bounties, goblin (ears): 4,000 silver.*

He looked at it for a while. Then he asked the skill one last question about the bounty.

**Actual goblin threats eliminated: 0.**

Four thousand silver. Borrowed, at compounding interest, from a devil. For barrels.

Beside the purple quest board, the leaderboard had stopped. Nobody had taken it down. The columns had frozen at the exact moment of the strike, with Gareth the Bold at the very top: *1,104 ears*. Gareth had already begun referring to himself, at the stand-up and elsewhere, as *the all-time ear champion*. Mike suspected it would end up in his Hero Moment by Friday.

---

The Rusty Flagon was on the corner of Cooper's Lane and the square, a low, smoky, comfortable place with a sagging ceiling, a fire that smoked, and ale that was better than it had any right to be. Mike had never been inside before. Neither, it turned out, had Velka.

They went there that night to debrief the quest. That was what they had both said. *Debrief*. A quest debrief, between the guild's senior clerk and one of its mages, about the goblin farms, and Agatha, and what had happened at the negotiation. A professional conversation, over a professional ale. Velka had made a point of using the word *debrief* three times on the walk over.

And when they sat down at a small table in the corner, she took a folded sheet of paper out of her coat pocket, smoothed it flat, and laid it on the table between them.

It was an agenda.

> *1. Goblins.*
>
> *2. Agatha.*
>
> *3. Any other business.*

Mike looked at the agenda. Then he looked at her.

"So it's definitely not a date," he said.

"It has an *agenda*," said Velka, as if that settled everything.

The tavern keeper, a large, kindly woman with flour on her arms, came by with two ales. She looked at the two of them. She looked at the agenda. Then, without a word, she went to the bar, came back with a small, stubby candle in a clay dish, lit it, set it in the middle of their table, and walked away.

Neither of them blew it out.

They got through *Goblins* reasonably well. They got through *Agatha*, who had gone home and was, Velka said, very quiet, and had asked Saint Redundia for guidance, and had not heard back. Then they talked about other things, things that were not on the agenda: about Tanner's Row, and about the guide, and about Velka's grandmother, who had taught her Illumination 1.0 when she was six and had never once let her cast it in the house. Somewhere in there, the candle burned down a good inch, and their ales were refilled twice, and neither of them looked at the agenda again. They never reached item three.

It was Velka who noticed Brannoc first.

"Mike," she said quietly. "Look."

The Guildmaster was at the bar. He was alone, on a stool too small for him, with a tankard in front of him and two more empty beside it. His big shoulders were hunched. He was talking. Not to anyone in particular, just to the room, in the low, aggrieved, rumbling voice of a large man who has had a bad week and three ales.

"...a *debt*," Brannoc was saying. "Two hundred years of it. Not *my* fault. Moats. *Moats*! And then the goblins go and *unionize*. Who unionizes? *Goblins*. And the clerk. Clerk stands there and tells the goblin, right to my face, that we don't *need* the ears. After all I've done for that boy—"

Mike went very still.

Behind the bar, polishing a tankard, was a waiter. He was young, slim, and neat, with glossy dark hair combed back from a smooth, eager face. A white towel was draped over one arm. He was listening to Brannoc with the full, total, shining attention of a man who has just heard the most fascinating thing in the world. And every few seconds, he nodded, and refilled Brannoc's tankard, and said something back.

Mike leaned forward a little to hear.

"...and the *goblins*," Brannoc was saying.

"Your key stakeholders," said the waiter warmly. "Of course."

Brannoc paused. "My *what*?"

"Stakeholders, Guildmaster. Everyone with a stake in your outcomes. The goblins are clearly *very* invested."

Brannoc considered this. Something in his face shifted slightly. "Stakeholders," he repeated. "Yes." He took a drink. "And all those blasted *ears*—"

"A misaligned output pipeline," said the waiter sympathetically, wiping the bar. "You were optimizing for the wrong deliverable. It happens to the best visionaries."

"*Visionaries*," said Brannoc slowly.

"And the debt." The waiter leaned closer, lowering his voice. "Guildmaster, I have to be honest with you. From where I'm standing, that isn't a debt at all." He set down the tankard. "That's a *liquidity opportunity*."

Brannoc stared at him. His face, which had been gray and slumped and hopeless a moment ago, began slowly to fill with color, like a bladder filling with air. Mike watched it happen. He watched the shoulders straighten and the chin come up, and the old, bright, dangerous light come back into the Guildmaster's eyes.

"A liquidity opportunity," Brannoc breathed.

In the corner, Mike felt the back of his neck go cold. It was a very specific coldness. He had felt it twice before in his life. Once in a conference room in Raleigh, the day Chad had introduced the new VP of Digital Transformation. And once, a few weeks before that, at a company offsite, when he'd seen that same VP at a hotel bar, listening, nodding, and repeating everything Chad said back to him, only shinier.

Velka was watching his face. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing," said Mike. "Everything. I don't know yet."

At the bar, the waiter folded his white towel neatly over his arm. He leaned across the bar toward the Guildmaster, close and confiding, and his eager, shining face was lit by the fire like a lamp.

"Guildmaster," said Rupert Spindlewick, "have you ever considered that what this guild really needs is a *strategy*?"

On the little table between Mike and Velka, the candle flickered, and leaned, very slightly, toward the north.
