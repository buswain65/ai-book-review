# Chapter 26: The Retrospective

It was Friday.

Mike woke up on the morning after the door with that word already in his head, the way you wake up with a song. *Friday.* The first one. The first Friday under the new terms, the day Malphus had written into the contract in red ink: no new work. Fix one old thing. Pay down the debt.

He lay in bed with his boots on the floor beside it and thought about which old thing to fix.

There were a lot of candidates. The armory roof. The OMNIFILE's fanfare. The purple curtain. But the thing he kept coming back to wasn't a thing at all. It was the last eight years. Everything that had gone wrong, in order, and nobody, in all that time, ever once sitting down together and asking *why*.

In his old life, they'd had a name for that kind of meeting. They had scheduled one after every disaster at Synergex, and every single one had turned into an hour of people pointing at each other, until Chad Pemberton stood up at the end and said that the real lesson was *communication*, and everyone went back to their desks, and nothing changed, and three months later it happened again.

But Mike had read about the other kind. The kind where you weren't allowed to point.

He got up and put his boots on, and went to the guild, and up to the war room, and took down the board's last sheet of rules from the door. In its place, he pinned a new one.

> *RETROSPECTIVE. TEN O'CLOCK.*
>
> *WHAT HAPPENED, NOT WHO.*
>
> *NO GONG.*
>
> *NO BLAME.*

---

Rupert was the first to read it.

He came up the stairs at a quarter to ten, without his belt, which was still hanging on the hat stand downstairs, where Gareth had left it, and stopped in front of the door, and read the sign. His face lit up like a lamp.

"*Blameless*," he breathed. "Oh, Mike. Mike, this is *wonderful*." He turned to Mike with shining eyes. "Psychological safety! I've championed this from day one. It's been at the very core of the Ink Office's values. This is exactly the culture I've been trying to build."

"Great," said Mike.

"No, really. I'm so glad you're finally on board." Rupert squeezed his shoulder. "And I'd love to help. Actually, would it be all right if I facilitated? I've brought some materials."

He held up a sheet of paper. Stuck to it, in neat rows, were several dozen small yellow squares of paper, each with a dab of glue on the back. Somebody had written on every single one of them, in Tolliver's large, neat handwriting. Mike leaned in to read one.

> *ROOT CAUSE: COMMUNICATION.*

He read the next one. It said the same thing. So did the one after that. So did all of them.

"We pre-populated," said Rupert. "To save time."

"Thanks," said Mike. "I'll run it."

They came in one by one. Durgan and Pip, from the Basement. Velka, with her hat. Sister Agatha, with her bag. Sir Roderick, still faintly gray with crypt dust, with a long roll of paper under his arm. Tam, with three pens. Priscilla, with her pink notebook clutched to her chest. Gareth the Bold, who had not been invited, but who had come anyway, because he had heard it was a meeting and that there would be chairs. The three Champions, in a row, looking nervous. Osric, who had not gone back into his stack since the audit, and who came up the stairs one at a time, very slowly, with his green ledger under his arm.

Hesper Vane came, with her stamp and her wax and her nail file, to witness and notarize, and sat down at the end of the table and began to file her nails at once.

And Malphus came. Mike had sent him a card, inviting him as a neutral witness, because he had seen everything and blamed nobody, and because, if Mike was honest, it felt right that he should be there. He came up the stairs with his red ledger under one arm and, in his other hand, a large brown teapot, which he set on the table without comment, and poured himself a cup.

Brannoc came last.

He came in very quietly, for Brannoc, and did not stand at the head of the table. He walked all the way around it, to the very back of the room, to the chair in the corner under the window, the one nobody ever sat in, and sat in it. He folded his arms. He had his empty mug in one hand. He looked at the floor.

He didn't say anything.

Everyone noticed. Nobody said anything about it.

Mike went to the front of the room. He asked the skill how long this was going to take.

**Estimated retrospective length: 2 hours.**

Sister Agatha raised her hand.

"Before we start, dear," she said kindly, to the room at large. "If anyone would rather not remember the last week, I'd be very happy to help. Just three days, mind. But it might take the edge off."

There was a pause. Everyone looked at each other. It had, after all, been a very long week.

Nobody took her up on it.

Mike noticed, though, that Rupert looked at Sister Agatha for a long moment before he looked away. He looked at her the way a man looks at a lifeboat.

"I can *absolutely*—" said Priscilla suddenly, from nowhere, and then stopped, and looked down at her hands, and folded them. "Sorry. Habit." She took a breath. "It's Friday."

"It's Friday," agreed Mike. "This counts."

He picked up the chalk.

---

The idea was simple. Mike explained it as plainly as he could.

"We're going to put everything that happened up on the wall," he said. "In order. By date. Starting with the door. For every single thing, we write three lines. *What happened.* *What we knew, before it happened.* And *what we decided.*" He paused. "That's it. No names. Nobody is on trial. We're not here to find out who. We're here to find out *why*. Because if it's somebody's fault, then the fix is to get rid of somebody. But if it's *why*, then the fix is to change something, and then it doesn't happen again."

"Love it," said Rupert warmly. "Love it, love it."

And so they began.

Mike and Pip did the writing. Pip had brought the guild's records up from the archive in a long line of boxes, and Mike pulled them out one at a time and read them, and Pip wrote the three lines on a card in his small, neat hand, and pinned it to the long wall of the war room, in order, left to right.

*The front door is moved to the other wall. Known: it was load-bearing; Durgan objected. Decided: move it anyway.*

*The demon stirs. Known: the Basement had warned about the door. Decided: the Basement's wards are fragile.*

*The Basement is put on a Performance Improvement Scroll.*

They worked their way along the wall. And Rupert walked along behind them.

He did it very casually. He had his sheet of yellow squares in one hand. Every time Pip pinned up a card, Rupert stepped up beside it, peeled off one of his little squares, and stuck it on the bottom corner, with a gentle, helpful pat.

*ROOT CAUSE: COMMUNICATION.*

He did it on the door card. He did it on the demon card. He did it on the scroll card. He did it on the next six cards, all the way along the wall, until every single event on it had a small yellow square on the corner saying the same word.

Mike let him finish.

Then he went back to the beginning of the wall, to the door card, and peeled the yellow square off. And then the next one. And the next. He walked the whole length of the wall, peeling them off, one by one, without saying a word. When he got to the end, he had a small stack of them in his hand. He held them out to Rupert.

"Communication isn't a cause," said Mike. "It's a word you put on a cause so you don't have to say what it is."

Rupert looked at the stack of squares. He opened his mouth. Then he closed it again and took them.

Behind him, at the end of the table, very quietly, Hesper Vane stopped filing her nails for just a moment, and then went on.

---

They kept going.

The trouble with the guild's records, Mike discovered, was not that they were bad. His own records were very good. The trouble was that there were long stretches where nobody had written anything down at all. Whole weeks when things had happened, important things, that had left no mark on paper anywhere. Nights when the bell had rung and nobody logged it. Changes that somebody had just *done*, on a whim, without telling anybody.

Every time they hit one of those gaps, Pip went very still. Then he opened his slate, and consulted something, and wrote the card anyway, with a date, and pinned it up.

The third time he did it, Velka leaned over to see what he was reading from.

"Pip," she said slowly. "Is that the demon's list?"

Pip looked up. He had the expression of a gnome who has been caught doing something he is very proud of.

"I copied it down," he said. "In the crypt. While it was reading. All of it." He tapped the slate. "It's remarkably complete. Look." He pointed. "We didn't have a date for when the OMNIFILE started playing the fanfare at night. The demon did. *Third night after installation, two hours past midnight, fanfare, nine bars, off-key.*" He turned the slate around so she could see. "It has better dates than we do. It has better dates than *anyone* does."

"You're using the *demon* as a primary source," said Velka.

"It's the one stakeholder nobody ever consulted," said Pip, with dignity. "And it kept the best notes."

There was a short silence while everyone considered this. Then Durgan grunted. It was, Mike knew from long experience, the grunt of agreement.

Sir Roderick stood up and unrolled the long sheet of paper he had brought under his arm. It was the map of the crypt under the guild, copied from his chalk on the walls onto paper, by lamplight, overnight. Every room. Every stair. *ROOM 3: Skeleton (friendly?).* *ROOM 4: Skeleton (not friendly). Avoid.* He pinned it to the wall beside the cards about the seal, very carefully, and stood back to look at it, and Mike thought he had never seen a man look so quietly proud of anything.

The cards kept coming. The wall filled. They reached the end of the long wall and turned the corner and filled the short one. They reached the window, and went around it, and kept going.

And then they ran out of wall.

Pip stood holding a card about the night bell, looking at the last few inches of space. Then he looked up.

"Ceiling," said Durgan.

So they went onto the ceiling. Two of the Duke's honor guard, who had been loitering at the bottom of the stairs out of habit, still waiting for someone to tell them to do something, were sent for ladders. They came back with them, very pleased, and held them steady while Pip climbed up with a fistful of cards and pins and kept going, right across the ceiling of the war room, so that by eleven o'clock the history of the last eight years ran all the way around the walls and up over everyone's heads.

It was then, while everyone was looking up, that Osric stood.

He did it very slowly. He had not said anything all morning. He had sat at the end of the table beside Tam, with his green ledger, writing small notes, and nobody had paid him much attention. Now he got up out of his chair, holding on to the edge of the table, and walked very slowly across the room, past the wall of cards, all the way to the far left-hand end, to the place where the timeline started, with the card about the door.

He didn't put anything after the door. He went to the space *before* it.

He took a pin from the box. From inside the cover of his green ledger, he took out a card. It was very old. It was yellowed and soft at the edges, and the ink on it had gone brown. It had clearly been written a long time ago, and kept in that ledger ever since, waiting.

He pinned it up, at the very beginning, before everything else.

> *Edwin left. Nobody asked why.*

The room went quiet.

Everyone looked at the card. Then everyone looked at Osric, standing beside it, very small, with his blue-black ink-stained hands folded in front of him.

Mike had heard Edwin's name a thousand times. *Edwin knew.* Edwin had been the clerk before him. Edwin had left a single scrawled page and quit, and Mike had walked in on his first day to find nobody who knew anything, and nothing written down. In eight years, he had never once wondered why. Nobody had. That was, he supposed, the whole point of the card.

"Osric," he said gently. "Why did he leave?"

Osric looked at the card for a long moment.

"Nobody listened," said Osric.

Then he walked slowly back to his chair, and sat down, and opened his ledger, and didn't say anything else.

Mike stood looking at the card for a long time. He thought about a forge on Anvil Lane. He thought about two words on a reference. *He listens.*

At the back of the room, under the window, Brannoc was looking at the card too.

---

They read the wall from left to right.

That was the second half of it. Putting it up was one thing. Reading it was another. They started at the door card and moved along, slowly, one event at a time, and at every single one, Mike asked the room the same question.

"What did we know, before it happened?"

And again and again, it turned out, the answer was the same. They had known. Somebody had known. Somebody had always known, and had said so, and had written it down, and there it was, on the wall, in a card beside the disaster: an estimate. A warning. A list.

*The Contraption fails in the river. Known: the Known Issues list, eleven pages, item three: promise rune fails if the client reads the contract carefully.*

*The Big Rewrite migrates only some of the records. Known: a formal objection, two signatures.*

*The crypt runs late. Known: an estimate of nineteen days.*

The card beside it said *Decided: fourteen.*

Nobody said whose decisions they were. That was the rule. But the cards said *what was decided*, and the records said *by which office*, and the dates said *when*, and as the morning went on and they moved along the wall, everybody in the room began, slowly, to see the shape.

There had been a lot of bulletins, over the past few months. Mike had filed every one of them. They said that this failure had been the result of a clerk's poor estimates, and that failure had been the result of a clerk's failure to escalate, and the other had been the result of legacy documentation, which was *on you, Mike*. And there, on the wall, right beside each of those failures, with dates, was a decision. And every single one of those decisions had been made, and signed, and stamped, by the Office of the Chief Ink Officer.

Nobody said it out loud. Nobody had to.

Rupert, who had gone gradually quieter as they moved along the wall, cleared his throat.

"I think," he said, "we need to be really, really careful here not to assign *blame*."

Velka didn't even look at him.

"Nobody has," she said.

Rupert opened his mouth. Then he looked at the wall. Then he closed his mouth again.

At the end of the table, Malphus set down his teacup.

"If it would help," he said pleasantly, "for completeness," and he opened his red ledger, and took out a thin folder, and laid it on the table. It was the folder from the audit, the one with one word written on the front of it in red ink. *FICTION.*

Pip, without being asked, took the bulletins out of the folder one by one, and pinned each of them up on the wall, right beside the event it described.

*CONVERGENCE CHAMPIONS*, beside the meeting where everything had been piled onto one day.

*HISTORIC DUKE ALIGNMENT*, beside the card that said *Mike told the Duke no.*

And then, because it was the newest and had gone up on the front board only that morning, *DOOR 2.0 LAUNCH*, beside the card that said, simply, *The whole guild carried the door back to where it had always been.*

> *DOOR 2.0 LAUNCH*
>
> *The Office of the Chief Ink Officer is proud to announce the successful launch of DOOR 2.0, a legacy-informed entryway solution leveraging heritage architecture for next-generation threshold performance.*

Somebody at the back of the room made a sound that was very nearly a laugh, and turned it into a cough.

Then Pip pinned up Form E-1. *Escalation of Known Issues.* With Mike's note clipped to the corner, giving the date it was invented, which was the day after the failure it was supposedly meant to prevent. And then, beside it, a sheet from Mike's own drawer: an exit survey, from a resignation, with a question on it that said *Please rate the Chief Ink Officer's leadership*, and a number five that had been circled before anyone was asked, firmly, twice.

Rupert stopped talking.

He didn't say anything else for a long time.

---

When they reached the crypt, Gareth was looking at the wall with a puzzled frown.

It was the old crypt, the Sunken Crypt, from Mike's first year as senior clerk, and it took up nearly a whole section of the wall by itself. Nineteen days. Fourteen days promised. A tribunal. Twelve more adventurers sent in to speed it up. A collapsed entrance. And, on day nineteen, exactly as estimated, a clear crypt, and a guild that called it a failure, and a medal.

"What did we know," said Mike, "beforehand?"

He went around the room. Durgan had known about the ceiling. Pip had known about the water. Mike had known about everything, and written it down.

Then Mike turned to Gareth, as he turned to everyone, and asked him the same question.

"Gareth. What did you know, before?"

Gareth the Bold thought about it. He thought about it very hard. Mike could see him thinking: his brow furrowed, and his lips moved slightly, and he stared up at the ceiling, where the timeline had gone.

"Nothing," he said at last, honestly. "Nobody ever tells me anything." He shrugged his enormous shoulders. "They just give me medals."

Nobody laughed. It was too true.

Gareth looked at the wall for a while longer, at the part about the crypt. At the dates. At the card that said *Known: an estimate of nineteen days*, and the one beside it that said *Crypt cleared, day nineteen.* At the one that said *Delays: two, caused by an adventurer going off alone to fight something.* And at the small card at the end, in Mike's handwriting: *Medal awarded for heroics.*

He looked at it for a very long time.

Then, slowly, he reached up to his chest, where the medal from the crypt still hung, polished and bright, on its ribbon. He unpinned it. He held it in his big hand and looked down at it.

He walked across the room, past Pip and the cards and the ladders, to where Durgan was sitting with his arms folded, and he held the medal out.

Durgan looked at it. He looked up at Gareth. He looked at the medal again.

"Hm," said Durgan.

And he took it, and pinned it on his own chest, beside the stone dust.

Gareth went back to his chair and sat down, looking lighter, somehow, than Mike had ever seen him.

Halfway through the next section, Malphus picked up his large brown teapot, stood, and walked down the length of the table, all the way to the back of the room, to the chair under the window. He stopped beside Brannoc. He didn't say anything. He just tipped the pot, and filled Brannoc's empty mug to the brim with hot tea.

Then he went back to his seat.

Brannoc looked down at his mug for a long time. For as long as Mike had known him, he had carried that mug, everywhere, empty, because Wren Halloway carried a mug and Wren Halloway was very successful.

Now it was full.

Brannoc held it in both hands. He didn't drink it. He didn't say anything. But he didn't put it down, either.

---

The next box was the oldest one.

Tam opened it. It was from the very bottom of the archive, and the very beginning of Mike's time at the guild: his first week, eight years ago, when he was twelve years old. It was full of the small, careful things that a twelve-year-old clerk files in his first week, because he doesn't yet know which things don't matter: a list of the Registrar's moods, a diagram of the bell rope, a note about which drawer stuck.

And near the top, two sheets of paper, written in a child's careful handwriting, addressed to the Guildmaster.

> *Suggestion: write down all of the Registrar's rules in one place, so new clerks don't have to guess.*
>
> *Suggestion: keep one list of all the quests, ranked by what matters most, so everyone works on the same thing.*
>
> *— Mike Dobbins, junior clerk*

Underneath, in a different hand, in large confident letters: *Noted. — B.*

Tam pinned them up on the wall, at the far left, right after Osric's card about Edwin.

And then, beside them, without saying anything, he pinned up the program from the day the Champions had been celebrated, the day Rupert had first arrived. The day they had presented, to the whole guild, to cheering and applause and a single medal, the Ink Office's groundbreaking new *Ink Strategy*.

There were two items on it.

*Write the Registrar's rules down.*

*Keep one ranked quest list.*

Word for word.

The three roommates looked at the two sheets side by side for a long time. They looked at the date on the first one, eight years old. They looked at the handwriting, which was a child's. Mike watched them. He saw Dex's face, which was usually so cheerful and so blank, slowly change.

"We never knew," said Dex quietly, "what *convergence* meant."

At the end of the row, very slowly, Bramble lifted his lute, and played three descending notes.

*Wah. Wah. Waaah.*

Everyone in the room turned to look at him.

Bramble looked down at his lute, as if it had done that on its own.

Mike stared at him. It was the sad trombone. It was, note for note, the exact sad trombone from every game show and every cartoon and every terrible office party of his entire first life. He had not heard it in twenty years. For one wild, dizzy second, he looked at Bramble's round, mild, blinking face and wondered, very seriously, whether Bramble was from Raleigh too.

Bramble blinked at him. He was not from Raleigh. He just had a very good ear for a feeling.

The room was quiet again.

"We just wrote down what he told us," said Tolliver, after a while. He said it quietly, looking at the floor. He wasn't the sharp, confident Tolliver who had invented Form E-1 in front of the whole guild. He was just a young man who had been handed a job he didn't understand and told he was brilliant at it. "Rupert said it was ours. He said we'd thought of it." He looked at Mike. "We didn't, did we."

Mike could feel the room waiting. It would have been easy, right then. Everyone was looking at the three of them. Everyone knew. All it would take was one sentence, the kind of sentence the guild had said to *him* so many times. *That's on you.*

He didn't say it.

He asked the only question that a retrospective was ever really for.

"What would have helped?"

Tolliver looked up, surprised. He thought about it. He thought about it properly, for a long time, the way nobody had ever asked him to think about anything.

"Training," he said.

It was, Mike thought, the truest thing anyone had said all day.

"I'll teach you," said Tam. He said it so quickly that he seemed surprised himself. "The Registrar, I mean. All the rules. It's not hard. You just have to know them." He went pink. "If you want."

Tolliver looked at the thirteen-year-old. Then he nodded.

"Yes," he said. "Please."

And Bramble, who had been sitting very still with his lute on his knee, suddenly began to play something. A little tune. Bright and simple. And then, to everyone's surprise, he began to sing it, quietly, in a pleasant tenor voice:

> *Black ink only, knock three times,*
>
> *never on its left side,*
>
> *nothing seven before noon,*
>
> *and if it hums, step back...*

It was the Registrar's rules. All of them, in order. Set to a tune that you could not get out of your head.

Tam's mouth dropped open. He grabbed a pen and started writing down the words.

It was, Mike realized, the first good idea any of the roommates had ever had. It was genuinely, practically, actually useful. In a week, every junior clerk in the guild would be humming it.

He wrote a card for it, and pinned it up at the end of the wall: *Song (Bramble). Useful.*

---

There was one section left.

It was the seal. It ran along the top of the window and up onto the ceiling, where Pip had had to stand on the very top step of a ladder to pin the last cards. Nineteen years of it. Hundreds of patches, each with a date. A night the demon had stirred. A Performance Improvement Scroll. And five small cards in a row, each one saying the same thing: *Scroll, red ribbon, thrown down the Basement ladder.*

At the very end, in a different color of ink, was the last card. Pip had written it the night before.

*Root cause: the seal was anchored to the front door. The door was moved. Found in: a story about a goat.*

Mike stood under it and looked up.

"Let's do this one properly," he said. "Five whys."

Nobody knew what that meant, so he explained. You started with what happened. And you asked *why*. And then you asked *why* about the answer. And you kept asking, five times, until you got to the bottom of it, to the place where the thing that broke wasn't a thing anymore, but a habit.

"Why did the seal fail?" said Mike.

"Door moved," said Durgan.

Mike wrote it on the board. *1. The door was moved.*

"Why was the door moved?"

Nobody answered.

The silence went on for a moment. And in that silence, every head in the room turned, not quite all the way, not enough to be rude, toward the chair in the corner under the window, where Brannoc sat with his full mug of tea, looking at the floor.

He didn't say anything.

But everyone heard it anyway.

Mike wrote: *2. For a quick win.*

"Why did nobody know the door held the seal?"

"Nobody wrote it down," said Pip.

*3. It wasn't written down.*

"Why wasn't it written down?"

Pip looked at his slate. He read from it, in the precise voice he used for footnotes. "Nobody asked the one person who knew." He paused. "*See also: Bettany, goat.*"

*4. Nobody asked the person who knew.*

"Why," said Mike, "did nobody ask?"

There was a long pause. Then Velka spoke. She was sitting with her feet up on the chair in front of her and her hat pulled low, and she didn't look up when she said it.

"Nobody asks the Basement," said Velka.

The room was very quiet.

Mike wrote it on the board, under the other four.

*5. Nobody asks the Basement.*

He stepped back and looked at it. And it was there, all of it, in five lines, from top to bottom. Nineteen years. Five scrolls. A demon. A door. And at the very bottom of all of it, not a broken rune or a bad patch or a fragile ward, but a habit. A simple, ordinary habit. *Nobody asks the Basement.*

Pip raised his hand.

"Could we do a sixth why?" he said hopefully.

The entire room groaned.

Pip lowered his hand.

And then Brannoc stood up.

He did it slowly. He set his mug of tea down very carefully on the windowsill. Then he walked, without a word, from the chair at the back of the room, all the way down the side of the long table, past Velka and Tam and the roommates and Malphus, to the front, under the ceiling, where the cards about the seal were.

Everyone watched him.

He reached up and took down the card that said *Performance Improvement Scroll*. Behind it, pinned to the wall, was the scroll itself: the real one, the one that had been sitting in the Basement since the door was moved, with Durgan's name on it. He took that down too.

Then he turned to Pip. He held out his hand.

Pip understood. He reached into his satchel and took out the five small scrolls, tied with red ribbon, that he had brought up from the drawer marked *MIKE*, for the record. He put them into Brannoc's hand.

Brannoc looked at them all for a moment. Six scrolls. Then, one by one, slowly and carefully, he tore each of them in half.

The sound was very loud in the quiet room.

When he was done, he let the pieces fall to the floor. Then he turned around, and walked all the way back down the side of the table, to his chair in the corner under the window, and sat down. He picked up his mug of tea.

He still hadn't said anything.

Durgan looked down at the torn pieces on the floor for a long time. Then he looked down at the medal on his chest. Then he looked at the pieces again.

"Hm," said Durgan.

It was, Mike thought, the longest speech Durgan had ever made.

---

They wrote what they would change.

It fit on one page. Mike had thought it would be longer. But when you took all the cards on all the walls and the whole ceiling and asked, of each one, *what would have stopped this*, the answers kept coming back to the same few things. So they wrote those down, and only those.

> *1. Two people read every contract before it is signed.*
>
> *2. The front door does not move.*
>
> *3. Fridays are for paying down debt.*
>
> *4. Ask the Basement first.*

That was all.

Mike read it out loud. Nobody added anything. Nobody suggested a fifth item, or a sixth, or a float. They just looked at it, and nodded, and that was the retrospective.

Except for one thing.

Rupert stood up.

He did it with his usual smile, the warm, confident one, but it seemed to Mike to be sitting on his face a little loosely now, like a coat that had been made for someone else. He cleared his throat.

"I just want to say," said Rupert, "that this has been an *incredible* journey."

He did not apologize. Mike had not, if he was honest, expected him to. Nobody in the room had.

"And it's with mixed emotions," Rupert went on, "that I share some news. I've been offered an exciting new role. As Chief Ink Officer." He paused for effect. "At Quint & Daughters Automata."

There was a pause.

"The people who make the OMNIFILE," said Pip faintly.

"An incredible organization," said Rupert. "Real *visionaries*. And I'm thrilled to be bringing everything I've learned here to the next chapter." He smiled around the room. "I'll be giving two weeks' notice, of course. To ensure an orderly transition."

Mike asked the skill. He couldn't help it.

**Estimated time until Quint & Daughters regrets this: 3 weeks, 2 days.**

He looked at the number for a while. Three weeks, two days. He knew that number. He had seen it once before, a long time ago, in a forge on Anvil Lane, the first time Fennick came through the door in his velvet hat with an order for twenty swords. *Estimated time until those swords break: 3 weeks, 2 days.*

He didn't say anything. He just wrote it down.

Rupert had already turned to the wall. He had a sheet of paper in his hand, and a pin, and before anyone could stop him, he had pinned it up, right at the very end of the timeline, after the last card.

> *ON TO NEW CHAPTERS*
>
> *After an incredible journey, I'm humbled to share that I'll be moving on. I'm so proud of what WE built here together, and so grateful for every learning along the way. Excited for what's next!*
>
> *— R. Spindlewick*

It did not mention the door. It did not mention the demon, or the seal, or the audit, or the bells, or the belts, or the Duke, or a single thing that had happened. It sat at the end of the long wall of cards like a cheerful little flag on top of a landslide.

Malphus stood up. He walked over to the wall. He read it, front and back, the way he read everything. Then, without a word, and without anyone asking him to, he unpinned it, opened his red ledger, took out the folder marked *FICTION*, slid the bulletin neatly inside, at the back, and closed the folder again.

He sat back down and poured himself more tea.

Priscilla, who had been very quiet for nearly two hours, suddenly clapped her hands together.

"Oh! A farewell *party*!" she said brightly. "Rupert! We have to give you a send-off. I can have it ready in—"

She stopped.

She looked at the calendar on the wall. She looked down at her hands. She took a breath.

"Not on a Friday," said Priscilla.

And the whole room laughed.

It was the first time anyone had ever laughed in a retrospective, in either of Mike's lives. It went on for a while. Even Rupert laughed, a little, after a moment, though Mike was not sure he knew exactly what at.

---

Hesper Vane notarized the one-page list.

She set down her nail file, dripped her wax, and pressed her stamp into it, and then she looked up at the room over the top of her spectacles, with her usual expression of very dry, very slight approval.

"Old enough to learn," said Hesper Vane, "old enough to change."

Everyone signed it.

Durgan signed it, in square careful letters, with the medal on his chest. Pip signed it, and footnoted his own signature. Velka signed it. Sister Agatha signed it. Sir Roderick signed it, and drew a small map beside his name, out of habit. Tam signed it. Osric signed it, very slowly, with his blue-black hand. Priscilla signed it, and did not add *(yet!)*. Gareth signed it, and asked if he could keep the pen. Tolliver and Dex and Bramble signed it, in a row, and Bramble hummed the Registrar song under his breath while he did.

Brannoc signed it last.

He came up from the back of the room and stood over the page, and he did not sign it right away. He read it. He read the whole thing, all four lines, with his finger under each word. Then he read it again. And then he signed his name, at the bottom, carefully, in ordinary letters.

He went back to his chair.

He still hadn't said a word.

Malphus leaned over and looked at the finished page.

"Nice margins," he said.

Mike took it and pinned it to the war room door, in the place where the rules had been that morning. He stood back and looked at it. Then he looked at the room, at the walls covered in cards and the ceiling covered in cards and the people sitting under them, at the medal on Durgan's chest and the full mug of tea in Brannoc's hand.

It had been two hours. Exactly.

There was only one thing left that he wanted to know. He asked it quietly, looking at the chair under the window.

**Estimated time until Brannoc says something: 1 day.**

Mike nodded to himself, and put the chalk down, and went to find lunch.

It was, after all, Friday.
