## Chapter 10: Dragon-Proof

The contract was four pages long, and Mike read every word, which made him the first person in the guild to do so.

He read it at his desk, right after the stand-up, while Priscilla floated around the hall accepting congratulations. Page one was the usual: names, dates, the seal of the River Merchants' Consortium, a crest showing a barge with an expression of tremendous self-satisfaction. Page two was where the trouble started.

*The Guild shall provide a DRAGON-PROOF caravan escort service, said service to be fully DRAGON-PROOF.*

Mike read the sentence twice, looking for a definition. There wasn't one. The sentence simply defined itself in a small, confident circle and moved on.

*Said service shall also be FIREPROOF, WATERPROOF, and BANDIT-RESISTANT (premium tier).*

*The first caravan shall depart in SEVEN (7) days.*

*All services GUARANTEED, or the Guild shall refund the fee in full, TRIPLED.*

Mike put the contract down. He picked it back up. He turned to page four, the last page, and found that it was not part of the contract at all. It was a sales scroll, rolled flat and pinned to the back, in Priscilla's handwriting and violet ink.

At the top, in large looping letters, it said: *DRAGON-PROOF! Peace of Mind on Every Road!*

Under that was a drawing. It showed a sturdy covered wagon trundling cheerfully along a road under a smiling sun. Above it, a large green dragon was diving out of the sky, breathing a great gout of orange flame, and the flame was bouncing harmlessly off the wagon in little curved lines, the way rain bounces off an umbrella in a children's book. The dragon looked surprised. The wagon looked smug. The horse was winking.

And under the drawing, in three neat columns, were the prices.

> **DRAGON-PROOF BASIC**
>
> Dragon-proof escort. Standard protection.
>
> **DRAGON-PROOF PLUS**
>
> Everything in Basic, plus: decoy sheep.
>
> **DRAGON-PROOF PREMIUM**
>
> Everything in Plus, plus: *priority* dragon-proofing. Sheep wears a hat.

Mike looked at the third column for a long time.

He found Priscilla at the front counter, signing a well-wisher's hat. He held up the scroll. "Where did you get this drawing?"

"I drew it!" said Priscilla, delighted. "Isn't it *lovely*? Look at the horse."

"I see the horse." He pointed to the pricing columns. "Priscilla, what is *priority* dragon-proofing?"

Priscilla considered this with a small frown, as though it were an interesting question someone had asked at a party. "Well," she said, "it's like regular dragon-proofing. But *first*."

"First before what?"

"Before the regular."

"There is no regular. There's no *any*. We have never dragon-proofed anything."

"Which is why," said Priscilla, brightening, "Premium is such good value!" She patted his arm. "They bought Premium, Mikey. The whole year. It's the biggest sale in the guild's history."

She turned back to the well-wisher's hat.

Mike went back to his desk. He sat down. He laid the contract flat in front of him, beside Priscilla's wagon, its winking horse, and its sheep in a hat, and he asked the skill.

**Estimated time to build a dragon-proof escort: unknown.**

**Estimated time until the first caravan departs: 7 days.**

One of those numbers, he reflected, was much more useful than the other. Unfortunately, it was the wrong one.

---

He started where you always start. He tried to find out what anybody actually wanted.

The consortium's representative was a man named Master Gilbert Thrum: stout, prosperous, with a gold chain across his waistcoat and the confident, friendly manner of someone who has bought many things and has never once been asked what they were for. He received Mike in a private room at the Merchants' Hall, poured him a small glass of something expensive, and beamed.

"The dragon-proof service! Wonderful. Wonderful. We are *so* pleased."

"Thank you, Master Thrum. I just want to make sure we build exactly what you need. When you say *dragon-proof*, what specifically are you—"

"Proof," said Thrum. "Against dragons."

Mike waited for more. There was no more.

"Right," he said. "But proof how? Against fire? Claws? Being picked up? Carried off? Some dragons just sit on things—"

"Proof," said Thrum, more slowly, as if Mike were foreign. He made a little gesture with both hands, as though shaping something in the air: a wagon, maybe, or a feeling. "Against. Dragons." He smiled warmly. "You know."

Mike did not know. He finished the expensive drink, thanked Master Thrum for his time, and walked back to the guild in the rain.

Priscilla was more forthcoming. "Oh, it's simple," she said. "It's *basically* a wagon with a shield on it."

"A shield."

"A *big* shield."

"How big?"

"Dragon-sized," said Priscilla, as if this settled it.

Gareth was even more forthcoming. He leaned against Mike's desk with his medal glinting and said, "Dragons? Easy. I've fought two this week."

"No, you haven't."

"In the *crypt*."

"There were no dragons in the crypt, Gareth. I was there. You weren't."

Gareth looked at him with genuine, wounded puzzlement, as if Mike had questioned the existence of the moon. "It's in the *painting*," he said.

Which left one person.

Mike found him in his office that evening, under the one-eyed dragon head, eating a pork pie. Brannoc was in a good mood. The guild had made the biggest sale in its history, the Duke had said "ADEQUATE" at the stand-up, and the pie was excellent. He waved Mike into a chair.

"Guildmaster," said Mike. "You're the only person in this guild who's ever actually killed a dragon."

Brannoc's chest swelled. "The Wyrm of Kessel Ridge," he said. "Forty years ago."

"How did you do it?"

It was a simple question. Mike had expected the usual: a story about courage, and a great sword, and probably a boar. Instead, something unexpected happened.

Brannoc put down the pie.

His eyes went past Mike, past the weapons on the walls, past the one-eyed dragon head, to somewhere very far away. Somewhere cold, Mike thought, high on a ridge, a long time ago. When he spoke again, his voice was different: quieter, slower, without the boom.

"They don't go for the wagon," Brannoc said. "Folk always think they go for the wagon. The gold. They don't. They go for whatever moves fastest. That's what they see. That's what they hunt. You want to keep a wagon safe from a dragon, you don't armor the wagon. You give it something faster to chase. And then you have to be ready, when it comes down, because it comes down *so quick*, lad, you've no idea, and you get one chance, and—"

He stopped.

He blinked. He looked at Mike, and at the pie, and the faraway look closed like a shutter. The boom came back into his voice.

"Make it strong, lad," he said, and picked up the pie. "That's the secret. Strong."

Mike sat there for a moment longer. He wanted to ask more. Something in the Guildmaster's face told him not to.

He wrote it down that night, though, word for word, in the back of his guide, under a new heading: *Things Brannoc Knows.* It was the first entry.

---

"Four times," said Pip.

He was standing at the bottom of the ladder with his slate, which was covered from edge to edge with calculations, and his spectacles, which were steamed up from the effort.

"Four times what?" said Mike.

"Dragonfire." Pip tapped the slate. "I've worked it out from the old bestiaries. A full-grown dragon's breath runs at roughly four times the total capacity of our fire ward. Footnote: our *whole* fire ward. The one built for a dragon attack. Footnote: they built it for a *small* dragon. Footnote: at a *distance*." He took his spectacles off and polished them furiously. "And the fire ward is *already* feeding three bells."

"So it's impossible," said Mike.

"Completely," said Pip.

Durgan, at his desk, put down his pamphlet, swung his boots to the floor, and cracked his knuckles.

"Right," he said. "Let's build it."

They had six days.

The Basement had built impossible things before. Mike had been in the guild long enough to know that most of the building's quiet miracles had come from this room: the roof that stayed up on spite, the chandelier that had only set fire to one bard. But he had never watched them work on a deadline before, and he found it equal parts inspiring and alarming.

They started with a wagon, an ordinary covered merchant's wagon that Mike requisitioned from the guild stables by filing a form and then approving it himself. Durgan wrapped it, inch by inch, in ward-twine: a thin, silvery, faintly humming cord, spun with protective runes, that he had been keeping on a great wooden spool in the corner for, he claimed, "something stupid." By the end of the second day, the wagon looked like a parcel that had been tied up by a very anxious person.

Pip added the fire-parasol: a great folding canopy of enchanted canvas that could spring open over the wagon in an instant, like a vast umbrella, and was supposed to scatter flame. On the second test, it scattered Pip.

They added the decoy sheep.

This had been in the contract, under Plus, and so it had to exist. Mike requisitioned one from a farm outside town, by filing another form and approving it himself. She arrived on the third day: a small, round, dirty-white sheep with a black face and an expression of deep, abiding indifference to everything.

Pip looked at her with tremendous tenderness. "I'll call her Duchess," he said.

Duchess escaped eleven minutes later. She was found that afternoon in the main hall, standing in the exact center of the stand-up circle, beside the dog. Neither the dog nor the sheep appeared surprised to see each other.

And then, on the fourth day, they ran out of power.

The ward-twine needed power to hum. The fire-parasol needed power to spring. Every rune on the Contraption, as they had started calling it, needed power to do anything at all, and there was simply none left. Durgan traced every line on the board. Pip ran every number. Every ward in the building was spoken for. Most were spoken for twice.

"There's one option," said Pip, very quietly.

Durgan looked at him. "Don't."

"It's a binding. A very old one. It doesn't draw on a ward at all. It draws on *belief*." Pip held up his slate. On it, very small, was a rune Mike had never seen: a circle with a line through it, and a tiny knot. "A promise rune. It holds exactly as long as the client believes in it. Footnote: completely. Footnote: in their heart."

There was a long silence in the Basement.

"And if the client stops believing?" said Mike.

"Then it stops holding." Pip swallowed. "Footnote: immediately."

Mike thought about Master Gilbert Thrum, and his gold chain, and his warm confident smile, and the little gesture he'd made in the air. *Proof. Against. Dragons. You know.*

"He'll believe," Mike said. "He believes everything."

So Pip carved the promise rune into the wagon's front axle, small and neat, where no one would look. And as it turned out, the promise rune still needed a *little* power, just to get started, the way a fire needs a spark. There was only one place left with any to spare.

Durgan stood at the board for a long time with his hand over the fat amber cluster labeled *FIRE (MAIN)*. Three silvery threads already ran out of it, up through the ceiling, to three bells. He did not say anything. Then he added a fourth thread.

The board groaned. It was a long, low, tired sound, like an old man getting out of a chair.

And so, on the evening of the fourth day, Mike stood in the guild stableyard and looked at the finished Contraption.

It was a wagon, wrapped in silver twine until it looked like a cocoon. It had a great folded canvas parasol bristling on top like a furled sail. A small brass bell hung from the back, for reasons nobody could fully explain, though Pip insisted it was "structural." There was a promise carved where nobody would see it. Tied to the back by a long rope, eating a cabbage, was a sheep.

It had been built out of twine, and promises, and the last spare breath of a ward that was already holding up three bells.

It was, Mike thought, the most honest piece of engineering he had ever seen. It looked exactly like what it was.

---

Priscilla wanted a demonstration.

"Just a little one!" she said. "For the consortium! So they can *see* it! Day after tomorrow."

"It isn't ready," said Mike.

"It doesn't have to be *ready*. It just has to be *shown*."

Mike, very briefly, saw a conference room in Raleigh. A sales engineer named Kevin, clicking through a series of screenshots of a product that did not exist, while the client nodded and Brittany smiled and Mike, at the back of the room, watched his own unbuilt feature appear on the screen, perfectly finished, in a picture.

The skill offered its numbers, as it always did.

**Estimated time until the Contraption works: 19 days.**

**Estimated time until the demonstration: 2 days.**

Nineteen days. Of course it was nineteen. It was always nineteen. Mike was beginning to suspect it was the universe's favorite number, and that the universe was doing it on purpose.

He went down to the Basement and asked the skill a different question. Not how long, but how much. *How much fire can the Contraption actually take, right now, before it fails?*

The skill considered.

**Estimated fire tolerance: approximately one candle.**

Mike stared at the line. Then he went to find Velka.

---

The demonstration was held at noon on the sixth day, in the field behind the guild stables, before an audience of eleven consortium merchants in fur-trimmed coats, seated on a row of chairs that Bertram had carried down from somewhere without being asked.

The Contraption stood alone in the middle of the field, silver and humming faintly. Duchess grazed beside it, wearing a small green felt hat with a feather. The hat had been in the Premium tier.

Priscilla stood in front of the merchants, radiant. "Ladies and gentlemen! The River Merchants' Consortium's very own dragon-proof caravan escort! And now, to demonstrate, I give you, a *dragon*!"

She swept her arm toward the far end of the field.

Velka stepped forward, rolled up her sleeves, and cast.

She had tested it six times in the antechamber of the Basement the night before. Mike had watched her do it. She had muttered the whole time about how this was beneath her and how illusions were *last year's* magic. She had also gotten every single detail exactly right.

The air above the field shimmered, twisted, and tore open, and the dragon came through.

It was forty feet long. It was the green of old copper and deep water, with a ridge of black spines down its back and wings like the sails of a warship. Its eyes were molten gold. Its claws gouged the air. It threw back its great horned head and *roared*, a sound like an avalanche falling down a well, and in fact that sound was Bertram, standing behind a hedge, banging a large iron cooking pot with a ladle. But it was very convincing.

The merchants screamed. Master Thrum went over backward in his chair. One merchant, a tall, thin woman in purple, simply stood up and began walking very quickly in the opposite direction, and had to be fetched back.

The dragon wheeled once over the field, magnificent and terrible. It dove at the Contraption. It opened its vast, fanged, molten jaws.

And it breathed a small, steady flame. Precisely the size, and approximately the heat, of one candle.

The little flame drifted down toward the Contraption. The parasol snapped open with a bang, scattering it neatly into the air. The ward-twine hummed. Underneath the wagon, unseen, the promise rune glowed faintly as Master Thrum, flat on his back in the grass, stared up at it with his mouth open and his whole heart full of belief.

The candle flame went out.

The Contraption stood, untouched, silver, triumphant.

The dragon roared one more time, a very good, ringing, pot-heavy roar, and vanished in a shimmer of light.

There was a long, stunned silence in the field.

Then Master Gilbert Thrum scrambled to his feet, flushed and glowing, and began to applaud. The other merchants joined him, and then they were all on their feet, cheering and clapping and slapping each other's backs, the way people do when they have been very frightened and are not anymore. Even the tall woman in purple, who had been fetched back, applauded.

"*Magnificent!*" cried Thrum. "*Dragon-proof!*"

Mike, at the edge of the field, let out a breath he'd been holding for two days. Beside him, Velka pushed her sleeves back down. She did not look at him, but the corner of her mouth was doing something.

"Forty feet," Mike said quietly.

"I made it *sixty* in the test," said Velka. "I scaled it back. The proportions were wrong."

"It was perfect."

"I know," she said, and the corner of her mouth did the thing again.

And then, in the middle of the applause, as the merchants were still cheering, Priscilla clasped her hands together and said, glowing, to no one in particular and everyone in general:

"*And it floats!*"

The applause faltered.

Master Thrum turned to her, eyes shining. "Ooh," he said. "Does it?"

Mike turned very slowly to look at Priscilla.

Priscilla did not look back at him. Priscilla was looking at Master Thrum, with the radiant, total, untroubled confidence of a woman who had never once in her life been held to anything she said.

"It *will*," she said.

The merchants cheered again. Somewhere behind the hedge, Bertram set down his pot.

---

The first caravan left on the morning of the seventh day.

It was twelve wagons long, loaded with wool, wine, and bolts of dyed cloth, bound downriver for the markets at Westmarch. At its head rolled the Contraption, silver and humming, parasol furled, brass bell tinkling. Behind it, on her rope, wearing her Premium hat, came Duchess. Behind Duchess came the crypt party: Roderick, Agatha, Velka, Bertram, and Mike, who had decided that if anything went wrong, he wanted to be there to document it.

Nothing went wrong.

No dragon came.

They rolled downriver for three days, in good weather, along a quiet road, through farmland and woodland and nothing, and the sky above them stayed empty and blue the entire way. Every few hours Mike looked up. Every few hours, there was nothing there.

On the second day, he asked the skill, out of pure curiosity: *What was the chance of a dragon attacking this caravan?*

**Estimated probability: 0%.**

**There has not been a dragon within two hundred miles of Brindlemoor in forty years.**

**The last one was killed by Guildmaster Brannoc.**

Mike read that last line twice. Then he looked back up at the empty sky for a long time.

The caravan reached Westmarch on the third day, safe and sound and entirely un-dragoned, and Master Thrum met it at the gates personally, weeping with joy.

"Not a single dragon got through!" he cried, embracing the Contraption, and then Duchess, and then, rather awkwardly, Roderick. "Not *one*! Dragon-proof! Absolutely, completely, *verifiably* dragon-proof!"

The consortium renewed on the spot, for five more caravans.

---

They heard the news at the guild before Mike got back. And when he walked through the front doors two days later, dusty and tired, the first thing he saw was the gong.

It hung from a wooden frame in the middle of the front hall, just inside the doors: a great round disk of polished brass, the size of a cartwheel, with a padded mallet on a hook beside it. A brass plate was screwed to the frame. *For Great Sales.*

Priscilla was standing in front of it, holding the mallet, waiting. When she saw Mike, her face lit up.

"Mikey! You're just in time!" She raised the mallet over her head. "FIVE MORE CARAVANS!"

*BWONNNNNNNG.*

The sound went through Mike like a sword.

For a moment, he wasn't in the guild hall at all. He was walking past the sales pit at Synergex, on the morning after Brittany sold the routing feature, and the little brass gong was ringing, and everyone in sales was cheering, and Mike was walking to his desk to find a ticket in his queue that said **ROUTING (SOLD)**.

He stood very still in the doorway while the gong's long, shimmering note died away. The hall erupted in applause. Brannoc came out of his office, beaming, and clapped Priscilla on the back. Gareth whooped. The dog barked. Duchess, who had come in through the back, bleated.

And from the edge of the crowd, where he had been called upstairs for yet another meeting about the stand-up, Durgan Flintmantle stood with his arms folded, watching the gong.

He did not clap.

When the cheering had died down, he spoke, quietly, to nobody in particular. Mike, who was standing next to him, was the only one who heard.

"Hall hasn't burned down in eleven years," said Durgan. "Nobody's rung a gong."

Then he turned and went back down the ladder.

---

Mike found him in the Basement that night, along with Pip, sitting at the board in the low blue light.

He had brought paper. He laid a fresh sheet on Pip's desk and, at the top, in his neatest hand, wrote a title. Pip read it over his shoulder and nodded slowly.

*Contraption: Known Issues.*

They worked on it together until past midnight.

> *1. Not dragon-proof.*
>
> *2. Sheep wanders off.*
>
> *3. Promise rune fails if the client reads the contract carefully.*
>
> *4. Floats: no.*
>
> *5. See item 1.*

It went on from there: the parasol's tendency to scatter anyone standing within six feet of it, the bell (still structural, still unexplained), the ward-twine fraying where it crossed the wagon's axles, the fact that the whole thing drew on a fire ward that had never been built to carry it. By the time they were finished, it ran to three pages. Then they started fixing what they could. Pip re-spun the frayed twine. Mike rewrote the Contraption's care instructions in plain language and filed them with the Registrar, so that someone, someday, might read them. Durgan stood at the board, watching.

"How bad?" Mike asked him, around one in the morning.

Durgan didn't answer at first. He just pointed.

The great amber cluster labeled *FIRE (MAIN)* was glowing brighter than Mike had ever seen it. Four silvery threads ran up out of it now, to three bells and a wagon. And beside it, in Pip's tiny, careful handwriting, a new brass label had been screwed into the stone, with a single number.

*112%.*

"It wasn't built for this," said Durgan. "None of it was built for this."

And then, as Mike was looking at the number, something happened on the board that made all three of them go still.

The old line flickered.

The red-gold one. The one that ran on its own along the base of the wall, up the pillar, across the ceiling, and away to the front of the building, to the doorframe, and down into the floor. The one that nobody touched. For one second, maybe less, the whole length of it dimmed, like a candle in a draft, and then came back.

Pip made a small sound.

Durgan did not make any sound at all. He just stood there, looking at the line, with an expression Mike had never seen on his face before.

And from the grate in the wall, at that exact moment, booming down from the main hall overhead, came the voice of Guildmaster Brannoc. He had clearly had a very good evening, and possibly some wine.

"...a *grander* entrance, that's what this guild needs! A guild that's made the biggest sale in its history deserves a front door to match! Something people can see from the *square*! I want it on the other wall, facing the fountain!"

A murmur of agreement.

"Just move the door," boomed Brannoc. "It's a *door*. How hard can it be?"
