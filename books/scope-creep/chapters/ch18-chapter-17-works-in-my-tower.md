# Chapter 17: Works in My Tower

Velka's tower stood at the end of Wickstaff Lane, on the scrappy north edge of Brindlemoor, where the houses gave up being respectable and started leaning on each other for support. It was a tall, narrow spire of gray stone, slightly crooked, with a single round window at the very top and a door so small that Mike had to duck. It was, she told him on the doorstep, the cheapest tower in town.

"Why is it so cheap?"

"Nobody else wanted it," said Velka. "It's very old, it's very drafty, and every so often it hums." She shrugged. "I like it."

Inside, there were stairs. A great many stairs, spiraling up the inside of the tower in a tight stone corkscrew, past little landings crowded with the debris of a person who thought very fast and cleaned up very rarely. There were shelves of books, most of them open. There were jars of things Mike decided not to look at closely. And on every landing, on every wall, on every flat surface, there were spell drafts, hundreds of them: sheets of paper covered in runes and diagrams and Velka's spiky handwriting, and almost every one of them crossed out.

*Fireball 3.0. NO.*

*Fireball 3.1. Better, but no.*

*Lightning 2.0. Absolutely not. (Eyebrows.)*

*Levitation 4.4. Went through the roof. Literally. Fix roof.*

At the very top was a single round room under the round window, with a desk, a narrow bed, and a fat orange cat asleep on a cushion. Pinned along the curve of the wall, unrolled to its full length, was the résumé. Mike had seen it before, under her arm, but never all at once. It went most of the way around the room. Every spell she had ever invented was on it, versioned and dated, from *Spark 0.1 (age six)* to *Buoyancy 6.0 (beta)*, which had been added at the very end in ink so fresh it still shone.

The cat opened one eye and looked at Mike.

"That's Footnote," said Velka.

"Your cat is called *Footnote*?"

"He was always underneath things," said Velka, as if this were obvious. "Small, at the bottom, and nobody reads him. Now, *look*."

She went to the desk, where a large porcelain washbasin was waiting, half full of water. Floating in the water, upside down, was a tiny wagon.

It was a model of the Contraption. A very good one, about the length of Mike's forearm, perfect in every detail: the cocoon of silver thread, the furled parasol, the little brass bell, even a tiny wooden sheep on a string. Velka turned it the right way up, set it at the bottom of the basin, and stepped back.

Footnote got up from his cushion, stretched, padded across the desk, climbed onto the little wagon, and sat on it, as cats do with anything new. Velka didn't even notice. Mike would remember that later.

"Buoyancy six point oh," she said. "Beta."

She spoke a word and drew a quick, tight sign in the air.

The model wagon rose from the bottom of the basin. It rose gently and steadily, like a leaf in a spring, cat and all, until it broke the surface of the water, and then it sat there, floating, perfectly level, turning very slowly in a little circle. It was beautiful. Footnote, riding on top, purred like a small engine.

"*There*," said Velka. Her face was glowing. "It floats. Priscilla promised it would float, and now it floats."

"It does," said Mike, and meant it. It was genuinely lovely work.

Then, because he had to, he asked the skill.

**Estimated time until this spell fails outside this tower: 4 seconds.**

He looked at the line for a moment. Then he looked at Velka, flushed and proud, watching her tiny wagon turn in its washbasin. And he said nothing at all, because he had been her once: a long time ago, in another life, demonstrating a beautiful thing on his own computer to a room of people, absolutely certain it would work everywhere else.

---

Priscilla booked the preview for the very next morning.

Of course she did. Mike had mentioned the spell at the stand-up, carefully, as "promising, in early testing." By the end of the stand-up, Priscilla had heard "done." By lunch, she had sent a pigeon to the River Merchants' Consortium. By dinner, Master Gilbert Thrum had replied, in a fever of excitement, that he and four of his partners would be delighted to see the Contraption float at the river dock at nine o'clock sharp.

"Tomorrow?" said Velka, when Mike told her. "That's... fine. It works. You saw it work."

"I saw it work in your tower."

"So it *works*."

Mike opened his mouth. Then he looked at her face and closed it again.

The river dock was a long wooden jetty at the bottom of Cooper's Lane, smelling of tar and fish, with the broad brown river sliding past it, slow and deep. The Contraption had been wheeled down at dawn and stood at the very edge of the jetty, silver and humming, on a wooden ramp that ran down into the water. Duchess the sheep was tied to the back, in her Premium hat. A small crowd had gathered: the five merchants of the consortium in their fur-trimmed coats, Priscilla in a new hat with a feather, Brannoc, the crypt party, a few curious fishermen, and Bertram, holding a long boat hook, because Mike had quietly asked him to bring one.

"Ladies and gentlemen!" cried Priscilla. "The dragon-proof, fireproof, waterproof, bandit-resistant Contraption, as you've never seen it before! *Floating!*"

Velka stepped forward. She rolled up her sleeves, took a deep breath, spoke the word, and drew the sign in the air.

Mike counted.

*One.*

*Two.*

*Three.*

*Four.*

The Contraption did not move.

Everything else did.

The stack of crates beside the ramp rose gently into the air, turning slowly, like the little wagon in the washbasin. A coil of rope lifted off the jetty and hung in midair, uncoiling lazily. Duchess the sheep, still tied to the back of the Contraption, floated upward until she reached the end of her rope and hung there, bobbing like a woolly balloon, wearing a hat and an expression of total indifference. Two of the merchants left the ground, flailing, with cries of alarm. And Master Gilbert Thrum, stout and prosperous and gold-chained, rose straight up off the planks of the jetty, his fur-trimmed coat flapping, and drifted out over the river.

"*Ooh!*" said Master Thrum.

He did not sound frightened. He sounded *delighted*. He spread his arms and turned slowly in the air above the slow brown river, and his face lit up like a child's.

"Ooh! Look at me! Look at the *river*!"

Beneath him, on its ramp, the Contraption tilted, slid, and with a long, sucking, deeply undignified noise, sank to its axles in the mud at the bottom of the bank. It stayed there.

There was a silence on the jetty.

Then Priscilla, without a flicker of hesitation, stepped to the very edge of the planks, cupped her hands around her mouth, and called up to the floating merchant.

"Master Thrum! While you're up there! Can I interest you in our *Premium Float* tier?"

"*Yes!*" cried Master Thrum, from twenty feet above the river. "Yes! Absolutely! Whatever it is!"

Priscilla produced a contract from somewhere about her person, rolled it into a tube, tied it to a quill, and threw it up to him. He caught it on the second try, signed it against his own knee, in midair, with a flourish, and threw it back down. Priscilla caught it neatly.

Mike, watching from the end of the jetty, glanced at the sunken Contraption. Down under its front axle, in the mud, he knew there was a tiny carved rune, a circle with a line through it and a knot: Pip's promise rune, the one that held only as long as the client believed. He could feel it glowing, faintly, even from here.

It was holding. Of course it was holding. Master Thrum was floating over a river, signing contracts in the air. He had never believed in anything more in his life.

It took Bertram a quarter of an hour, with the boat hook, to fetch him back. He did it calmly and professionally, reaching out from the end of the jetty and snagging Master Thrum by the belt, then reeling him in hand over hand, the way you'd bring in a kite. Then he did the two other merchants, and the crates, and the rope. He left Duchess until last. She didn't seem to mind.

Velka had not moved. She was still standing at the head of the ramp, staring at the Contraption in the mud. Her face had gone very white.

---

"It *worked*," she said, back at the guild, an hour later. "It worked in my tower."

They were in the Basement, because it was the only place Velka would agree to go. She was pacing in front of the rune board, back and forth, with her hands balled into fists. Pip was watching her from his ladder with great sympathy. Durgan was watching her from his chair with the expression of a man who had heard this sentence many times before, in many forms, from many people.

"I know," said Mike.

"You *saw* it work."

"I did."

"So why didn't it work at the *dock*?"

Mike took out a fresh sheet of paper and a pencil, and sat down at Pip's desk.

"Okay," he said gently. "Let's find out. What's different about your tower?"

Velka stopped pacing. "Nothing's *different*. It's a tower. It's where I work."

"Humor me. Start with the ground."

Velka frowned. Then, slowly: "Well. There's a ley line under it. A small one. That's probably why it hums." She waved a hand. "But it's tiny. It wouldn't make a difference."

Mike wrote it down. *1. Ley line under tower. None at dock.*

"The water?"

"It's *water*."

"Where did the water in the basin come from?"

A pause. "The rain barrel."

*2. Rainwater in basin. River water at dock.*

"And the wagon itself?"

"It was a *model*, obviously, I wasn't going to bring a whole wagon up eleven flights of stairs—" She stopped.

*3. Model at one-twentieth scale. Full size at dock.*

Mike looked at the list. Then he turned it around so she could see it.

"Three differences," he said. "Any one of them could break it. You tested it somewhere that wasn't anything like where it had to work."

Velka looked at the list for a long moment. He watched her face. He watched her get there.

Then her expression cleared, and she smiled, and she snapped her fingers, and Mike's heart sank, because he knew that smile. He had seen it on the faces of a great many very clever people, in a great many conference rooms, a moment before they suggested something enormous.

"Then that's easy," said Velka. "We'll bring the tower."

Mike stared at her.

"Not the *whole* tower," she said impatiently. "A copy. A little one. We build a tiny version of my tower *on the wagon*. Same stones. Same basin of rainwater. Same chair, even, just to be safe. The spell will think it's at home." She spread her hands. "Then it'll work everywhere."

At the back of the Basement, Bertram, who had come down the ladder to return the boat hook, set it against the wall. He took a measuring tape out of his pocket. And without a word, he began to measure the length of Pip's desk, as if estimating how much stone would fit on a wagon.

Mike put his face in his hands.

In his first life, a team down the hall at Synergex had once had a program that ran perfectly on one developer's computer and nowhere else. They had spent a month trying to find out why. In the end, they had given up, and packaged the program up together with a complete copy of that developer's computer, so that wherever it went, it would always think it was at home. Then they had shipped the whole thing to customers. Everybody in the industry had started doing it. They gave it a name, and conferences, and it had made several people extremely rich.

It was, Mike reflected, a perfectly reasonable idea. It had worked very well. That was the frightening part.

"Let's," he said through his fingers, "try one thing first."

---

He called it *staging*. He did not explain the word.

The idea was simple, and he explained it simply. They would not test the spell in Velka's tower anymore. They would test it somewhere as much like the river dock as they could possibly make it, so that if it failed, it would fail where it was safe to fail, in front of nobody, and they would find out *why*.

They built the test site at the guild stableyard, which had a large, muddy horse pond at one end. Mike requisitioned a full-size wagon from the stables by filing a form and approving it himself. Bertram, without being asked, spent an afternoon hauling buckets of river water up Cooper's Lane and pouring them into the pond until it was as brown and silty as the real thing. Pip checked the ground with a slate and a dowsing rod and declared, with satisfaction, that the stableyard was "completely ley-free, footnote, thank goodness."

And Mike brought a notebook. On the first page, he wrote: *Float Test Log.*

They started that night, after the hall had closed, by lantern light. Velka stood at the edge of the pond. Mike stood beside her with the notebook. The wagon sat at the bottom of a muddy ramp. The horses, in the stalls behind them, watched with great interest.

Velka cast Buoyancy 6.0.

The wagon did not move. A wheelbarrow on the far side of the yard rose six feet into the air and hung there.

Mike wrote it down.

*Test 1. Buoyancy 6.0. Wagon did not float. Wheelbarrow floated. Retrieved wheelbarrow.*

Velka glared at the wheelbarrow. Then at the notebook. Then at Mike.

"You don't have to write *everything* down."

"Yes," said Mike, "I do."

They went on.

They went on for four nights. Every night, after the guild closed, they came back to the horse pond, and every night Velka tried another version, and Mike wrote down what happened. They started by taking away the differences, one at a time. For a night, Velka cast the spell standing on a slab of stone carried from the yard of her tower, in case the ley line had been leaking into her feet. It failed. For a night, Bertram replaced the pond water with rainwater, bucket by bucket. It failed. They tried a model wagon at one-twentieth scale, in the river water. It failed.

Mike's notebook filled up.

> *Test 9. Buoyancy 6.2. Wagon sank. Velka says wagon was "already sinking before she cast." Noted.*
>
> *Test 10. Buoyancy 6.2, rainwater. Wagon sank more slowly. Horse floated briefly. Horse unharmed. Horse unimpressed.*
>
> *Test 11. Buoyancy 6.3. Wagon floated. Then the pond floated. Velka disputes that the pond floated.*
>
> *Test 12. Buoyancy 6.3, repeated. The pond floated.*

After test twelve, they spent an hour retrieving a pond.

They argued, of course. They argued about everything. They argued about whether the pond had floated (it had), and whether Mike's handwriting was deliberately smug (it was not), and whether a spell could be "basically working" if it only floated the wrong things (it could not). Velka accused him of thinking he was always right. Mike pointed out that he usually was, which did not help.

But somewhere around the third night, Mike noticed that the arguments were changing.

They still argued. But Velka had started arriving earlier than he did, and bringing two mugs of tea instead of one, and she had started leaning over his shoulder to read the test log as he wrote it, close enough that he could smell the smoke in her hair. Sometimes she corrected his spelling of rune names. Sometimes, when a test failed in a particularly spectacular way, she laughed, and then he laughed, and then they stood at the edge of the muddy pond at midnight laughing like idiots while the horses watched. And once, after test seventeen, when the wagon sank and she sat down on the edge of the ramp with her head in her hands, he sat down beside her, and they stayed there for a long time without saying anything, and it was not an argument at all.

On the fourth night, they had run out of differences.

The ley line was gone. The rainwater was gone. The scale was gone. They had matched every condition of her tower except the tower itself, and the spell still did not work. It worked on her desk, at the top of her tower, and nowhere else in the world.

"What else was there?" said Mike, staring at the notebook. "In the tower. When it worked. Think. Anything."

Velka thought. She closed her eyes and stood very still in the lantern light, and Mike could see her reconstructing the room: the desk, the basin, the window, the résumé on the wall, the wagon in the water.

Her eyes snapped open.

"Footnote," she said.

They went and got him.

It took a while. Footnote did not wish to leave the tower, and did not wish to go out into the night, and very much did not wish to be carried through Brindlemoor in a basket. He told them so, at length. But at last they set the basket down at the edge of the horse pond, and opened it, and Footnote climbed out, looked around the stableyard with deep disdain, looked at the wagon at the bottom of the ramp, and then, as cats do with anything new, walked down the ramp, climbed up onto the wagon, and sat on it.

Velka cast Buoyancy 6.3.

The wagon rose.

It rose gently and steadily out of the muddy water, perfectly level, cat and all, until it floated at the surface of the pond, turning very slowly in a little circle. Nothing else floated. Not the wheelbarrow. Not the horses. Not the pond. Just the wagon, and Footnote, who sat on top of it purring like a small engine.

Mike and Velka stood at the edge of the pond and looked at it for a long time.

"It's the purring," said Velka slowly. "The frequency. It must be stabilizing the—" She stopped. "It was the *cat*. The whole time. It was never the spell. It was the *cat*."

Mike looked down at his notebook. Then he wrote, carefully, in neat letters, at the bottom of the page:

*Test 23. Buoyancy 6.3, with cat. Wagon floated. Nothing else floated.*

*Requirements: one cat.*

He showed it to her. She looked at it for a long moment.

"Well," she said at last, very slowly, "the consortium has a lot of caravans—"

"Velka."

"They could each have a cat—"

"We can't ship a cat," said Mike, "with every caravan."

Out on the pond, Footnote yawned enormously and went to sleep.

---

The next evening, before they went to the pond, Velka came down to the Basement to fetch Mike, and Footnote came with her in his basket, because he had decided overnight that he was now part of the project.

Pip was at the board. He turned around as they came down the ladder, and saw the basket, and the large orange cat inside it.

"Oh!" said Pip, delighted. "A cat! What's his name?"

"Footnote," said Velka.

Pip went very still. He took off his spectacles. He put them back on. He looked at the cat. The cat looked at him.

"*Footnote*," Pip whispered.

He came down off his ladder, very slowly, as if approaching something sacred, and knelt beside the basket. Footnote considered him for a long moment. Then the cat stretched out one large orange paw and rested it, briefly and with great condescension, on Pip's knee.

Pip had to go and sit down.

---

The fifth night was the last night, and it was Velka who ended it.

They had tried everything. Mike's notebook was nearly full. They had tried Buoyancy 6.3 without the cat; it floated the pond. They had tried 6.4, and 7.0, which Velka called *alpha* and which had briefly floated Mike. They had tried, God help them, a recording of a cat purring, made by Pip on an enchanted slate; it floated the slate.

At midnight, Velka sat down on the end of the ramp with her boots in the muddy water and stared at the wagon, which was sitting at the bottom of the pond, where it had been for most of the week.

She was quiet for a long time.

"There's one more thing I could try," she said finally. Her voice was very small.

Mike waited.

"It's old," she said. "It's really old. My grandmother taught it to me when I was six. Levitation one point oh." She made a face. "It's the first spell anyone learns. It's in every primer in the province. It's slow, and it's plain, and it's boring, and I've *never* put it on my résumé, because it isn't *mine*, and it isn't *new*, and nobody would be impressed by it in a hundred years."

She looked at her hands.

"But it works," she said. "It's always worked. Everywhere. Every time. My grandmother used it to lift the washing."

Mike said nothing. He didn't need to.

Velka got up. She stood at the edge of the pond, in the mud, in the lantern light. She closed her eyes. And she cast the oldest, plainest, most boring spell she knew, the way her grandmother had taught her, slowly, word by word, with a few small changes to make it work on water instead of washing.

The wagon rose.

It did not glide like a leaf in a spring. It did not turn in elegant circles. It simply rose, steadily, a little clumsily, until it reached the surface of the pond, and then it sat there. Level. Floating. Without a cat.

Nothing else moved. Not the pond, not the horses, not the wheelbarrow.

Mike opened the notebook.

*Test 31. Grandmother's Levitation 1.0, adapted. Wagon floated. Nothing else floated.*

"Again," he said.

She did it again. It floated. She did it again. It floated. She did it ten times, in a row, by lantern light, until her voice was hoarse and the horses had gone to sleep, and every single time, the wagon rose from the bottom of the pond, plainly and clumsily and perfectly, and floated.

*Test 32. Floated. Test 33. Floated. Test 34. Floated...*

When she finally stopped, Mike handed her the notebook and the pencil. "It needs a name," he said. "For the log."

Velka took the pencil. She thought about it for a moment. Then she wrote, at the bottom of the page, in her spiky handwriting:

*Float 1.0 (beta)*

She looked at it. It looked exactly like every other name she had ever given a spell.

Mike reached over, very gently, took the pencil from her fingers, and drew one neat line through the word *beta*.

Velka looked at the line. She opened her mouth to object. Then she closed it again, and let it stand.

*Float 1.0.*

She stared at the version number for a long time, by lantern light, as if it were written in a language she had never seen. One point oh. Not *beta*, or *alpha*, or *experimental*, or *pre-release*. Just finished. Just tested. Just a thing that worked.

The next morning, Mike passed her tower on his way to the guild and saw her through the round window at the top. She was standing in front of her résumé with a pen. As he watched, she drew a firm line through something near the end of it, *Buoyancy 6.0 (beta)*, he guessed, and wrote something new beneath it, in small, careful letters.

He couldn't read it from the street. He didn't need to.

---

The second preview was held at the river dock three days later, at nine o'clock sharp.

The five merchants came back, a little warily. Master Thrum came first and stood a long way from the edge of the jetty. Priscilla wore a different hat. Bertram brought the boat hook, just in case.

Velka stepped up to the head of the ramp. She wore no flourish and drew no complicated sign in the air. She simply cast Float 1.0, slowly and plainly, word by word.

The Contraption rose out of the river mud, steadily and a little clumsily, until it reached the surface of the water.

Then it floated.

That was all it did. It did not glide or turn. It did not float anything else. It just sat on the river, level and silver, humming faintly, its little brass bell tinkling, with Duchess standing calmly on the back in her hat. It floated for ten minutes. Then twenty. Then half an hour. The merchants watched it float the whole time, in respectful silence, the way people watch a thing that is simply and reliably doing exactly what it is supposed to do, which is a rarer sight than you'd think.

Then Master Thrum began to clap. The others joined in.

"*Floats!*" he cried. "It *floats*! As promised!" He seized Priscilla's hand and shook it. "Wonderful. *Wonderful*." Then, still shaking her hand, with exactly the same shining enthusiasm, he turned to look at the Contraption and said: "And can it *fly*?"

There was a pause.

Mike, at the end of the jetty, closed his eyes.

"It *will*," said Priscilla.

---

Rupert announced the success at the next morning's stand-up.

He did it with great warmth and humility, standing in the middle of the circle with his black belt on and his towel over his arm. He thanked the consortium. He thanked the Guildmaster. He held up a single sheet of paper with a drawing of the Contraption floating on it, which Tolliver had made.

"And so, team," said Rupert, "I'm proud to announce the successful delivery of *Float 1.0*. A Lean Six Sigil initiative from the Ink Strategy team."

The roommates bowed. Bramble played a chord. Brannoc rang the gong.

Mike, behind his pillar, sighed. He wasn't even surprised anymore. He hadn't really expected anything else.

But beside him, someone was breathing very fast.

He turned. Velka was standing at her chalk mark, in her mage's coat, with her fists clenched at her sides and two bright red spots burning on her cheeks. Mike had seen her furious before: at spells, at wheelbarrows, at him. He had never seen her furious *for* someone.

She stepped into the circle.

"*Initiative*," she said. Her voice was low and shaking. "An *Ink Strategy* initiative."

Rupert turned to her with a warm, encouraging smile. "That's right! Velka, thank you so much for your—"

"He spent five nights," said Velka, "at a *horse pond*. With *me*. Until midnight. In the mud. Writing down every single thing that went wrong, *thirty-one times*, until it went right." She pointed at Mike behind his pillar without looking at him. Every head in the circle turned to look at him. "He wrote the test log. He found the cat. He made me try the old spell." Her voice rose. "So where were *you*?"

There was a long silence in the hall.

Rupert looked at her. His face did not change at all. It stayed warm, and encouraging, and shining.

"Velka," he said gently. "I *love* the passion."

And he turned to the next item on the agenda.

Velka stood in the middle of the circle with her mouth open. Then she turned on her heel and walked out of the stand-up, out of the hall, and out of the new front door. Nobody stopped her.

Mike found that he was smiling. He was not entirely sure why. Rupert had stolen the credit, the gong had been rung, and nothing at all had changed. But somebody, in front of everybody, had said his name and said what he had done.

He had not realized until that moment how long it had been.

---

He walked her home that evening.

It was a cool, clear autumn evening, with the first stars coming out over the rooftops and the smell of woodsmoke in the air. Wickstaff Lane was quiet. Neither of them said very much. At the bottom of her tower, by the little door, Velka stopped, and turned, and looked at him, and seemed about to say something.

Mike got there first. He held out a parcel wrapped in brown paper and string.

Velka looked at it with suspicion. "What's that?"

"Open it."

She did. Inside was a hat.

It was not a remarkable hat. It was plain, brown, and felt, with a sturdy brim and a simple band, the kind of hat a sensible person might wear for twenty years. It was not anything like the hats she usually wore. It had no silver pin. It had no feather. It was, in its way, extremely boring.

"You've lost two since the crypt," said Mike. "One melted, one eaten. So I got you one that won't be."

Velka turned it over in her hands. "How do you know?"

"I tested it," said Mike.

She looked up at him. And for a moment, in the dusk, he thought she might laugh.

"You *tested* a *hat*."

"Fireproof," said Mike. "I held it over a candle for ten minutes. And wolf-resistant." He paused. "I borrowed a dog from the stables. He chewed it for an hour. Look. Not a mark."

Velka looked at the hat for a long time. Then, slowly, she put it on.

It fit. It looked, Mike thought, like it had always been hers.

She did not say thank you. She did not say anything at all. She looked at him from under the sturdy brown brim, and then she turned and went into her tower and shut the little door behind her. But at the top of the tower, a moment later, a light came on in the round window, and Mike could see her standing there, looking at herself in the glass, still wearing the hat.

She didn't take it off.

---

He got back to the guild late. The hall was dark and empty. The three bells hung silent behind the counter, the OMNIFILE stood in the round back room with its slot closed, and the Registrar hummed beside it, deep and steady. Mike sat down at his desk behind Osric's stack and lit his lamp. And because he always did, he asked the skill.

**Estimated time until the first payment to Malphus: 9 days.**

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 16 days.**

Then he noticed the letter.

It was lying on his desk, squared to the edge, as if someone had placed it there very carefully. It was heavy cream paper, folded twice and sealed with a large disc of silver wax, stamped with the sign of a tower surrounded by a ring of stars. Mike had seen that sign before, though only once or twice, on very old documents in the very back of the Registrar's ledger.

It was the seal of the Archmage of Brindlemoor.

He broke it open, and read the letter by lamplight. It was short, and written in a thin, spidery, very old hand.

> *To the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor,*
>
> *After two hundred and six years of service as warden of the city's barrier, I find myself somewhat tired.*
>
> *I intend to retire at the end of the month.*
>
> *I trust that you have someone in mind.*
>
> *— Thessaly*

Mike read it twice.

Then he read it a third time, more slowly, especially the last line.

He turned around in his chair, in the dark, and looked at the stack of paper beside his desk.

"Osric," he said quietly. "Does anyone know how the city barrier works?"

There was a long pause from inside the stack.

"Thessaly knows," said Osric.
