## Chapter 23: No, Your Grace

Mike had never said no to a client.

Not in this life, and not in the last one. He had come close. He had, once, at Synergex, in a meeting with Hartwell Logistics, opened his mouth to say *no, that can't be done by Friday*, and Chad Pemberton had put a hand on his shoulder and said *what Mike means is, we're excited to explore it*, and Mike had closed his mouth again, and that had been that. Eleven months later, he had died at his desk at two in the morning, exploring it.

He sat in the war room at seven o'clock on the morning of the sixth day, with the board behind him and two days of sand left in the hourglass downstairs, and he looked at the words in the right-hand column with his own name next to them.

*HARVEST FESTIVAL ESCORT. Tell the Duke. MIKE.*

*DONE MEANS: Mike still alive.*

He asked the skill what would happen if he just walked over there and did it.

**Estimated time until you say yes anyway: 4 minutes.**

Mike put his head down on the table for a while.

Then he got up and went to find some people to practice on.

---

"I'm the Duke," said Velka.

She was sitting in Brannoc's big chair at the head of the war room table, which Mike had dragged in from the office while Brannoc was at breakfast. She had a wooden spoon in her hand, held upright like a dragon tooth. She had pulled the brown hat down low over her eyes, to look more noble, and was frowning in a way that she clearly felt was ducal.

"Okay," said Mike. He stood in front of her. "Your Grace."

"MIKE," roared Velka, so loudly that Pip, who was sitting in the corner with a slate, dropped it. "THE ESCORT. MY FESTIVAL. BOTH GOLEMS. MARCHING."

"Your Grace," said Mike, "I'm afraid the guild—"

"I WANT BANNERS."

"Yes, Your Grace," said Mike.

There was a pause.

"Oh, no," said Mike.

"You said yes," said Velka, in her own voice.

"I said *yes, Your Grace*. That's not a yes. That's an acknowledgment. It's like nodding."

"You said it about the banners," said Pip, from the corner, consulting his slate. "I have it footnoted."

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time until you say yes anyway: 3 minutes.**

It had gone *down*.

"Again," said Mike.

They did it again. They did it four more times. Velka was a terrible Duke. She got the capital letters right, but she kept laughing at the end of her sentences, and once, she forgot herself completely and said, "Mike, honestly, just *tell* him," in a perfectly normal voice, which ruined it. And every single time, somewhere in the first minute, Mike heard himself say something that wasn't *no*. *Yes, Your Grace. Of course. I understand. Let me look into it.* They came out of him on their own, the way your hand comes up when someone throws a ball at you.

"Let me try," said Priscilla.

She had been watching from the doorway with her arms folded. Mike looked at her doubtfully. But she was already coming around the table, and Velka was already getting up out of the big chair, and Priscilla sat down in it and took the wooden spoon.

And then something happened to her. She didn't make her voice louder. She didn't frown. She just settled back in the chair, slowly, and let her face go soft and a little sad, and tilted her head, and looked at Mike with an expression of patient, lonely, enormous expectation, the look of a man who had been promised things his whole life, and who knew, deep down, that he was about to be promised something again.

She tapped the spoon twice on the arm of the chair.

"Surely," said Priscilla, in a low rumble that was so exactly the Duke that the hair stood up on Mike's arms, "*surely* the guild can manage *one* little escort. For an old friend."

Mike opened his mouth.

"I'm sure we can find a way," he heard himself say.

"Oh, that's *worse*," said Velka.

**Estimated time until you say yes anyway: 90 seconds.**

Priscilla got up out of the chair. She handed Mike the spoon, solemnly, the way a general hands over a sword.

"He'll say *surely*," she said. "He always says *surely*. Never, *ever* answer *surely*. If you answer *surely*, you've already lost." She tapped the spoon. "And watch for this. When he taps the tooth twice, that's when it's coming. That's when he's about to ask you for the thing he really wants." She sighed. "I've said yes to that tap more times than I can count."

Mike looked at her. In eight years, he had never once thought of Priscilla as someone who *knew* anything. She was the one who promised wyverns by Friday. But she had been at that counter longer than anyone, saying yes to every client in Brindlemoor, and it turned out that if you said yes to people for long enough, you learned exactly how they got it out of you.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it.

Then Durgan came up from the Basement, covered in stone dust, to get a pencil, and Velka said, "Durgan, sit in the chair."

Durgan looked at her. He looked at the chair. He sat in it.

He didn't say anything. He didn't do a voice. He didn't hold the spoon. He just sat in the Guildmaster's chair, with his big arms folded across his chest, and looked at Mike, from under his eyebrows, with an expression of complete, bottomless, stony silence.

Mike stood in front of him.

Durgan said nothing.

The silence went on. And on. It filled the room. Mike felt it pressing on him, the way the silence had pressed at the end of every meeting he'd ever been in, the silence that somebody had to fill, the silence that was always, always filled with *yes*.

"Okay," said Mike. "Fine. Yes."

Durgan raised one eyebrow by a quarter of an inch.

"I didn't even ask anything," said Durgan.

**Estimated time until you say yes anyway: 11 seconds.**

Mike sat down on the floor.

They were all quiet for a while. Pip finished a footnote. Velka took off her hat and turned it around in her hands. Durgan, who had not moved from the chair, seemed to be thinking.

Then Tam put his head around the door to say that a lumber guild contract had come in, and Velka said, "Tam. Come and be the Duke."

Tam went white. "Me?"

"You," said Velka. "Sit."

Tam sat in the enormous chair. His feet didn't reach the floor. He held the wooden spoon in both hands, like a candle. He looked at Mike with huge, anxious eyes, and swallowed, and said, in a small, wavering, terrified voice:

"M-Mike. The escort. Please?"

And Mike, who was sitting on the floor and was very tired, and who could not, under any circumstances, bring himself to say yes to a thirteen-year-old in a chair that was too big for him, just to get it over with, heard himself say:

"No, Tam."

There was a silence.

"*No, Your Grace*," corrected Velka.

"No, Your Grace," said Mike.

Tam looked at him. Then, slowly, he smiled. "That was good," he said. "That was really good."

Mike didn't ask the skill. He was afraid of what it would say. He got up off the floor and took the spoon back.

---

Brannoc caught him in the hall on the way out.

"Mike," he said. "Mike, Mike, Mike." He put a big arm around Mike's shoulders and steered him into an alcove, the way people steer you when they're about to explain something to you for your own good. "I hear you're going to see His Grace."

"Yes."

"About the festival."

"Yes."

"Good. Good." Brannoc nodded several times. "Now, I just want to say. I'm not telling you how to do your job. But in my experience, with clients like His Grace, it's never about saying *no*." He made a face, as if the word tasted of something. "It's about *managing expectations*. You don't say no. You say, *let me take that away and look at it*. You say, *we'll see what we can do*. You say, *great idea, let's circle back*." He squeezed Mike's shoulder. "Nobody likes a *no*, Mike."

"No," agreed Mike.

Brannoc looked at him sharply, as if suspecting a trick, and let him go.

Rupert was waiting at the bottom of the stairs. He had a sheet of paper.

"Mike! So glad I caught you." He pressed the paper into Mike's hand. "Tolliver and I put together a few talking points. Just a little *script*. To help you land the message."

Mike read it.

> *Your Grace, we're deeply aligned on the vision of a festival-forward partnership. While our capacity landscape is evolving, we're committed to exploring a range of escort-adjacent solutions, and we look forward to co-creating a festival experience that exceeds expectations.*

Mike read it twice. He read it very carefully, the second time, the way he read contracts from the lumber guild, looking for the clause that would hurt him. And he found it. He found that the whole thing was the clause. It said yes. It said yes in every single line. It just said it wearing a hat, and a false mustache, and a long coat, so that later, when the escort didn't happen, nobody would be able to prove it had ever said anything at all.

"Thanks," said Mike.

He took it back to his desk and filed it, in the drawer with the other things Rupert had written down, and dated it.

Then he put on his coat. At the door, he stopped. And he thought about a forge on Anvil Lane, and a velvet hat going away around a corner, and a woman with forearms like hams who had not even looked up.

*You can say that, you know. It's allowed.*

He went out.

---

The Duke of Varrenhold's hall was on the hill above the river, a long, high, gloomy building full of tapestries and armor and portraits of earlier Dukes, all of whom looked as though they had been promised something and were still waiting for it.

The steward met Mike at the door.

Mike had known the steward a long time. Longer than the steward knew. When Mike was ten years old, scrubbing pots at the Drowned Goose until one in the morning, the steward had been Lord Ashcombe's man, the one who came down to the kitchen at midnight to demand a pie, and then a second pie, and then a third pie, because his lordship couldn't decide. Then Lord Ashcombe had gotten tired of pies, and the steward had moved on, the way a certain kind of person always moves on, to whichever master paid the most for demands in the middle of the night. He was thin and smooth and gray, and he moved like a draft under a door.

"Master Dobbins," he said, with a bow so shallow it was almost an insult. "His Grace is expecting you. Before you go in, I thought we might have a quick *pre-brief*."

He drew Mike aside into a little anteroom. He produced a small card from his sleeve and held it out.

"A few words," said the steward, "that His Grace must never hear. For his own comfort."

Mike looked at the card. It said, in neat copperplate:

> *NO*
>
> *CAN'T*
>
> *LATE*
>
> *IMPOSSIBLE*
>
> *FRIDAY*

"Friday?" said Mike.

"His Grace," said the steward delicately, "has had some disappointments involving Fridays."

Mike thought about Priscilla at the front counter, eight years ago, on his very first morning, beaming at a nervous farmer: *Oh, absolutely, we can have the wyvern removed by Friday.* He thought about every Friday that had ever been promised to anyone, in two worlds. He supposed the Duke had collected quite a lot of them.

"His Grace," said the steward, "has never been disappointed. Not in forty years. It would be very bad for him." He smiled thinly. "And very bad for the guild."

Mike put the card in his pocket. He asked the skill one question on the way in, because he wanted to know how long he had.

**Estimated time until the steward interrupts you: 30 seconds.**

---

The Duke sat at the far end of the long hall, in his great carved chair, holding the dragon tooth.

He looked older than Mike remembered. That was the first thing Mike noticed. Up close, without a crowd around him, without the guild hall's noise, he was just an old man in a very big chair, with a great white beard and tired eyes, and the dragon tooth across his knees like a walking stick.

"MIKE," said the Duke.

"Your Grace."

"THE ESCORT," said the Duke. "MY FESTIVAL." He smiled, a broad, hopeful smile. "BANNERS. DRUMS. BOTH GOLEMS."

Then he lifted the dragon tooth and tapped it, twice, on the arm of the chair.

*Tap. Tap.*

Mike heard Priscilla's voice in his head. *That's when it's coming.*

"SURELY," said the Duke, leaning forward, "SURELY THE GUILD CAN—"

"No, Your Grace," said Mike.

The word came out of him clean and plain and ordinary, like a stone dropped into a pond. He felt it leave. He felt the hall go still around it.

Behind him, exactly thirty seconds after Mike had walked in, the steward came flying through the door with his hand out and his mouth open, crying, "Your Grace, what Master Dobbins *means*—"

He was too late. It was said.

The Duke stared at Mike.

"WHAT?" he said.

Mike took a breath. He did not apologize. He did not soften it. He did not say *we'll see what we can do*. He just explained it, plainly, the way the board explained it, the way he'd explain it to anyone.

"The seal under the guild is failing, Your Grace. If it fails, the demon gets out. The city barrier is drawn from the same seal, so if the seal fails, the barrier fails too. If the barrier fails, the city falls. And the festival with it." He paused. "We have two days. We have enough people to save the seal and keep the guild's records straight, and that's all. We don't have enough people to march. And the old Registrar can't fit through its own door."

The Duke said nothing.

He sat there for a long time, in his great chair, with the tooth across his knees, looking at Mike. The steward stood frozen, halfway across the floor. Somewhere far off in the hall, a clock ticked.

Then the Duke said, in a strange, quiet voice, for him:

"SAY IT AGAIN."

Mike blinked. "Your Grace?"

"SAY IT AGAIN."

"No," said Mike.

Something happened to the Duke's face.

It started around the eyes. It spread down through the great white beard. It was, Mike realized, after a moment, a smile, but not the hopeful one from before. It was a different smile entirely. It was the smile of a man who has been handed something he did not know he had been waiting for.

"NOBODY," said the Duke of Varrenhold, "HAS SAID THAT TO ME IN FORTY YEARS."

He was not angry. He was *delighted*.

"Your Grace," began the steward, rushing forward, "may I bring you some tea, to calm—"

The Duke turned his head and looked at him.

"NO," said the Duke.

The steward stopped dead.

The Duke's eyes widened. He looked down at his own mouth, almost, as if it had done something surprising. He sat up straighter in his chair.

"NO," he said again, experimentally, to nobody in particular. He seemed to taste it. "*NO*."

"A cushion, Your Grace? For your back?"

"NO!"

A footman appeared in the doorway at the far end of the hall. "Your Grace, about lunch—"

"NO!" roared the Duke joyfully. Then he paused. He frowned. "...ACTUALLY, YES. I'M QUITE HUNGRY." He pointed the dragon tooth at the footman. "BUT NO TO THE SOUP."

The footman bowed and fled. The Duke sat back in his chair, beaming, flushed, a little out of breath, like a man who has just ridden a horse for the first time and discovered he isn't going to fall off.

Then he turned back to Mike, and his eyes narrowed. Not unkindly. Thoughtfully. The look of a man who has learned the rules of a new game and wants to see how far it goes.

"ONLY THE OMNIFILE, THEN," he said. "ONE GOLEM. MARCHING."

"No, Your Grace."

"ONE GOLEM ARM. ON A POLE."

"No, Your Grace."

The Duke considered this. He tapped the tooth on his knee.

"A BANNER," he said. "WITH A GOLEM ON IT."

Mike opened his mouth to say no. Then he stopped, and asked the skill, because that was the rule, and because a no was only worth something if it was *true*.

**Estimated time for a banner with a golem on it: 2 hours.**

"A banner," said Mike, "yes. But not before the festival. The day after the audit."

The Duke thought about that for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, with tremendous satisfaction, as if they had just concluded a treaty between nations.

"And there's more," said Mike, because that was the other half of what Brunhild had done, and he'd thought about it all the way up the hill. You didn't just say no. You said what you *could* do, and when, and you meant it. "We'll give you one guard for the parade route. Gareth the Bold. He'll walk beside your carriage the whole way." Gareth would, Mike suspected, be happier about this than about anything that had ever happened to him. "And next year, the whole guild will march at the festival. Banners, drums, everyone. I'll put it on the calendar today. With a real date."

The Duke looked at him.

"A REAL DATE," he said softly.

"A real one."

The Duke was quiet for a long time after that.

---

When he spoke again, he did it in what was, for him, a low voice. Which was to say, ordinary shouting.

"DO YOU KNOW," he said, "HOW MANY THINGS I HAVE BEEN PROMISED?"

Mike thought about the Duke's file in the guild records. It was the thickest file in the building. It was thicker than the lumber guild's.

"A lot," said Mike.

"EVERYTHING," said the Duke. "I HAVE BEEN PROMISED *EVERYTHING*. A NEW BRIDGE, BY SPRING. IT WAS LATE. SWORDS FOR MY GUARD. THEY BROKE. AN ESCORT AT DAWN. IT CAME AT NOON. A CARRIAGE THAT COULD FLY." He waved the tooth vaguely in the direction of the river. "IT FLEW. *ONCE*. FOR A MINUTE. THEN IT TURNED UPSIDE DOWN." He shook his head. "FORTY YEARS. AND EVERY SINGLE PERSON WHO EVER CAME THROUGH THAT DOOR SAID *YES*. YES, YOUR GRACE. OF COURSE, YOUR GRACE. BY FRIDAY, YOUR GRACE." He sighed, an enormous sigh that seemed to come up from the floor. "AND THEN NOTHING WAS EVER ON TIME, AND NOTHING EVER WORKED, AND NOBODY EVER TOLD ME WHY."

He looked down at the dragon tooth.

"DO YOU KNOW WHY I SAY *ADEQUATE*?"

Mike shook his head.

"BECAUSE IT'S THE HIGHEST WORD THAT'S EVER BEEN TRUE," said the Duke simply.

Mike didn't say anything. He found that he couldn't.

"YOU ARE THE FIRST PERSON," said the Duke, "IN FORTY YEARS, WHO HAS COME IN HERE AND TOLD ME SOMETHING I DIDN'T WANT TO HEAR. AND ALSO THE FIRST PERSON WHO TOLD ME WHEN SOMETHING *WOULD* HAPPEN AND SOUNDED AS IF HE KNEW." He nodded. "THAT IS WORTH MORE THAN AN ESCORT."

There was a pause.

And because it seemed like a day for true things, Mike asked a question he'd wanted to ask for a long time.

"Your Grace," he said. "Why do you need an escort every night? At midnight?"

The Duke looked at him blankly.

"I DON'T," he said.

Mike stared at him.

"Your Grace. The night bell. Every night, since the guild put it up. Midnight, sometimes later. *The Duke of Varrenhold requires an escort.* We've sent someone every single night."

"I HAVE NEVER REQUIRED AN ESCORT AT MIDNIGHT IN MY LIFE," said the Duke, puzzled. He frowned, searching his memory. "I ONCE MENTIONED," he said slowly, "AT DINNER, THAT I COULDN'T SLEEP. ONCE." He shook his head. "I ONLY MENTIONED IT ONCE."

Mike reached into his jacket.

He had brought the Duke's file. He had brought it because he brought files to everything; because the board said *bring what you know*; because, in two lives, he had learned that the person with the records was the person who got to say what had happened. He opened it now to the back section, the thick one, the one he'd kept for every single night, because somebody had to sign off on every escort, and that somebody had always been him.

"Four hundred and twelve escorts, Your Grace," he said. "Every night since the bell went up. Billed at the guild's premium night rate. Requested through your steward. Each one with a standing order, signed—" He turned the page around. "—on your behalf."

The Duke looked at the page. He looked at it for a long time.

"WHERE," he said, "DID THEY ESCORT ME?"

Mike checked the notes. He had made notes. Of course he had made notes.

"Mostly," he said, "they stood outside your window."

The Duke absorbed this.

"I WAS ASLEEP," he said.

"Yes, Your Grace."

"FOUR HUNDRED AND TWELVE NIGHTS, THE GUILD SENT ARMED ADVENTURERS TO STAND OUTSIDE MY WINDOW. IN THE DARK. WHILE I WAS ASLEEP."

"Yes, Your Grace."

"AT A *PREMIUM RATE*."

"Yes, Your Grace."

The Duke turned his great white head, very slowly, all the way around, to look at the steward.

The steward was no longer standing in the middle of the floor. He had, at some point in the last minute, drifted backward, very quietly, like a draft, until he was standing just beside the door.

"Your Grace," said the steward, "I've just remembered an urgent errand."

And he was gone.

The Duke watched the door for a while after it closed. Then he turned back to Mike.

"FOUR HUNDRED AND TWELVE," he said, shaking his head. And then, to Mike's astonishment, he began to laugh. It was a great, booming, rolling laugh that shook the dusty tapestries on the walls, and it went on for some time.

"IT'S NOT FUNNY," said the Duke, wiping his eyes. "IT'S NOT FUNNY AT ALL." He laughed some more. "OUTSIDE MY *WINDOW*."

---

Mike bowed, and turned to go. He had done what he'd come to do. *DONE MEANS: Mike still alive.* He was still alive. He wanted very much to leave while that was still true.

He had nearly reached the door when the Duke called after him.

"MIKE."

He turned.

"MY WALL," said the Duke. "MY GUESTS WILL STILL SEE MY WALL. AFTER THE FESTIVAL. YES?"

Mike stopped.

He thought about Brannoc's office. The purple curtain, eleven silver crowns, *Office Improvements (Urgent)*. The two hundred and thirteen gilt frames behind it, floor to ceiling. The word that had crawled across every one of them, in red, like a worm, the night the OMNIFILE stamped the Duke's paperwork.

He thought about what Brannoc would want him to say. *Of course, Your Grace. The wall looks wonderful, Your Grace. Let's circle back.*

And he thought about the man in the chair, who had just told him that *adequate* was the highest word that had ever been true.

He wasn't going to lie to the one client who had just thanked him for the truth.

"Your Grace," he said. "About the wall. There's something you should know. The frames—"

The Duke held up the dragon tooth.

"NO," he said.

He said it with tremendous relish, the way a child says a word it has just learned is allowed.

"DON'T TELL ME," said the Duke. "I'LL COME AND SEE IT MYSELF. *AFTER*."

And he smiled. It was a slow, knowing, peculiar smile, and it went all the way up into his tired old eyes, and Mike, looking at it, had a sudden and terrible feeling that the Duke of Varrenhold already knew exactly what the wall said, and had known for some time, and was very much looking forward to seeing Brannoc's face.

"Yes, Your Grace," said Mike, and went out.

On his way down the hill, he realized that it was the first time all day he'd said *yes, Your Grace* and meant it.

---

Brannoc was pacing.

He was pacing in the main hall, in a long, tight oval around the front counter, holding his empty mug, and when Mike came in through the front door, he stopped pacing and stared at him with the face of a man waiting to hear whether his house has burned down.

"Well?" said Brannoc.

"I told him no," said Mike.

Brannoc made a sound like a bellows with a hole in it. He sat down heavily on the end of a bench. "We've lost him," he said. "Forty years. The Duke's account. *Forty years*. We've lost him. He'll take his business to Tanner's Row. He'll—"

The front door opened, and a runner came in, out of breath, in the Duke's colors. He was carrying a letter sealed with a boar standing on a smaller boar. Mike took it from him before anyone else could, carefully, by the edges. The Duke's paperwork was never, ever stamped. Not by anyone. That was the oldest rule in the building, and the one that had started all the trouble with the wall.

He broke the seal and read it out.

> *ESCORT DECLINED. GOOD.*
>
> *ALSO: THE NIGHT BELL IS NO LONGER REQUIRED. IT WAS NEVER REQUIRED. PLEASE TAKE IT DOWN.*
>
> *ALSO: THE STEWARD HAS LEFT MY SERVICE. HE DOES NOT KNOW THIS YET.*
>
> *ALSO: MY HONOR GUARD, TWENTY MEN, ARE AT THE GUILD'S DISPOSAL UNTIL THE DEMON BUSINESS IS DONE. PUT THEM WHEREVER THEY ARE USEFUL. THEY HAVE BEEN TOLD TO DO WHAT THEY ARE ASKED AND NOT TO ASK FOR ANYTHING. THEY FIND THIS CONFUSING. SO DO I. IT WILL BE GOOD FOR EVERYONE.*
>
> *— V.*
>
> *P.S. NO.*

Mike looked up from the letter. Brannoc was staring at him.

Then something happened in Brannoc's face. It cleared, like weather. He stood up. He squared his shoulders. He looked around the hall, at the clerks at their desks, at the adventurers at their tables, at Priscilla at the counter, at the Champions by the window, and he nodded slowly, with the deep satisfaction of a man who has been proven right.

"I always said," said Brannoc, "we should be straight with him."

Mike looked at him for a long moment.

He didn't say anything. He just handed Brannoc the letter, and went upstairs.

---

The war room was empty except for Durgan, who had come up from the Basement for more chalk. He was standing in front of the board, in front of the right-hand column, looking at the line with Mike's name on it.

*HARVEST FESTIVAL ESCORT. Tell the Duke. MIKE. DONE MEANS: Mike still alive.*

Durgan looked around as Mike came in. He looked Mike up and down, carefully, the way he looked at a ward that had taken a hit, checking it for cracks.

Then he turned back to the board, took a piece of chalk, and wrote beside the line, in square, careful letters:

*DONE.*

And under it, after a moment's thought, a small, neat check mark.

He put the chalk back in the tray. He didn't say anything. He went back down to the Basement.

Mike stood looking at the check mark for a long time. It was, he thought, the closest Durgan would ever come in his life to saying *I was worried about you*. And it was, in its way, the nicest thing anyone had ever written about him.

---

That night, for the first time since the bell went up, Mike went home and took his boots off.

He did it slowly. He sat on the edge of his bed, in his small room, and unlaced them, and pulled them off, one and then the other, and set them side by side on the floor. He looked at them for a while. Then he lay down.

Before he closed his eyes, he asked the skill.

**Estimated time until the night bell rings again: never.**

He slept.

At midnight, out of more than a year of pure habit, he sat bolt upright in the dark with his heart pounding and one hand already reaching for a boot.

He listened.

The street was quiet. A neighbor's cat was asleep on the windowsill. Somewhere across the city, the river moved slowly past the dock, past the upside-down Contraption, under the stars. Up on the hill, the Duke of Varrenhold was asleep in his bed, with nobody at all standing outside his window. And in the guild hall, behind the front counter, a bell hung on its hook, perfectly still, waiting for a rope that nobody would ever pull again.

He heard nothing at all.

Mike lay back down, and pulled the blanket up, and went back to sleep.

---

The next morning, there was a new bulletin on the front board.

> *GUILD BULLETIN: CLIENT SUCCESS*
>
> *The Office of the Chief Ink Officer is proud to announce a HISTORIC DUKE ALIGNMENT, achieved through the Ink Office's proactive expectation-management framework.*
>
> *Special thanks to the Convergence Champions for their contribution to messaging.*
>
> *— R. Spindlewick, C.I.O.*

Mike read it twice, standing in the hall with his tea.

Then he went to his desk, took out the drawer with Rupert's papers in it, and filed the bulletin, very neatly, in front of the other sheet: the one that said *we're deeply aligned on the vision of a festival-forward partnership*. He clipped them together, so that anyone who ever opened that drawer would see them both at once, side by side, in Rupert's own words.

Then he went to the front counter and took down the night bell.
