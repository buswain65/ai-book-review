# Chapter 25: Legacy

Nobody had to be told what to do.

That was the thing Mike would remember afterward, longer than the claw, longer than the voice, longer than the door. The floor shuddered, and Pip said *black*, and the skill said *forty-seven minutes*, and for one long second, the whole hall stood frozen, the way it had stood frozen at every crisis Mike had ever seen at the guild, waiting for someone to stand on a table and shout.

Nobody stood on a table.

Instead, one by one, without a word, people turned and looked up the stairs toward the war room. And Mike realized they weren't looking for Brannoc. They were looking for the board. They had all read it. They all knew what it said. And it said, beside every one of their names, exactly what to do.

So they did it.

Velka was the first to move. She was out the front door before anyone else had taken a step, pulling her hat down, shouting for the Duke's honor guard. Half of them went with her at a run, toward the east gate and the barrier, with their swords at their hips and confused, eager expressions on their faces, like men who had been waiting their whole lives for someone to give them a clear instruction. The other half fell in behind Durgan, who was already halfway down the Basement ladder with his tool bag.

Pip went the other way, out the back, at a speed Mike had never seen a gnome achieve, toward the east gate tower and the old archmage.

Gareth the Bold walked to the top of the crypt stairs, turned around, drew his sword, and planted his feet.

"Nobody shall pass," he announced, to the hall at large. He thought about it. "Who is a demon."

And Sir Roderick Ashvale, of the Order of the Lantern, went to the crypt door, and stopped.

---

The crypt under the guild did not have a proper door. It had an archway, at the bottom of the Basement, behind the furnace, where the old stone steps went down into the dark. Mike had been down them once, a week ago, with Durgan. He had not looked around much. He had been too busy looking at the seal.

Now he stood at the top of the steps beside Sir Roderick, and looked down, and saw what Roderick was seeing.

Nothing. Dark, and the smell of very old stone, and the faint, cold, wrong feeling that comes up out of places that have been underground for a long time. Somewhere far below, too faint to be sure of, something was breathing.

Sir Roderick held a scrap of paper in one gauntleted hand. It was Pip's copy of Thessaly's napkin: a little sketch of the crypt, in Pip's careful hand, with a note at the bottom saying *(copied from napkin; original had gravy on it)*. There were five rooms on it. Three of them were labeled *?*.

Sir Roderick looked at the paper. He looked at the dark. Mike could see his jaw working inside his helmet.

The oath of the Order of the Lantern was very simple, and Roderick had never broken it. *No knight of the Lantern shall enter a legacy dungeon that has not been documented.* He had stood at the edges of a dozen old ruins and refused to go in. He had been called a coward for it, and stubborn, and worse. He had only ever gone into one, the Sunken Crypt, and only because Mike had gone ahead of him on his hands and knees with a lantern and a notebook, and drawn every room before Roderick set foot in it.

There was no time for that now. Mike knew it. Roderick knew it.

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 38 minutes.**

Sir Roderick turned around. He looked at Mike.

He held out his hand.

Mike didn't understand for a second. Then he did. He reached into his jacket pocket, where he always kept a stick of chalk, because you never knew when you'd need to label something, and put it in Roderick's palm.

Sir Roderick closed his gauntlet around it.

"If it isn't documented," he said, "then I'll document it."

And he went down the stairs into the dark.

Mike followed with a lantern. Behind Mike came Sister Agatha, holding up her gray robes with one hand and her little cloth bag with the other. Behind her came Durgan and the Duke's men, and then, at a distance, very carefully, Brannoc, holding his empty mug as though it might be needed for something.

At the bottom of the first flight, Sir Roderick stopped, raised the chalk, and wrote on the wall in large, careful letters:

*ROOM 1: Stairs.*

They went on. At the bottom of the second flight, he stopped again.

*ROOM 2: More stairs.*

The third room was wider. There was an alcove in one wall. In the alcove, sitting up very straight with its bony hands folded in its lap, as if waiting for an appointment, was a skeleton. It wore the remains of a guild clerk's tabard.

Sir Roderick looked at it for a long moment. The skeleton looked back.

He wrote: *ROOM 3: Skeleton (friendly?).*

The fourth room had another skeleton. This one was not sitting up straight. This one was lying across the doorway at an awkward angle, in pieces, and had clearly been in a fight with something, and had clearly lost, and the something had clearly been very large.

Sir Roderick wrote: *ROOM 4: Skeleton (not friendly). Avoid.* He thought about it, and added an arrow showing which way to go around.

Mike, holding the lantern, found that he was smiling. Every few steps, Roderick stopped and wrote something. A crack in the ceiling. A step that was loose. A doorway that was lower than it looked. By the time they reached the bottom, the walls of the old crypt behind them were covered in neat white chalk notes, the first documentation that place had seen in two hundred years.

It was, Mike thought, the most heroic thing he'd ever seen anyone do in a dungeon.

---

The seal chamber was at the very bottom.

It was round, and cold, and low-ceilinged, and in the middle of the floor was the great ring of carved runes that Mike had knelt beside a week ago. Then, it had been mostly gray, with one rune glowing faintly red near the edge. Now, it was not mostly anything. Half the ring was black. Not dark, or shadowed. *Black*, like a hole, like the runes had been carved out of the floor and nothing had been put back in their place. The blackness was spreading, slowly, rune by rune, around the circle, the way ink spreads across wet paper.

Durgan was already there. He had come down the short way, by a shaft only he knew about, and he was on his knees at the edge of the black with his chisel, carving. He was carving as fast as Mike had ever seen anyone carve, his big arms moving like pistons, stone chips flying, new runes appearing one after another along the edge of the spreading dark.

It wasn't fast enough. Mike could see it wasn't. For every rune Durgan carved, the black took two.

And then the floor cracked.

It cracked right down the middle of the ring, with a sound like a tree splitting, and a long, jagged line of darkness opened across the stone. Through it came a breath of air so hot and old and stale that the lantern flame bent sideways. And from far below, through the crack, rising slowly, like something surfacing from the bottom of a very deep lake, came a voice.

It was enormous. It was slow. It was furious. And it was, unmistakably, very, very sleepy.

"WHO," said the voice. "MOVED. THE DOOR."

Nobody answered. Nobody could.

"TWO HUNDRED YEARS," said the voice, getting louder, and angrier, and more awake. "TWO HUNDRED YEARS I HAVE BEEN DOWN HERE. NOBODY TOUCHES ANYTHING. I SLEEP. IT IS VERY NICE. AND THEN—" There was a pause, a deep, grinding, resentful pause. "THEN SOMEBODY HAS A *QUICK WIN*."

Behind Mike, Brannoc made a small noise.

"DO YOU KNOW," said the demon, "HOW MANY TIMES I HAVE BEEN WOKEN UP? BY YOU PEOPLE? CHANGING THINGS?" There was a sound from the dark that might have been a page turning. A very large page. "I'LL TELL YOU. I'LL TELL YOU EXACTLY. BECAUSE I WROTE THEM DOWN."

And the demon began to read.

"THE DOOR," it said. "MOVED. TO THE OTHER WALL. FOR *NO REASON*."

"A BELL. AT MIDNIGHT. EVERY NIGHT. FOR OVER A YEAR."

"A FANFARE. *A FANFARE*. AT THREE O'CLOCK IN THE MORNING. FROM A GOLEM."

"SOMEBODY FED THE GOLEM. AFTER DUSK. DO YOU KNOW WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU FEED A GOLEM AFTER DUSK? I DO. I *HEARD* IT."

"A SHEEP," said the demon, with tremendous bitterness. "IN A HAT. I COULD *HEAR* THE HAT."

It went on. It went on for some time. A gong. A second gong. A stand-up that lasted ninety minutes, during which someone in full plate armor fell over. An eleven-course tasting menu, eaten directly above it, very loudly. Every quick win, every small change, every *won't take a minute*, in order, with dates, going back years.

Pip, who had arrived at the bottom of the stairs halfway through the list, out of breath, with his slate in his hand, stood frozen in the doorway with his mouth open. His eyes were huge behind his spectacles. He looked horrified. He also looked, Mike thought, more thrilled than he had ever looked in his life.

"It *documented* it," Pip whispered.

"I WROTE IT ALL DOWN," roared the demon, so loudly that dust fell from the ceiling. "*SOMEBODY* HAD TO."

And through the crack in the floor, slowly, came a claw.

It was black, and gleaming, and it was the size of a cart. It came up through the crack like a tree growing, and the stone groaned around it, and it opened, finger by finger, each one longer than Mike was tall, and felt about blindly across the floor of the chamber, the way a man feels for his slippers in the dark.

Far above them, from the top of the Basement ladder, a calm, pleasant voice drifted down the stairs.

"Afternoon, Vorgath," called Malphus. "Your account is current."

The claw stopped.

From very deep in the dark, there was a pause.

"OH," said the demon. "*YOU*."

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 9 minutes.**

---

Pip had not come alone.

Behind him, being carried down the last flight of stairs in his sedan chair by four of the Duke's honor guard, wrapped in his blanket with his lizard peering out of the top of it, came Thessaly, Archmage of Brindlemoor, retired.

The guards set the chair down at the edge of the chamber. Thessaly looked around, at the low stone ceiling, at the ring of runes, at the black spreading across it, at the crack, and at the enormous claw groping slowly across the floor toward them. His ancient face lit up with pure, delighted recognition.

"Oh!" said Thessaly. "I remember this one!"

"Sir," said Mike. "The seal. It's failing. We need to know how it works. Right now."

"Oh, yes, of course, of course." Thessaly nodded happily and settled back in his chair. "Well. It all started with Bettany."

There was a groan from every person in the chamber, including, Mike was fairly sure, the demon.

"Bettany was my goat," said Thessaly fondly. "This was, oh, a hundred and eighty years ago now. Wonderful goat. Followed me everywhere. She ate *everything*, you know. Ate my spell notes once. Ate a whole hat."

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 6 minutes.**

"Sir," said Mike.

"Now, the founders wanted it done for the grand opening, you see," said Thessaly, "and they wanted it done cheap, and Bettany had got into the storeroom that week, and—"

Pip was writing. He was writing faster than Mike had ever seen him write, his stylus scratching across his slate, his eyes darting between the old man and the claw. He didn't interrupt. He didn't hurry. Mike understood, watching him, that Pip had done this before, in the round room at the top of the east gate, all those afternoons. He knew you couldn't hurry Thessaly. You could only steer.

"And the *door frame*, sir?" said Pip, gently, between sentences.

"Oh, Bettany *loved* door frames," said Thessaly. "Loved to chew on them. Something about the wood. Did I ever tell you about the weathervane?"

"You did, sir," said Pip. "Twice. The door frame?"

"Well, she fell in love with it, you see. The weathervane. On top of the old mill. Used to stand at the bottom and *bleat* at it, all night—"

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 2 minutes.**

The claw had reached the edge of the ring. It closed slowly around one of the gray runes, the last ones still holding, and the rune began, very faintly, to darken.

Thessaly was describing the weathervane in great detail.

---

At thirty seconds, Sister Agatha stood up.

She had been sitting on the bottom step all this time, quite calmly, with her cloth bag on her knees. Now she set the bag down, and smoothed her robes, and walked across the chamber, past Durgan and his chisel, past Roderick and his chalk, past Mike and his lantern, to the edge of the crack. She looked down at the enormous black claw with an expression of mild, professional interest, the way she looked at a sprained ankle or a broken arm or Gareth's toe.

"Well," she said. "I can't do anything about the seal. It isn't alive."

She reached out and laid one small, wrinkled hand flat on the back of the claw.

"But *that* is," said Sister Agatha. And she patted it.

Saint Redundia, Goddess of Backups, was not a showy goddess. There was no flash of light. There was no choir. There was just a soft, faint, gray glow around Agatha's hand, like early morning, and a feeling in the air of something being very gently put back where it belonged.

And the demon, exactly as Saint Redundia's blessing always did, was restored to precisely the way it had been three days ago. Body, and mind, and memories included.

Three days ago, the demon had been asleep.

The claw went limp. The fingers uncurled from the rune. The great black hand sagged, and settled, and then began to slide, slowly, back down through the crack into the dark. And from far below, sleepy and fading and suddenly very far away, the voice said:

"...five... more... minutes..."

And then, rising up through the floor of the chamber, long and deep and slow and enormously content, came a snore.

Everyone stood very still.

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 3 days.**

He let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding for a week. Around the room, he heard everyone else do the same. One of the Duke's guards sat down on the floor. Sir Roderick leaned on the wall, very carefully, so as not to smudge his chalk.

Sister Agatha brushed off her hands, went back to her step, sat down, opened her cloth bag, and took out her lunch.

And Thessaly, who had not noticed any of this, went right on talking.

"...and so, because Bettany kept eating the west corner of the door frame, *every single night*, I thought, well, I can't put the anchor rune in the floor like I'd planned, she'll get at it there, too, she gets at *everything*, so I put it somewhere she couldn't reach it."

Pip's stylus stopped.

"Where, sir?" he said quietly.

"In the door frame," said Thessaly. "The *top* of the door frame. Carved it myself, right into the lintel. Couldn't reach that, could she?" He chuckled. "Well. She tried."

There was a silence in the chamber.

"The anchor rune," said Mike slowly. "The rune that holds the whole seal. It isn't down here."

"Oh, no," said Thessaly. "It's in the door."

"The *front* door?"

"Well, of course the front door. The seal holds on to the door, the door holds on to the seal. That's the whole design." He beamed around at them. "Lovely and simple. Never had a lick of trouble with it, in all the years I was looking after it. As long as nobody—"

He stopped.

He looked around at their faces, one by one. Something slowly dawned in his ancient, watery eyes.

"You *moved* my door?" said Thessaly, sounding genuinely hurt.

Nobody said anything.

Durgan was still on his knees by the seal, with the chisel in his hand. He had stopped carving. He was looking down at the ring of runes, at the black half and the gray half, at the hundreds of small, bright, careful patches that he had carved into it over nineteen years, one after another, every time something went wrong, every time a rune darkened, every time a scroll came down the ladder.

"Nineteen years," said Durgan.

He said it very quietly. Mike had never heard him say anything quietly before.

"Nineteen years," said Durgan. "Didn't know it was the door."

Nobody had told him. Nobody had ever written it down. The answer had been sitting, all that time, inside a story about a goat, in the head of an old man at the top of a tower, waiting for somebody to sit still long enough to hear it. Durgan had spent nineteen years patching a crack whose cause was on another floor of the building. And every time it broke, they had blamed his patches.

Durgan looked at the seal for a while longer. Then he stood up, slowly, knees cracking, and put the chisel back in his bag.

"Backup's not a fix," he said, to Mike. He nodded at the floor, and the snoring. "Same seal. Same crack. Wakes up again in three days." He looked at the stairs. "Need the door."

---

Brannoc had been standing at the back of the chamber this whole time. He hadn't said a word since the demon started reading its list. He was standing very still, holding his empty mug, and his face, in the lantern light, was a color Mike had never seen it before.

Now he cleared his throat. Everyone turned to look at him.

"Well," said Brannoc. "Can't we just—"

He stopped.

He stopped right in the middle of the sentence, with his mouth still open. Everyone in the chamber saw it happen. They saw the rest of the sentence come all the way up to the front of his mouth, the way it always did, the way it had been coming out of him, fully formed, for as long as anyone could remember. *Can't we just move the demon. Can't we just move the door. Can't we just do it faster. Can't we just.*

And they saw him stop it.

He closed his mouth. He looked at the seal. He looked at the crack. He looked at Durgan, and at Thessaly, and at the chalk on the walls, and at Mike.

"We move it back," said Brannoc.

---

The whole guild moved the door.

It was not, it turned out, a door so much as a wall with a hole in it. The front door of the guild hall was set in a frame of solid stone, three feet thick and twelve feet high, carved all over with old runes, and it had taken a team of masons to move it to the other wall in the first place. It weighed more than the Registrar. It weighed, Pip estimated, more than a small church.

"I can lift it," said Velka.

She had come back from the east gate at a run when the runner reached her, with her honor guards panting behind her, and she was standing in the middle of the hall in her soot-stained coat and her brown hat, looking up at the door frame with her fists on her hips.

"I've got a new spell," she said. "I've been working on it all month. Gravity inversion. Full-field. I think it could lift the whole wall. Maybe the whole hall."

Mike didn't say anything. He just looked at her.

Velka looked back at him. Then she looked at the door frame again. Then she sighed, a long, deep sigh, the sigh of a mage who has been dreaming of casting something enormous in front of a crowd since she was nine years old.

"No," she said. "Float 1.0."

It was her oldest spell. Her plainest. Her most boring. It was the spell that had floated a little toy wagon in a washbasin, and then a real wagon on a muddy horse pond, thirty-one times in a row, every night after the guild closed, with Mike writing down what happened. It was the spell that had held the Contraption level on the river when everything else on it had gone wrong. It had never done anything impressive in its life. It had only ever done exactly what it said.

Velka raised her hands and cast it.

The door frame rose off the ground. Six inches. Exactly six inches, no more and no less, steady and level as a table, humming very faintly, the way the wagon had hummed on the pond.

Gareth the Bold, who had come down from the crypt stairs to watch, looked at it with great interest.

"Can it go higher?" he asked.

"Untested," said Velka.

Mike had never in his life been prouder of a single word.

---

They carried it across the hall.

Everybody carried it. Durgan took one corner and Pip, standing on a box, took the bottom of the same corner. Ten of the Duke's honor guard took the long sides. Sir Roderick, chalk still in his gauntlet, took a section of the lintel. Tam and Osric, who was very small and very old and had never carried anything heavier than a ledger, each took a handle that Durgan had hammered into the stone for them. Priscilla took a piece, and Gareth took a piece, and Sister Agatha, having finished her lunch, took a piece, mostly for moral support. The Champions took pieces. Dex and Bramble and Tolliver all crowded in along one side.

And Rupert was handed a corner. He took it with a look of great surprise, as if he had not expected the corners to be real.

"I just want to say," Rupert announced, as they began to move, a little breathlessly, "that what we're doing here is not a *rollback*."

Everyone ignored him.

"It's a *relaunch*," said Rupert. "I think we need to be really clear on that, messaging-wise. This is Door 2.0. A legacy-informed entryway." He shifted his grip on the corner, and took out a scrap of paper and a pencil from his pocket with his free hand, and began, as they walked, to draft. "*Door 2.0 Launch*," he murmured. "*Leveraging heritage architecture for next-generation*—"

"Rupert," said Durgan, without turning around. "Lift."

Rupert lifted.

And at the heaviest end, the front end, where the great carved lintel was and where most of the stone was, Brannoc walked backward across his own guild hall, holding the door frame up with both of his big arms, red in the face, sweating through his best tunic. His empty mug was in his belt. He had put it there without being asked.

"I moved it," he had said, when Durgan assigned the corners, and walked straight to the front end before anyone could stop him. "I'll carry it."

Nobody had argued. Mike didn't think anyone had known what to say.

They were halfway across the hall, moving slowly and steadily, the door frame floating level six inches above the floorboards, when Brannoc stopped walking. Everyone stopped with him. He looked up at the frame. He looked at the empty space in the far wall where it was going. He looked at the old marks on the floor, from two hundred years ago, where it had stood.

"While we've got it up," said Brannoc thoughtfully, "what if we put it a little to the left?"

Every single person in the hall froze.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. Nobody breathed. The door frame hung in the air above the floor, humming. Durgan's knuckles went white. Pip dropped his pencil. Thessaly, in his sedan chair by the hatch, made a small, horrified sound. Even Rupert stopped drafting.

The silence went on for a long, long time.

"...That was a joke," said Brannoc.

He looked around at all of them.

It was, Mike was fairly sure, the first joke anyone had ever heard Brannoc make. In eight years. In any language. Nobody was entirely sure it *had* been a joke. Nobody was sure Brannoc was sure. They all stood there, holding a door in the air, looking at the Guildmaster's face, trying to work it out.

And then Velka laughed. And then Priscilla laughed. And then Pip, and Sister Agatha, and the Duke's men, and then, to everyone's astonishment, Durgan, a short, gruff, rumbling bark of a laugh that nobody at the guild had ever heard before, and then all of them, the whole guild, laughing, holding a door in the air in the middle of the hall.

Brannoc looked faintly pleased, and faintly confused, and faintly as though he might want to try that again some time.

"Lift," said Durgan, still laughing, and they lifted.

From his chair beside the Basement hatch, where he had sat through all of it with his legs crossed and his cup of tea balanced on his knee, Malphus took a small, satisfied sip.

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time to move the door back: 41 minutes.**

He didn't look at the clock after that. He didn't need to. He just carried his piece of the door, and listened to Rupert drafting, and to Thessaly in his chair telling the honor guard about the weathervane, and to the soft, steady hum of Velka's oldest spell.

At forty-one minutes, exactly, they lifted the great stone frame up and over the old marks on the floor and set it down into the hole in the original wall where it had stood for two hundred years. Velka lowered her hands. The frame sank the last six inches. And it settled into place with a deep, low, solid *click*, like a key turning in a lock.

Thessaly struggled up out of his sedan chair. He shuffled across the hall in his blanket, with his lizard peering out of the top, and he reached up as high as he could, and laid his ancient, spotted hand flat against the underside of the lintel, against a small, worn rune that nobody had looked at in two hundred years.

It lit.

Far below them, down the Basement ladder, down the stairs past *ROOM 1: Stairs* and *ROOM 2: More stairs* and the friendly skeleton and the unfriendly one, in the round chamber at the very bottom, Durgan would tell them afterward, the black runes in the great ring began to change. One by one. Black, to red. And red, slowly, to plain gray stone.

And the crack in the floor closed.

---

That evening, when the hall was empty and quiet again and the honor guard had gone home and Thessaly had been carried back up to his tower, Durgan came up the ladder with his chisel.

He went to the door. He looked at it for a while. On the inside of the old frame, there were two small notes, carved years apart. One was in a wizard's thin, faded script: *TEMPORARY. REPLACE BEFORE WINTER.* The other was in Durgan's own square letters, from the night he'd patched the seal after the door was moved: *TEMPORARY. SORRY.*

He didn't touch them. He moved a little farther along the frame, to a clean patch of stone at eye height, and began to carve.

Mike watched from his desk behind Osric's stack. It took Durgan nearly an hour. When he was done, he stepped back.

> *LOAD-BEARING.*
>
> *HOLDS THE SEAL.*
>
> *DO NOT MOVE.*
>
> *SEE GUIDE, PAGE 1.*

Underneath it, in thin, spidery, delighted letters, Thessaly had already signed his name that afternoon, in chalk, before they carried him home. Durgan carved over the chalk so it would stay. And underneath *that*, in tiny letters, Pip had added a footnote. Mike went over to read it.

> *¹ See also: Bettany (goat).*

At the Basement hatch, there was a scraping sound. Sir Roderick Ashvale came up the ladder, very slowly. His armor was gray with dust. His cape was torn. The stick of chalk in his gauntlet had been worn down to a stub the size of a fingernail. He had stayed down in the crypt all afternoon and all evening, long after everyone else had come up, because, he said, he was not going to leave until the map was finished. Every room. Every stair. Every skeleton.

"Done," said Sir Roderick, to nobody in particular.

He sat down on the bottom step of the stairs and fell asleep sitting up, in full plate, with his chalk still in his hand.

Mike went up to the war room.

The board was still there, exactly as they'd left it, a long time ago, it felt like, in a different week. *WHAT ACTUALLY MATTERS.* *1. THE SEAL. DURGAN. DONE MEANS: Red rune not red.* He picked up the chalk and wrote beside it, in small, neat letters:

*DONE.*

He looked at the board for a long time. Every line in the left-hand column now had the same word beside it. *DONE. DONE. DONE (to Tam). DONE. Mike still alive.* The right-hand column, *NOT DOING*, was still there too: the float, the cape, the armory roof, the feast, the six gestures of goodwill, *MOVE DEMON?* Not one of them had been done. And the guild was still standing.

He asked the skill.

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: unknown.**

Mike stood in the war room and smiled.

A week ago, *unknown* would have frightened him. He'd have lain awake over it. Now he knew what it meant. It meant the answer wasn't written down anywhere yet. It meant it depended on them. On whether anyone ever moved the door again. On whether anyone ever read page one of the guide. On whether, the next time somebody had a quick win, somebody else said no.

It meant the future was *theirs*.

He was about to put the chalk down and go home. Then, out of pure curiosity, because he was who he was and he couldn't help it, he asked one more question.

**Estimated time until someone suggests moving the door again: 4 years, 2 months.**

Mike laughed out loud, alone in the war room, at nine o'clock at night.

Then he wrote it on the board, in the bottom corner, under *Plan owner: M. Dobbins*, so that whoever came after him would know.
