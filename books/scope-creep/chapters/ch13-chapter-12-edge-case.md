# Chapter 12: Edge Case

The morning after the Grand Reopening, the guild hall was warm.

Not pleasantly warm, the way a hall gets with a good fire going, or a crowd. It was warm the way a room is warm when there is something in it that shouldn't be: a dense, close, faintly sticky heat that seemed to come up from the floor rather than down from the sun. Mike felt it the moment he walked through the new front door. He stopped on the threshold, under the copied runes, and stood there for a moment with the hair rising on the back of his neck.

Then he noticed the candles.

There were twenty of them in the main hall, in brass sconces along the walls, lit every morning by the night watchman before he went home. Every single flame was leaning. Not flickering, not guttering, but leaning, steadily, all in the same direction, as if a wind were blowing that nobody could feel. They leaned north. Toward the old wall. Toward the bricked-up doorway with its little brass plaque.

Mike walked the length of the hall, looking at each one. Every flame pointed the same way, like twenty small, bright fingers.

It got worse from there.

The stand-up dog would not come in. It sat on the cobbles outside the new door, whining, and when Gareth tried to drag it inside by the scruff, it lay down flat and refused to move. Duchess the sheep stood beside it, equally immovable, and stared through the doorway at something nobody else could see.

Priscilla came out of her office with a fresh contract and a look of puzzlement. "Has anyone been at my ink?" she asked the room. She held up the page. Every word on it had been written in her usual violet, but somewhere between the quill and the paper, the ink had dried a deep, wet, rusty red.

Around the north side of the building, the tourist in the straw hat, who had apparently returned to lean on the heritage plaque every morning since he'd discovered it, leaned on it and leapt back with a yelp. The brass was too hot to touch. He complained loudly to the tour company. The tour company complained loudly to Priscilla, who sold them a small canvas awning for the plaque, "for comfort."

And from the back room, the Registrar was humming.

It was a low, steady, rising sound, like a beehive in a stone wall. Mike went and stood outside the door and listened to it, and did not go in. He knew the rule. He had written it down himself, on page 41. *Rule 23: If the Registrar begins to hum, step back and wait. Do not try to help.*

He stepped back. He waited. And he asked the skill.

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 46 days.**

One day gone. Exactly as promised.

---

Brannoc noticed nothing, until he walked into his office.

Mike heard the roar from the other end of the hall, and for one terrible moment thought it was the demon. He ran. He burst through the Guildmaster's door with his heart hammering, and found Brannoc standing in the middle of the room, staring at his wall.

The wall of ADEQUATEs. Two hundred and thirteen gilt frames, floor to ceiling, each holding a single cream page with a single word, from the Duke of Varrenhold. Mike had seen them a hundred times: black ink, all capitals, the boar-on-a-boar seal.

They weren't black anymore.

Overnight, every single one had turned red. Not faded, not stained, but completely and uniformly red, a deep rich crimson that seemed to glisten slightly in the leaning candlelight, two hundred and thirteen times. *ADEQUATE. ADEQUATE. ADEQUATE.* From floor to ceiling, the wall looked like it was bleeding politely.

Brannoc turned around. His broad face was split by an enormous grin.

"*Look at that!*" he said. "Isn't it *marvelous*? Somebody's redone the whole wall!" He stepped back to admire it, hands on his hips. "Red! Bold! Much more *striking*. I'll bet it was Priscilla. She's got an eye for this sort of thing."

Mike looked at the wall. The wall, he could have sworn, looked back.

"Yes, sir," he said faintly. "Very striking."

---

The stand-up went ahead at eight o'clock, by the window, as usual. The dog and the sheep were absent. The candles leaned north. Nobody remarked on any of it. A stand-up, as Brannoc liked to say, was a stand-up.

There was one new addition that morning. Gareth had hired a bard.

He had decided that his Hero Moment needed music. So by the window, on a stool, sat a thin, nervous man with a large brown beard and a brand-new lute, plucking something soft and stirring while Gareth retold, for the eleventh day running, how he had held off two dragons at the mouth of the Sunken Crypt with nothing but his sword and his voice.

Mike glanced at the bard and frowned. There was something familiar about him. The beard. The nervous way he kept glancing up at the ceiling.

Then he remembered. Eight years ago, in the Basement, on his second day: Durgan, kicking reprimand scrolls back toward a drawer. *Brannoc wanted an enchanted chandelier. Lights itself, dims at night, plays a little tune when a hero comes in. Chandelier set fire to a bard.*

Mike looked at the bard. He looked up at the chandelier, a great wheel of black iron and crystal hanging directly above the stand-up circle. He looked back at the bard, who was sitting almost exactly underneath it.

"Excuse me," Mike whispered to the bard. "Have you played here before?"

"Once," whispered the bard, still plucking. "Years ago. It didn't go well." He glanced up at the chandelier again. "They said it was fixed."

Gareth's voice swelled into the second dragon.

"...and as the great beast reared above me, its jaws aflame—"

On the east wall, the new door's copied runes flared.

It happened all at once. The runes blazed up red-gold from the bottom of the frame to the top, as bright as a struck match. The great new oak door slammed shut by itself with a crash that shook the windows. The stone floor of the hall heaved, a single long, rolling lurch, as if something enormous underneath had turned over in its sleep. Sixty people staggered. The Duke of Varrenhold braced himself against the window frame with the dragon tooth.

And overhead, with a sound like a struck bell, the chandelier burst into flame.

It did not catch fire gradually. It went up all at once, every candle and crystal and iron curl of it, in a great roaring ring of orange flame. And then, as if it had been waiting eight years for this, it swung on its chain, hard and deliberately, and sent a long tongue of fire curling down toward the stool by the window.

Toward the bard.

The bard had time to look up. He had time to say nothing at all. Then his beard caught.

At the edge of the circle, at his chalk mark by the coat rack, Durgan Flintmantle raised one hand.

He didn't move from where he stood. He didn't hurry. He simply lifted his broad, square hand toward the burning chandelier and said a single word that sounded like a door closing. Then another that sounded like it opening again.

The flames went out.

All of them, at once: the chandelier, the beard, the end of the bard's new lute. They didn't flicker or die down. They were just gone, as if they had never been there. The chandelier hung, smoking gently, swinging slowly on its chain, its crystals ticking as they cooled. Every candle in the sconces still leaned north.

Dispelled. Cast again. It still worked, Mike thought, his heart pounding. On small things, it still worked.

The hall was absolutely silent.

The bard sat on his stool, under the chandelier, with a faint wisp of smoke rising from what was left of his beard. He looked down at his lute. He looked up at the chandelier. Then he said one word, quietly and with infinite weariness, to nobody in particular.

"*Again?*"

Nobody was hurt. Mike checked, twice. A few people had fallen over when the floor heaved, and the bard's beard would take some months to recover, but nobody was hurt. Durgan went back to standing at his chalk mark, arms folded, as if nothing had happened, and nobody in the circle looked at him. Mike realized, slowly, that most of them didn't understand what they had just seen. They had seen a fire, and then they had seen it stop. They had not seen *why*.

By the window, the Duke of Varrenhold, still braced against the frame, raised the dragon tooth.

"IS IT DONE YET?" he asked.

---

The inquiry convened that afternoon in the council room. It was called *The Inquiry into the Chandelier Incident*. It said so on a piece of paper pinned to the door, in Priscilla's handwriting, in what had been violet ink and was now red.

The panel sat behind a long table at the front: Guildmaster Brannoc in the middle, Priscilla on his left, Gareth the Bold on his right with his medal on. Durgan sat alone on a chair facing them, his hands on his knees. Pip sat at the side, clutching his slate. Mike sat at the back with a satchel full of paper on his lap.

Hesper Vane had not been invited. Mike had checked the list. Neither had the wizard from the capital, who had installed three bells and a wagon on the fire ward and copied the seal onto the new door. Mike had checked that too. He had sent a note, in fact, to the wizard's lodgings, and received a reply saying that the wizard had already gone back to the capital, with his payment, on the morning after the Grand Reopening, and was unavailable for questions.

Before the first witness was called, a junior clerk went around the room handing out copies of a single sheet of paper. *Findings of the Inquiry*, it said at the top. Mike took his copy and began to read, and then stopped, and then went back to the top and read the date.

It was dated yesterday.

The day of the Grand Reopening. The day *before* the chandelier caught fire.

He read the findings anyway. They were short. *The Inquiry finds that the Chandelier Incident was caused by fragile wards, and by a lack of resilience in the work of the Ward Maintenance Department.* Then a space for signatures, already signed.

Mike folded the page very carefully and put it in his satchel.

"Right," said Brannoc, rubbing his hands. "Let's get to the bottom of this. I've heard about a method." He stood, went to a slate on an easel, and picked up a piece of chalk. "It's called the *Five Whys*. They do it at Tanner's Row. Saw it through the window. You keep asking *why*, five times, until you find the root." He beamed. "The *root cause*."

He wrote on the slate, in large letters.

*1. Why did the chandelier catch fire?*

He turned to the panel. Priscilla raised her hand. "Fragile wards!"

"Fragile wards," agreed Brannoc, writing it down.

*2. Why were the wards fragile?*

"The Basement," said Gareth.

"The Basement." Brannoc wrote it down.

*3. Why the Basement?*

Brannoc stopped. He looked at the slate. He looked at the panel. He tapped the chalk against his chin and thought, very hard, for some time.

Then he wrote, in large, confident letters:

*Because.*

He stepped back and looked at it with deep satisfaction. "There. Root cause." He looked at the remaining space on the slate. "We can probably skip four and five. It's pretty clear."

Mike stood up.

"May I try?"

Brannoc squinted at him, then shrugged, and held out the chalk.

Mike went to the slate. He rubbed out Brannoc's answers. He left the questions. And under the first one, he wrote:

*1. Why did the chandelier catch fire? The fire ward was overloaded.*

"Overloaded?" said Priscilla.

"It was running at a hundred and twelve percent of capacity," said Mike. "The Basement measured it. It's on the board."

*2. Why was it overloaded? Three bells and a wagon were connected to it.*

There was a small stir on the panel. Priscilla looked at Brannoc. Brannoc looked at the ceiling.

Mike wrote the third question.

*3. Who ordered the bells and the wagon connected to the fire ward—*

"*Out of scope*," said Gareth the Bold.

Mike stopped, chalk in hand.

Gareth was sitting back in his chair, arms folded, looking extremely pleased with himself. He had heard the phrase somewhere, Mike guessed, and had liked it, and had been waiting for an opportunity to use it.

"That's out of scope," Gareth repeated, savoring it. "The inquiry is into the *chandelier*. Not the *bells*."

"The bells caused the—"

"Out," said Gareth, "of scope."

Brannoc took the chalk back. He rubbed out Mike's third line. Then he rubbed out the second, and the first. Then he wrote, in the clean space, exactly what he had written before: *Fragile wards. The Basement. Because.*

"There," he said. "Much tidier."

Then they called Durgan.

They asked him one question. Mike had expected more, and then realized that of course they would ask only one, because they already had the answer and needed only to hear it said.

Brannoc leaned forward. "Ward Maintenance. Were the wards fragile?"

Durgan sat on his chair with his hands on his knees and looked at the Guildmaster with his small, dark, steady eyes. When he answered, he did not raise his voice.

"They were carrying three bells," said Durgan, "a wagon, and a demon."

There was a long pause in the council room.

Priscilla had her quill poised over the minutes. She looked at Durgan. She looked at Brannoc. She looked down at her page.

"So," she said brightly, writing, "*yes*."

---

Mike tried. He had to try.

When they had finished with Durgan, he stood up at the back of the room and opened his satchel and began laying out the paper on the long table in front of the panel, one document at a time, in order.

"This," he said, "is a work order from eight years ago. Stamped by the Registrar. It shows the Basement was assigned the stables wards. That's the first time anybody ever assigned them anything in writing."

The panel looked at it.

"This is an incident record from earlier this year. It shows the Duke's bell was connected to the fire ward by an outside contractor, at the Guildmaster's request, and that Ward Maintenance was not consulted."

Gareth yawned.

"This is a list," said Mike, laying down three pages, "of every known problem with the Contraption, including the fact that it also draws on the fire ward." He laid down a fourth page. "And this is a formal objection to moving the front door, filed four days ago. It says that Ward Maintenance warned that moving the door would break the seal on the demon. It's signed by the Guildmaster, overruling it." He tapped the great, sweeping, curling signature at the bottom. "And witnessed by the guild's notary."

He stepped back.

The panel looked at the papers. They did not pick them up. Priscilla turned the first one over with one fingertip, glanced at it, and turned it back.

"Mikey," she said kindly. "These are just *documents*."

Brannoc nodded slowly, as if this were a profound point. He gathered the papers into a stack, tapped the edges straight, and handed them back to Mike across the table with a warm, fatherly smile.

"Let's take the documents offline," he said.

"Also," said Gareth, "out of scope."

Mike looked at the stack in his hands. At the top, upside down, was Brannoc's signature, so big it curled off the edge of the page.

He didn't argue.

He gathered the papers up, every one, and put them back in his satchel in order. Then he walked out of the council room and down the hall to the back room, knocked three times on the lectern, and addressed the Esteemed Registrar. He filed copies of every one of them: the work order, the incident record, the Known Issues, the objection with its two signatures. Then he took out his own copy of the Findings of the Inquiry, with its date, and filed that too.

The great brass stamp came down six times. **BOOM. BOOM. BOOM. BOOM. BOOM. BOOM.**

"FILED," said the Registrar, through its hum. Then, for the first time Mike could ever remember, it said a second word.

"NOTED."

---

The Performance Improvement Scroll arrived the next morning.

It was delivered to the Basement by a junior clerk who came halfway down the ladder, threw it, and fled. It landed on Durgan's desk with a soft thump, a thick roll of official paper tied with an official red ribbon and sealed with the guild seal.

Durgan opened it at his desk while Mike and Pip watched.

> *PERFORMANCE IMPROVEMENT SCROLL*
>
> *Durgan Flintmantle, Rune-Tender, Ward Maintenance Department*
>
> *Following the findings of the Inquiry into the Chandelier Incident, the following improvements are required within thirty days:*
>
> *1. Wards shall be thirty percent (30%) more resilient.*
>
> *2. Ward Maintenance shall respond to all incidents faster.*
>
> *3. Ward Maintenance shall attend all daily stand-ups in full, without exception.*
>
> *4. Ward Maintenance shall report, each week, the number of incidents prevented.*
>
> *Failure to meet these improvements may result in further action.*

Pip read it over Durgan's shoulder, and his face went through several colors.

"*Respond faster*," he said. "He was *at the stand-up*! That's the only reason he was there to put the fire out! If he'd been down here, at the board, where he's *supposed* to be—" He stopped, and stabbed a finger at item three. "And they want him at *every* stand-up! For ninety minutes! Every *morning*! While the board—" He stabbed at item two. "—while they want him to respond *faster*—"

He ran out of breath. He stabbed at item four.

"And *this*," said Pip, his voice cracking. "*Incidents prevented*. How do you *count* a fire that didn't happen?" He turned to Mike, eyes huge behind the spectacles. "Footnote: how do you count a *fire that didn't happen*? Footnote: that's *every fire*! That's the *whole job*! Nineteen years of fires that didn't happen! Where do I *write that down*?"

Mike looked at the scroll, and asked the skill, because he couldn't think of anything else to do.

**Estimated time to meet this improvement plan: requirements contradictory.**

He showed nobody. There was no need.

Durgan read the scroll a second time, slowly, all the way through. His face didn't change. When he had finished, he rolled it up again, carefully, and retied the red ribbon. Then he leaned over, pulled open his reprimand drawer, the one that had not shut in eight years, and tried to fit the scroll in on top of all the others.

The drawer gave a groan, a creak, and a long splintering crack.

Then it came clean off its runners.

It hit the floor with a crash and burst, and a great avalanche of rolled scrolls spilled out across the Basement flagstones: hundreds of them, nineteen years of them, all tied with the same official red ribbons, rolling in every direction, under the desks and around the ladder and against the foot of the glowing rune board, until the floor of the Basement looked like it had been carpeted in red.

There was a long silence.

Durgan looked at the scrolls. He looked at the empty drawer frame. Then he looked at Mike, and at Pip, and settled back in his chair, and picked up his pamphlet.

"Leave it," he said.

And they did.

---

He worked through the night.

Nobody asked him to. Nobody had assigned it. There was no work order and no request, and the guild had just spent a full day establishing, on paper, that the problem was his fault. But when the hall upstairs went dark and quiet, Durgan Flintmantle climbed the ladder with a lantern and a bag of chisels, went to the new door on the east wall, and knelt in front of the copied seal.

Mike went with him. So did Pip. They sat on the floor of the dark guild hall in a small circle of lantern light, with the candles in their sconces still leaning north, and watched him work.

He did it rune by rune. He didn't try to rebuild the whole seal; there was no time, and it couldn't be done here anyway, on the wrong stone. He went over the wizard's copy line by line, curve by curve, finding every place where the copy had gone slightly wrong. A stroke too shallow. A turn too sharp. A rune that looked right but meant something very slightly different, the way a word can be spelled correctly and still be the wrong word. And he fixed each one, gently, with the tip of a chisel and a low word, the way you might soothe a horse.

Around one in the morning, the trapdoor creaked behind them. Mike turned.

It was Velka. She was carrying a teapot and three cups, and she looked a little embarrassed to have been caught doing it.

"It's real tea," she said, a little defensively. "Not the fire-ward kind."

She poured four cups. Durgan accepted his without looking away from the seal, and nodded once. Pip took his with both hands and held it against his chest. Velka handed the last one to Mike, and then, without asking, sat down beside him on the floor in the lantern light.

She didn't say anything about the inquiry. She didn't say anything about anything. She just sat there next to him, shoulder almost touching his, and drank her tea, and watched Durgan work.

Mike found that it helped more than anything anyone had said all day.

It was nearly dawn when Durgan finished. He sat back on his heels and looked at the seal. The copied runes glowed softly in the dark, still pulsing in that long, slow rhythm, like breathing. But the rhythm seemed a little slower now. A little deeper. Like a sleeper settling.

Then Durgan leaned forward one last time with the very tip of his smallest chisel, and beside the wizard's faithfully copied *TEMPORARY. REPLACE BEFORE WINTER.*, he carved a short note of his own, in small, square, dwarvish letters.

*TEMPORARY. SORRY.*

Pip looked at it for a long time. He opened his mouth, the way he did before every footnote.

Then he closed it again, and said nothing.

Mike asked the skill. He wasn't sure what he expected.

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 49 days.**

Three days. Durgan had bought three days back, on his knees, overnight, unpaid, blamed, with a chisel. Mike started to say something, and found he couldn't, and looked at Durgan instead. The dwarf was putting his chisels away in their bag, one by one, very neatly.

---

They were still sitting there, the four of them, on the floor by the new door, when the knock came.

It was the dead hour before dawn: later than midnight, earlier than morning. The hour when nobody should be awake, and nobody should be knocking.

Three knocks. Slow. Courteous. On the new oak door, right above their heads.

Nobody moved.

"The Duke?" whispered Velka.

"The Duke rings," said Mike.

He got up. He wasn't sure why. He found later that he couldn't remember deciding to do it, only that his hand was suddenly on the iron latch of the new door, which was warm under his palm, and then he was pulling it open.

There was a man standing on the step outside.

He was very tall and very thin, in a long, neat, charcoal-gray coat buttoned to the throat. His face was pale and pleasant and entirely unremarkable, the face of a man who might work in a counting house, or a bank, or anywhere that kept very careful track of very small numbers. Under one arm he held a ledger, thick and heavy, bound in something dark that was not quite leather. In his other hand he held a small glass inkpot, stoppered with silver. The ink inside it was red. It was the exact deep red of the ADEQUATE letters on Brannoc's wall, and the signature in the Registrar's oldest ledger, and the crypt contract that Mike had signed with his thumb when he was twelve years old.

The ink, Mike could see even from here, was faintly steaming in the cool night air.

The man smiled at him politely. It was a very nice smile. It did not reach anywhere at all.

"Good evening," he said. "Or morning. I'm never sure, at this hour." He inclined his head. "Malphus. Of Accounts."

He glanced past Mike, into the dark hall, at the candles all leaning north, at the copied runes glowing on the door frame. His eyes lingered for a moment, with something like professional appreciation, on the two small notes carved side by side.

Then he looked back at Mike.

"I'm here," said Malphus, "about the interest."
