# Chapter 7: Day Fifteen

The Sunken Crypt of Varrenhold, when the river finally let go of it, looked less like a tomb and more like a drain that had given up.

It sat in a hollow of the dry riverbed a mile below the town bridge: a squat stone doorway half-buried in cracked gray mud, choked with dead reeds, slick with green slime, and smelling powerfully of every fish that had ever lived. A pair of very old boots stood beside the entrance, neatly side by side, as if their owner had taken them off politely before going in.

"Party Three," said Mike, consulting his notes. "*Wet. Lost boots.*"

"Found them," said Velka.

Hesper Vane was already there when they arrived, sitting on a folding stool with her satchel at her feet and a ledger open on her knee. She had brought a parasol against the sun, and her expression suggested she had brought it against everything else as well.

"I'm to witness commencement," she said. "*The first day on which the party of the guild shall set foot within the said crypt.*" She dipped her quill. "Whenever you're ready. I have nowhere else to be."

They were not ready. Sir Roderick had stopped ten feet from the doorway, armor gleaming, arms folded, and was regarding the crypt with the expression of a man who has been asked to lick it.

"Two hundred years old," he said.

"Roderick—"

"Rebuilt by a *river*. Visited by six parties, none of whom wrote anything down except *wet*." He shook his head. "I'm sorry, clerk. My oath stands. I don't go into legacy."

Mike had expected this. He had spent a week thinking about it, between bells. He reached into his satchel and brought out a fat, blank, leather-bound book, a box of charcoal, and a measuring chain.

"Then here's a deal," he said. "I go in first. Every room I enter, I map. Every trap I find, I write down: where it is, what it's attached to, what it does. You only go into rooms I've already documented."

Roderick stared at him. "*You'd* go in first? You're a clerk."

"I've been documenting a legacy system for eight years," said Mike. "It has a golem. I'll manage."

The paladin looked at the book in Mike's hands for a long moment. Something moved in his long, serious face: suspicion, then surprise, then something that might have been hope.

"Every room?" he said.

"Every room."

Roderick unfolded his arms. "Then I'll follow the map."

That left one more problem. Velka was already rolling up her sleeves, crackling faintly at the fingertips, with the bright, dangerous look of someone who had brought several new spells and intended to use all of them.

"One rule," said Mike. "Any new spell gets tested in the antechamber first. Before you use it anywhere near the rest of us."

"Testing is for people with—"

"Nothing to prove. I know. Antechamber."

She glared at him. He held her gaze. After a moment, she rolled her eyes elaborately and said, "*Fine*."

The fifth member of the party was Bertram, the porter, a gray, leathery man of no particular age who carried three packs at once without complaint. He had, according to the guild's records, carried things into six dungeons. Mike had asked him about them on the walk down. Bertram had said, "No," and that had been the end of it.

Sister Agatha went in first, as cleric and lantern-bearer. Her sandal touched the wet stone of the threshold.

On her stool, without looking up, Hesper Vane wrote in her ledger: *Commencement. Day 1.*

**Estimated time to clear the Sunken Crypt: 19 days.**

Mike stepped through the doorway after her, opened his book to the first page, and began to draw.

---

On the morning of day two, a pigeon arrived.

It landed on a rock at the crypt mouth, fluffed its feathers, and held out one leg with a tiny scroll attached. The scroll bore the boar-on-a-boar seal. Inside, in capitals, it said:

**IS IT DONE YET?**

Mike wrote on his slate *Day 2 of 19. On schedule*, held it up to the pigeon, and released it. The pigeon looked at the slate for a long moment, as if memorizing it, and flew away.

That afternoon, a second pigeon arrived. This one was from Brannoc. The note said:

**WHY NOT?**

Mike held up the same slate.

On day three, a third pigeon appeared, a small white one with a pink ribbon around its neck. The note was in violet ink and smelled of perfume.

*Mikey!! Told His Grace the crypt will be done by TUESDAY!! He was SO pleased!! XO, P. (Head of Client Delight)*

Mike read it twice. "Which Tuesday?" he asked the pigeon.

The pigeon did not know. Nobody ever found out. Priscilla, when asked later, would say "*a* Tuesday" with total confidence and change the subject.

After that, the pigeons came every day: the Duke's in the morning, Brannoc's in the afternoon, and Priscilla's whenever she had promised someone something new. Mike started leaving the slate propped against a rock at the entrance, permanently updated. The pigeons learned to read it on their own.

Meanwhile, the work went on.

The crypt was exactly as bad as Roderick had feared. Room by room, Mike found the evidence of two centuries of people doing things to it and writing nothing down. There were doors that had been bricked up and then broken through again by someone else. There was a staircase that went down, then up, then down, for no reason anyone could reconstruct. There was a vault half full of river silt, where they found the skeleton of a mule still wearing a saddlebag labeled, in faded paint, *PARTY 4*.

And there were traps. On day six, in a corridor lined with stone faces, Mike found a pressure plate. Studying it, he found that it was tied by a thin copper wire to a second plate in the next room, which was tied to a third plate by the door, which was tied, as far as he could tell, to the ceiling.

He drew all three on the map, with the wire, and an arrow, and a note: *DO NOT DISARM ONE WITHOUT THE OTHERS.*

Sir Roderick studied the page for a long time. Then he looked at Mike with an expression of deep, grim, personal vindication.

"*Told* you," he said.

And then he went in. Carefully, exactly along the route Mike had drawn, setting his armored feet only where the map said it was safe. Room by documented room, the paladin who never went into legacy dungeons went deeper into the oldest dungeon he had ever seen. Every evening, he asked to see the new pages, and read them the way some men read scripture.

On day eight, they reached the Great Vault.

It was a long, high chamber of black stone, still wet from the river, with alcoves along the walls holding the tombs of minor Varrenholds. At its far end, behind a rusted gate, was the inner sanctum where the Third Duke lay with his signet ring. Between them and the gate, something large had been dead in the silt for a very long time and had not, apparently, finished.

"Right," said Velka, flexing her fingers. "I've got just the thing. Fireball 4.0. It's in beta, but it's going to be *amazing*—"

"Antechamber," said Mike.

"Oh, come *on*. We're right here."

"Antechamber."

She glared at him. He looked back. Agatha studied the ceiling. Roderick looked at his map.

With tremendous dignity, Velka stalked back down the corridor to the small, empty, stone antechamber at the entrance to the vault. She turned to face its far wall. She drew herself up, said a word, and flung out both hands.

Fireball 4.0 left her palms in a roaring orange sphere, hit the far wall, bounced off it, hit the side wall, bounced off that, hit the ceiling, came screaming straight back down the middle of the room, and passed so close over Velka's head that her hat burst into flames.

Then it hit the floor and went out.

There was a long silence in the antechamber, broken only by the small crackling sound of a hat on fire.

Velka took off the hat, dropped it on the floor, and stamped on it. She looked at the scorch marks on all four walls. Then she looked, slowly, through the door, down the corridor, into the Great Vault, where her friends were standing in a stone room exactly like this one, only longer and with fewer exits.

Mike, at the door, said nothing at all.

Velka walked past him without a word and went back into the vault. She took care of the large, dead, unfinished thing with Lightning 2.2, which she had already tested in her tower, twice.

After that, she tested everything in the antechamber. She didn't mention it. She didn't need to. Every time she walked back from the antechamber, she glared at Mike, and every time, he nodded as though he hadn't noticed, and Agatha hummed a hymn to herself and smiled at her knitting.

Every night, the skill told him the same thing.

**Estimated time to clear the Sunken Crypt: 19 days.**

On schedule. Exactly on schedule. Just not the schedule anyone had promised.

---

On day fourteen, Brannoc and Priscilla came down to the crypt mouth with a picnic.

They brought a hamper, a striped blanket, two bottles of wine, a roast chicken, and a long canvas banner on two poles. When Mike climbed out of the crypt into the afternoon sun, covered in silt and charcoal, they unrolled it with a flourish.

*CONGRATULATIONS!*

"Well?" said Brannoc, beaming. "Done?"

Mike looked at the banner. He looked at the chicken. He looked at his book, which held, as of that morning, fifty-one pages of documented rooms, and a sanctum gate they would reach in four days.

"It's day fourteen of nineteen," he said. "We're exactly on schedule."

The Guildmaster's face clouded over. "The *schedule*," he said, "says fourteen."

"That's the *deadline*, sir. The schedule says nineteen."

"There's a *difference*?"

Mike opened his mouth. He closed it again. There was, he reflected, no answer to that question that would fit on a banner.

Behind Brannoc, slowly and very quietly, Priscilla rolled up *CONGRATULATIONS* and tucked it under her arm.

---

The tribunal convened on the morning of day fifteen.

It took place at the crypt mouth, around a folding table that Bertram had carried down from town without being asked and refused to explain. Hesper Vane presided, on her stool, with her ledgers stacked beside her and her parasol open against the sun. Brannoc sat on one side of the table. Mike stood on the other, in his silty clothes, holding his book.

A small crowd had gathered on the riverbank above to watch. A tribunal was not something Brindlemoor saw every day, and it was a nice morning for it.

"The guild," said Brannoc, rising, "invokes the penalty clause." He unrolled his copy of the blood contract. "*Should the work exceed the term of fourteen days, the Estimator shall answer for the delay.*" He turned and pointed at Mike. "The Estimator!"

"Present," said Mike.

"And I would like," said Brannoc, "to present the guild's evidence."

He reached under the table and brought out a slate. A plain, ordinary, school-sized slate, in a wooden frame, that Mike had last seen eight years ago, on the wall of Brannoc's office. On it, in Brannoc's huge, slanting chalk, faded but still legible, it said:

**14 DAYS.**

And underneath, in violet chalk, with a little heart over the *i*:

*Witnessed — Priscilla*

Brannoc held it up for the crowd. "Fourteen days! Agreed! Witnessed! And here we are on day fifteen, *unfinished*."

Mike looked at the slate.

And for a moment, he wasn't standing on a riverbank in Brindlemoor. He was in a conference room in Raleigh, with fluorescent lights and stale coffee, looking at a photograph of a whiteboard on a laptop screen: *2 weeks*, in Chad's handwriting, sent to a client under the subject line **COMMITTED!**. Across the table, a woman from HR was talking about accountability gaps.

He had been tried with this exhibit before. Different world, different chalk. Same exhibit.

"May I?" he said, and Hesper nodded.

Mike opened his satchel and took out a single sheet of paper. It was old and soft from handling, folded in thirds. At the top, in his twelve-year-old handwriting, it said: *Estimate: Sunken Crypt. 19 days.* Under that were the assumptions, the risks, and Pip's six footnotes. In the corner was the great round stamp of the Registrar, eight years old: **FILED.**

"This is the estimate," Mike said. "Nineteen days. It was filed with the Registrar the day the contract was signed. The fourteen came later." He laid it on the table beside the slate. "I didn't write the fourteen. The Guildmaster did."

The crowd on the bank murmured. Hesper Vane picked up the estimate, read it carefully from top to bottom, including the footnotes, then set it down, squared to the edge of the table.

Brannoc waved a hand. "Then you should have estimated *better*."

There was a pause.

"Better than correct?" said Mike.

Brannoc considered this, frowning, as if it were an interesting philosophical point that he did not intend to concede.

Hesper Vane cleared her throat. It was a very small sound, but the riverbank went quiet.

"The tribunal has heard the evidence," she said, in a voice like dry paper. "The contract states that the Estimator shall answer for the delay. A delay, by any reasonable reading, is the difference between when the work was due and when it is done." She turned a page in her ledger. "The work is not done. Therefore, the delay cannot yet be measured. Therefore, there is nothing yet to answer for." She dipped her quill. "Verdict deferred until completion."

Brannoc stared at her. "*Deferred?*"

"It's in the contract," said Hesper. "I wrote it."

The Guildmaster's face went through several expressions. Then he slapped the table with his huge hand and stood up.

"*Fine*," he said. "Then we'll make sure it's done *fast*."

**Estimated time to clear the Sunken Crypt: 19 days.**

Mike, for the first time in eight years, felt the skill hesitate. As if it, too, had seen what was coming.

---

The reinforcements arrived on the afternoon of day fifteen.

There were twelve of them. They came marching down the riverbank in a long, clattering column, swords, shields, spears, and one man inexplicably carrying a harp. At their head, in a magnificent new crimson cloak even larger than the one he had lost to the troll, strode Gareth the Bold.

"Fear not!" Gareth bellowed to the crowd still lingering on the bank. "Help has arrived!"

The crowd cheered. They did not know what they were cheering about either.

"The Guildmaster sends twelve of our finest," Gareth told Mike, clapping him on the shoulder hard enough to leave a bruise. "Twice the party! Half the time! Brief them, clerk, and let's get this done."

Mike looked at the twelve. The twelve looked at the crypt. The crypt, which had been dark and quiet and two hundred years old for two hundred years, did not appear to have an opinion.

**Estimated time to clear the Sunken Crypt: 23 days.**

He briefed them.

It took the rest of the day, and he was fairly sure, by the end, that it had taken years off his life.

It was his first day at the guild all over again, only backward. Twelve new people, none of whom had ever been in the crypt, none of whom had read the map, all of whom had questions. Where were the traps? Which ones were tied together? Which way was the vault? Why couldn't they just go *that* way? Was there a privy? Where was the privy? Why wasn't there a privy?

And every time one of them asked, another one answered.

"Mike knows."

"Ask Mike."

"Mike's got a book."

Every question took a few minutes to answer. Every answer started a new argument. With every one, the glowing numbers crept upward in front of his eyes.

**Estimated time: 24 days.**

**Estimated time: 25 days.**

Somewhere around the privy question, Mike found himself doing the math in his head, the way he had once done it on whiteboards for people who never looked at it.

Five people, he thought, had ten ways for any two of them to disagree. He had spent fifteen days learning all ten. Agatha disagreed with nobody. Roderick disagreed with legacy architecture. Velka disagreed with Mike, in a way that was becoming steadily less irritating. Bertram disagreed silently and carried things.

Seventeen people had a hundred and thirty-six.

And as far as he could tell, the reinforcements were working through all of them in the first afternoon.

**Estimated time: 26 days.**

They went in on the morning of day sixteen.

It was chaos within the hour. The twelve crowded the narrow corridors so tightly that nobody could swing a sword without hitting a colleague. They ate the supplies Bertram had carefully rationed for nineteen days in four. They argued about formation at every junction, then argued about the argument. One of them borrowed Mike's map to look at the traps and used it as a fan, and then put it down in a puddle. The man with the harp played it.

Sir Roderick followed Mike's route exactly, step by documented step, and watched twelve people walk all over the parts of the floor the map said not to.

"Clerk," he said quietly, his face gray. "Those plates in the corridor—"

"I know."

"They're stepping on—"

"I *know*."

The only reason nobody died in the corridor of stone faces, Mike decided later, was that the twelve were packed in so tightly that none of them could actually move their feet.

It was Gareth who found the lever.

He had gone back toward the entrance, bored, looking for a shortcut. He found one in an alcove beside the entrance tunnel: a great iron lever set in the wall, rusted and old. Above it, on a small brass plate, was engraved a single word.

*SAFE.*

"Ha!" said Gareth the Bold, and pulled it.

Mike heard the click from three rooms away. He turned in time to see Sir Roderick go absolutely white, the color draining from his long face as if someone had pulled a plug. The paladin's lips moved. Mike couldn't hear what he said. Later, he would learn that it was a name: the name of a squire, in the Barrows of Elm, who had pulled a lever that said the same thing.

Then the ceiling of the entrance tunnel came down.

It came down with a roar like the river coming back: tons of old stone and older mud, thundering into the passage in a single long avalanche, filling the tunnel from floor to roof. Dust boiled through the corridors. Lanterns went out. Somebody screamed. The harp made a noise that harps should not make.

Then silence.

---

In the dark, something rustled.

Then a voice, dry and leathery and entirely unbothered, said: "Seven."

It was Bertram. In the faint glow of Agatha's re-lit lantern, Mike could see him dusting off his three packs, one at a time, with the air of a man who had now carried things into seven dungeons and would, likewise, not be discussing this one.

"Everyone all right?" Mike croaked.

Agatha: "Fine, dear." Roderick: "...Yes." Bertram, a grunt.

Velka said nothing. Then, in the dark, Mike heard her mutter a single, quiet word, and a soft, steady, perfectly ordinary white light bloomed above her palm. It wasn't flashy or purple. It didn't flicker, bounce, or set anything on fire. It just lit the room.

Mike looked at it. He knew that spell. Every first-year apprentice in Brindlemoor knew that spell.

"Is that Illumination *1.0*?"

Velka didn't look at him. "Don't tell anyone," she said.

By its light, they took stock. The entrance tunnel was gone, packed solid with rubble. The five of them, the original party, were on the inside, sealed in, covered in dust, but whole. And the twelve reinforcements?

Mike listened. Through the rubble, faintly, from somewhere outside, came a familiar voice. It was raised in the warm, ringing tones of a man addressing a crowd.

"...and as I stood at the threshold of that ancient tomb, I thought to myself: *Gareth, this is your moment*—"

Gareth the Bold had gone outside to give a speech. And, it turned out, all twelve of the reinforcements had followed him out to listen.

They were all on the outside. All of them.

Through the rubble, the speech stopped. There was a confused murmur. Then Gareth's voice came back, louder, swelling into heroic urgency, without missing a beat.

"...and THAT is why I, Gareth, will personally lead the RESCUE!"

The crowd on the riverbank went wild.

Mike, in the dark, closed his eyes.

**Estimated time until Gareth takes credit: already happened.**

Then, while he was still looking at it, the skill quietly recalculated everything. It counted the rubble and the corridors and the sanctum gate, and the five people left, and the hundred and thirty-six ways to disagree that had just gone back down to ten.

**Estimated time to clear the Sunken Crypt: 19 days.**

Mike stared at it.

The same day. The exact same day as always. The only way, in the end, to get the job back on schedule had been to lose the reinforcements.

He started to laugh. He couldn't help it. He sat down on a rock in a sealed tomb under a river and laughed until there were tears cutting clean lines through the dust on his face, while Agatha patted his back, and Roderick looked worried, and Velka watched him with her small, steady, unspectacular light.

There was a fluttering in the rubble overhead.

They all looked up. Through a crack in the fallen stone no wider than a fist, something gray and determined was squeezing its way into the crypt. It popped free, fluffed its feathers, landed on Mike's knee, and held out one leg.

The scroll bore the boar-on-a-boar seal.

**IS IT DONE YET?**
