## Chapter 6: The Night Bell

Mike spent the first of his six hours trying to find an escort.

He went through the duty roster twice. The Thornwood party was in the Thornwood. The wolf party was chasing wolves, or possibly being chased by them; their last report had been unclear. Three adventurers were on Priscilla's list for a christening, a ribbon-cutting, and something described only as "a surprise." Gareth the Bold had taken four of the strongest fighters to look at his troll, which he now described as "probably medium." The rest were either injured, on leave, or purple.

"It wears off," Velka said defensively, as two of the purple ones went past. "Probably. In a few days."

"How many days?"

"It's a new spell. Nobody knows. That's what makes it *exciting*."

By the end of the second hour, Mike had a list of available escorts that contained exactly four names: Sister Agatha, Sir Roderick, Velka, and Mikel Dobbins, senior clerk, who had never escorted anything more dangerous than a stack of contracts.

Then the Duke's instructions arrived.

They came by footman, on heavy cream paper, folded three times and sealed with a blob of red wax the size of a biscuit, stamped with the Varrenhold crest: a boar standing on what appeared to be a smaller, less important boar. Mike broke the seal and unfolded the letter.

It was written entirely in capital letters.

> HIS GRACE THE DUKE OF VARRENHOLD REQUIRES AN ESCORT OF NOT FEWER THAN FOUR (4) PERSONS OF THE GUILD, ARMED, TO PRESENT THEMSELVES AT VARRENHOLD HOUSE AT THE STROKE OF MIDNIGHT. HIS GRACE WILL PROCEED ON FOOT. THE MATTER IS OF THE HIGHEST URGENCY. DISCRETION IS EXPECTED. LATENESS WILL NOT BE TOLERATED. THIS IS VERY IMPORTANT.

Mike read it twice. Every word was shouting. Even the commas looked angry.

He knew this letter. Not this exact letter, but its ancestor. In another life, in an office in Raleigh, a man named Doug at Hartwell Logistics had sent emails in exactly this voice: every word in capitals, every request the most important one ever, every message ending with some version of *THIS IS VERY IMPORTANT*, which usually turned out to mean that Doug's mouse was unplugged.

He turned the letter over, looking for where they would be going and why. The back was blank.

"Well?" said Sir Roderick, who had polished his breastplate to a mirror shine. "What's the danger? Brigands? A cursed relic? A rival house?"

"It doesn't say."

"Ah." Roderick nodded grimly. "The worst kind."

---

Varrenhold House stood at the top of the hill above the square, a great, rambling, ivy-choked mansion of gray stone, with too many chimneys and a weathervane in the shape of the boar on the smaller boar. They arrived at a quarter to midnight. Mike had insisted on being early. Everyone else had insisted on complaining about it.

At the front steps, Sir Roderick stopped dead.

"How old is this house?" he asked.

Mike, who had looked it up in the Registrar's ledger on the way out, out of pure habit, said: "About three hundred years."

Roderick took one deliberate step backward, onto the gravel.

"Legacy," he said. "I'll guard the perimeter."

"It's a *house*, Roderick."

"It's a three-hundred-year-old house. Do you know how many generations of Varrenholds have added secret passages to it? Do you know who documented them?" He folded his arms. "Perimeter."

Mike found that he did not, in fact, have an answer.

At the stroke of midnight, the great front doors opened and the Duke of Varrenhold appeared.

He was a tall man of about sixty, with a long, proud face, a magnificent white mustache, and the expression of someone who had been mildly disappointed by everything he had ever seen. He was wearing a velvet dressing gown in deep burgundy, embroidered with boars, and a pair of matching slippers. Behind him, a pace back and to the left, stood the steward, holding a lantern and looking as if he had attended this exact moment many times before.

The Duke looked down at them from the top step. His gaze passed over Agatha, over Velka, over Mike, and lingered with some suspicion on Sir Roderick, who was guarding the gravel.

"You are the escort," said the Duke.

He did not speak in capital letters, exactly. But somehow you could hear them anyway.

"Yes, Your Grace," said Mike. "Where are we escorting you?"

The Duke drew himself up. Then he descended the steps, slowly and grandly, slippers whispering on stone, and set off down the hill toward the square.

They followed him. Two hundred yards later, he stopped in front of a small shop with warm light glowing in its windows and a smell of sugar and yeast drifting out of its open door.

Mistress Pell's bakery.

Mistress Pell herself appeared in the doorway, older and floury and entirely unsurprised, holding out a paper bag. "Evening, Your Grace. One cinnamon bun, extra glaze."

The Duke accepted the bag. He inspected the bun. He took a bite.

Mike stood very still in the cool night air, in front of the bakery, at midnight, with three armed adventurers and a skill that was now displaying, with what felt like deliberate restraint, **Estimated danger: 0.**

"This," Mike said slowly, "was the urgent escort."

"The bakery opens at midnight to prepare for morning," said the steward quietly, beside him. "His Grace prefers his buns fresh. And his streets safe."

"It's two hundred yards."

"His Grace," said the steward, "prefers them *very* safe."

On the way back up the hill, the streetlamps had gone out, and Velka, eager to be useful, stepped forward. "Allow me. I have a new one. Illumination 5.1. I fixed the bug."

"Velka," said Mike, "maybe test it on something that isn't—"

She said a word and flung out a hand.

The Duke of Varrenhold turned purple.

Not the dressing gown. Not the slippers. The Duke: his face, his hands, his magnificent white mustache. He stood in the dark street, glowing faintly, a deep and regal plum.

There was a very long silence.

"Huh," said Velka. "Worked in my tower."

The Duke looked down at his purple hands. He looked at the bun, which was also purple. He took another bite anyway, chewed thoughtfully, and swallowed.

Then he turned and climbed the steps of Varrenhold House. At the top, he paused, looked back down at them, and delivered his verdict.

"ADEQUATE," said the Duke, and went inside.

---

Brannoc was delighted.

"*Adequate!*" he boomed the next morning, waving the Duke's follow-up letter, which said nothing else. It had arrived at dawn, on cream paper, in capitals, under the boar-on-a-boar seal. "Do you know how long it's been since His Grace called us adequate?"

"How long?" asked Mike, despite himself.

"Since last time!" Brannoc beamed. "We've got to keep him happy. Biggest client we've got. And I've had an idea."

The idea arrived that afternoon, in the person of a wizard from the capital: a thin, fast-talking man in a silver coat, carrying a brass bell the size of a melon and a long coil of shimmering, silvery cord.

"A priority bell!" Brannoc announced to the hall. "One end in His Grace's bedchamber, a nice tasseled pull by the bed. The other end right here, behind the front counter. Any time His Grace needs us, day or night, he gives it a tug, and *ding*. We come running!"

The hall cheered. Gareth cheered loudest, before asking what they were cheering about.

"How long will it take to install?" Mike asked the wizard.

"Oh, it's a quick job," said the wizard breezily, unspooling cord. "Hour or two. Bell like this needs a bit of power, obviously, but you've got wards in this building, haven't you? I'll just tap into one. Nobody'll notice."

Mike felt something cold settle in his stomach. "Which ward?"

The wizard waved a hand vaguely at the floor. "Whichever's got the most spare capacity."

Two floors below, the most spare capacity belonged to the fire ward. The one built for a dragon attack. The one Durgan used to boil his tea.

Mike found out exactly when the wizard tapped into it, because the whole hall heard the groan from below. It was a deep, grinding moan that came up through the floorboards like the building had stubbed its toe. The chandelier swayed. Somewhere downstairs, a teacup shattered.

By the time Mike got down the ladder, Durgan was on his feet beside the ward board, staring at the amber cluster labeled *FIRE (MAIN)*. A new silvery thread now ran out of it, up through the ceiling, toward the counter.

"Who," said Durgan, very quietly, "did *that*?"

"Brannoc hired a wizard," said Mike. "For a bell. So the Duke can ring for us."

Pip, on his ladder, put his face in his hands. "Footnote," he said, through his fingers. "*Again*."

Mike went back up the ladder. He knocked three times on the lectern and addressed the Esteemed Registrar, and in black ink, in his neatest hand, he filed an incident record:

> *Incident: Priority bell installed, drawing power from the fire ward (main).*
>
> *Installed by: Outside contractor.*
>
> *Requested by: Guildmaster Brannoc.*
>
> *Ward Maintenance consulted: No.*

The stamp came down. **BOOM.**

He brought a copy down to the Basement. Durgan read it twice, slowly, his lips moving slightly on the last line.

Then he looked up. "Can I have a copy of that?"

"That one's yours."

Durgan folded it carefully and put it in his breast pocket. He patted it once. It was the gentlest Mike had ever seen him treat a piece of paper.

---

The bell rang for the first time that night at a quarter past one.

Mike was asleep at his desk. He had stayed late to file the day's contracts, laid his head down on the lumber guild's paperwork for one minute, and woken up two hours later with the sound of the bell going off directly behind his left ear.

*DING. DING. DING.*

It was not a gentle bell. The wizard from the capital had clearly been told that it was important, and had tuned it accordingly. It sounded like a church tower falling down a flight of stairs.

Mike ran. Agatha, Velka, and Roderick, who had been asleep on benches in the hall because Mike had a bad feeling, ran with him. They pounded up the hill in the dark to Varrenhold House, where a window on the second floor was open and the Duke, still faintly purple, was leaning out of it in his dressing gown.

"WHAT TIME IS IT?" the Duke asked.

Mike, gasping, looked at the stars. "About half past one, Your Grace."

"ADEQUATE," said the Duke, and closed the window.

The next night, the bell rang at two. The Duke wanted the moon looked at, to confirm it was still there. Velka confirmed it. The Duke said "ADEQUATE" and closed the window.

The night after that, the bell rang at midnight and the Duke wanted a lullaby. Sister Agatha, who had a surprisingly lovely voice, sang him one in the courtyard while Roderick guarded the perimeter. At the end, the Duke coughed, and complained of a sore throat, and Agatha, before anyone could stop her, laid a hand on him.

There was a soft chime. The Duke blinked down at them from his window.

"WHY," he said, "IS THERE A CLERIC IN MY COURTYARD?"

They went home.

On the fourth night, the Duke stopped asking for things.

The bell rang at a quarter to three. They ran up the hill. The window opened. And the Duke leaned out, and looked down at Mike, and said:

"IS IT DONE YET?"

Mike, still catching his breath, stared up at him. "Is what done, Your Grace?"

"THE CRYPT."

"The crypt is still under the river, Your Grace. It won't drain for another five days."

The Duke considered this, frowning, as if the river had been insubordinate.

"STATUS TOMORROW," he said. "SAME TIME."

The window closed.

And so, every night after that, at a quarter to three, they ran up the hill to tell the Duke that the crypt was still under the river. Every night, the Duke received the news with the air of a man who suspected someone was being lazy, said "STATUS TOMORROW," and shut the window. Mike started writing the status on a slate in advance, to save time: *Crypt: still underwater. Estimate: unchanged.* Then he started holding the slate up without speaking. The Duke never noticed the difference.

The bell rang while Mike was in the bath. It rang while he was at his parents' for dinner, which was technically impossible, since the bell was at the guild. But he heard it anyway, in his head, and ran halfway down the street before he realized. By the fourth night, he had stopped taking his boots off. By the sixth, he slept in his coat.

Then, on the seventh night, at three in the morning, in a cold, driving rain, the bell rang again.

They ran up the hill. Mike was soaked to the skin before they reached the square. Rain sheeted off Roderick's armor. Agatha's robes clung to her like wet paper. Velka had attempted a waterproofing spell, and was now very dry and very slightly on fire.

The window opened. The Duke leaned out into the rain.

"JUST CHECKING IT WORKS," he called.

The window closed.

Mike stood in the downpour at the foot of Varrenhold House, rain running off his nose, and started, very quietly, to laugh.

"What's funny?" asked Velka, patting out a small flame on her sleeve.

"That bell," said Mike, wiping his eyes, "is the only thing in this entire guild that anyone has ever *tested*."

---

It was the skill that saved him, in the end.

On the eighth day, half-dead with exhaustion, Mike sat at his desk staring at the bell, and thought, the way he had thought about the crypt eight years ago, as clearly as he could: *When will it ring?*

**Estimated time until next bell: 2 hours, 51 minutes.**

He stared at the line. Then, carefully, he put his head down on his arms.

He woke up two hours and fifty minutes later, rested, alert, and with his boots already on. Thirty seconds after that, the bell rang.

From then on, Mike slept in exact, measured portions, timed to the minute, between rings. Twenty-five minutes here. An hour and twelve there. He began to feel almost human. He also began, he suspected, to look extremely strange, because he would be fast asleep on a bench one moment and sitting bolt upright the next, reaching for his coat, a full half-minute before anything happened.

Velka noticed on the ninth night.

They were sitting on a stone bench in the courtyard of Varrenhold House at three in the morning, waiting for the Duke to finish being told that the crypt was still underwater. The others had drifted off. Mike had his eyes closed. Velka was watching him.

He opened his eyes and stood up. Thirty seconds later, somewhere in the dark house above them, a window began to open.

"How," said Velka slowly, "do you *do* that?"

Mike looked down at her. Her short dark hair was rumpled from the bench, there was a faint scorch mark on her sleeve from the waterproofing spell, and she was looking at him with the same puzzled, narrow-eyed curiosity she'd turned on his guide.

"Practice," he said.

She didn't believe him. He could see that. But she didn't push either, and he found himself oddly grateful.

---

While they were walking the Duke to buns and telling him about rivers, everything else fell apart.

On Thursday, the Hollins wedding went ahead without any magic at all. There was a lute player instead, who did not know any songs that ended. Mrs. Hollins sent a letter to the guild that was not in capital letters, but was somehow worse.

The Westmarch griffin, unremoved, moved into the Westmarch barn, and then into the Westmarch farmhouse, and the family moved in with relatives in town. They sent no letter at all. They just took their business across the river, to the new guild on Tanner's Row.

Gareth the Bold fought his troll with four fighters instead of the full party. The troll turned out to be "large, actually," and Gareth came back with a black eye, a sprained wrist, and no cloak. Nobody ever learned what happened to the cloak. Gareth refused to discuss it.

One by one, the guild's regular clients began to drift across the river. The new guild on Tanner's Row, people said, did things differently. They answered letters. They came when they said they would.

Priscilla, faced with this, called a meeting and announced her solution.

"A *second* bell!" she said brightly. "For His Grace! So he never has to wait!"

It was installed the next day by the same wizard, from the same ward. Down below, the ward board groaned again, and Pip had to sit down.

The second bell was exactly as loud as the first. It rang from a second cord in a different room of Varrenhold House, the Duke's study, so that he could summon the guild without the inconvenience of going to bed. Unfortunately, the two bells were identical, and hung side by side, and nobody behind the counter could tell which one had rung, so nobody knew which window to run to.

Priscilla's solution was a third bell.

It was smaller, and it rang once for the first bell and twice for the second, to indicate which bell had rung. When all three went off at once, which they did, it sounded like a blacksmith's shop being thrown off a cliff.

That was the night Mike went into the Registrar's back room, knocked three times on the lectern, and asked for the Duke of Varrenhold's account.

The golem turned the great ledger's pages for a long time. When it stopped, Mike leaned in and read.

Then he read it again.

Then he went to see Brannoc.

The Guildmaster was in his office, standing in front of the wall behind his desk, hands clasped behind his back, admiring it. Mike had seen the wall before, many times, without really noticing it. It was covered with frames, dozens of them, neat rows of gilt frames, floor to ceiling, each holding a single sheet of cream paper.

He looked closer.

Each letter bore the boar-on-a-boar seal. Each was in capital letters. And each one said exactly one word.

**ADEQUATE.**

"Six years of them," said Brannoc proudly, sweeping a hand across the wall. "Every escort. Every job. His Grace has called us *adequate* two hundred and twelve times, lad. Do you know how many guilds can say that?"

"Guildmaster," said Mike carefully. "The Registrar says the Duke hasn't paid us in six years."

"Ah," said Brannoc, with great dignity. "His Grace pays in *prestige*."

Mike looked at the wall. He did the math. He couldn't help it. It was his skill, and his nature, and probably his curse. Two hundred and twelve escorts. Four people each, at the guild's standard night rate, plus overtime, plus the wedding, the griffin, the farmhouse, the clients on Tanner's Row, and a cloak.

The skill displayed the total in front of his eyes. He decided not to read it out loud. He was afraid it might get framed.

---

On the ninth night, the river dropped.

Pip saw it first: the last ring of runes on his gauge flickered, faded, and went dark, and the little stone dial glowed a pale, dry gold for the first time in eight years. He came up the ladder so fast he forgot to footnote anything.

And at almost the same moment, the door of the Guildmaster's office flew open, and Brannoc came storming out into the hall with a pink slip of paper in his fist.

"CLERK!" he roared. "Priscilla says the river's dropping! Didn't we sign something? About the crypt?"

Mike, at his desk, went very still.

In front of his eyes, a line of text that had been counting down patiently for eight years, through a whole childhood and a changelog and a writing desk, finally ran out of time.

**Estimated time until Brannoc remembers this contract: now.**

And at that exact moment, behind the front counter, all three of the Duke's bells began to ring at once.
