# Chapter 16: The Big Rewrite

The salesman arrived on a Tuesday, in a carriage painted bronze.

His name was Master Quint, of Quint & Daughters Automata, Purveyors of Fine Golems to the Capital and Surrounding Provinces, and he was a short, round, shiny man who seemed to have been polished along with his merchandise. He had a waxed mustache, a bronze-buttoned waistcoat, and a smile so bright and fixed it might have been cast in a mold. Two of his daughters came with him. They did not speak at all. They wheeled in a large shape under a velvet cloth, set it down in the middle of the guild hall, and stood on either side of it like bookends.

Rupert had arranged the whole thing. He had arranged, in fact, for the whole guild to watch. They were gathered in a half-circle around the velvet shape: clerks, adventurers, Brannoc, Priscilla, Gareth in his yellow headband, the roommates in their green belts. The Duke had come too, holding the dragon tooth, though it was not a stand-up day. Mike suspected he had simply forgotten what day it was and come anyway.

"Friends," said Rupert, his towel draped over his arm. "Today, we take the guild into the *future*."

Master Quint whisked off the velvet cloth.

The hall gasped.

It was a golem, but it was nothing like the Registrar. It was tall and slender and made of polished bronze, smooth all over, without a single seam or crack or patch. Its face was a calm, featureless oval with two small, bright, white lights for eyes. It had six thin, jointed arms, each ending in a different tool: a quill, a stamp, a blotter, a paper knife, a sealing-wax spoon, and something small and delicate that Mike could not identify. It gleamed. It did not hum. It did not do anything at all. It simply stood there, waiting, in perfect silence, as if it had never once in its life been confused.

Across its chest, in elegant engraved letters, was its name.

*OMNIFILE.*

"The OMNIFILE Mark Three," said Master Quint, in a voice like warm syrup. "The most advanced filing golem in the realm. No knocking. No incantations. No *rules*." He gave a delicate little shudder at the word. "Simply hand it your document, and it is filed."

He held out a contract to the OMNIFILE. It was, Mike noticed, written in blue ink.

One of the six arms reached out and took it. There was a soft *whirr*. The quill arm flickered. The stamp arm came down, *tok*, very neatly and very quietly. The contract vanished into a slot in the golem's chest. The whole thing had taken less than a second.

Then, from somewhere inside the OMNIFILE, came music: a short, bright, triumphant fanfare of trumpets, like the opening of a royal parade.

*Ta-da-da-DAAAA!*

The hall burst into applause.

"*Modern*," breathed Rupert, as if it were a prayer.

Mike was standing at the back, next to Durgan, who had been called upstairs again and was wearing his white belt. Durgan was looking at the OMNIFILE with the expression of a man watching someone sell a horse made of soap.

"It took blue ink," Mike murmured.

"It took blue ink," agreed Durgan.

"It didn't ask about the weather."

"No."

"Or the number seven. Or the side you're standing on."

Durgan said nothing. He didn't need to.

At the front, Master Quint had turned to Brannoc with a little bow. "Now, Guildmaster. Naturally, the OMNIFILE will be configured to replace your existing system. Every function. To do that, I'll need your requirements." He produced a silver pencil and a small leather notebook, and held them poised. "What does your current golem do?"

There was a silence.

Brannoc opened his mouth. He closed it. He looked at Rupert.

Rupert looked at Tolliver. Tolliver looked at Dex. Dex looked at his green belt.

"It... files things," said Brannoc at last.

"Splendid," said Master Quint, writing. "And what else?"

More silence.

"It stamps them," offered Gareth.

"Excellent. And?"

Rupert stepped forward smoothly, with the confident air of a man who had prepared for exactly this moment. He took a folded sheet of paper from his doublet and handed it to Master Quint.

"Our requirements," he said. "We put a lot of thought into this."

Master Quint unfolded the paper and read it aloud. It took him about three seconds.

"*Everything the old one does,*" he read. "*But faster. And modern.*"

He turned the paper over. The back was blank.

"Ah," said Master Quint. "Well. That's certainly... ambitious."

Mike stepped forward. He was carrying, under his arm, a thick bundle of paper bound in board and twine, three hundred and twelve pages long.

"This is everything the Registrar does," he said. "Every rule, every quirk, every exception. Eight years of testing. It's all written down." He held it out. "It's the complete list of requirements."

Master Quint's eyes lit up. He reached for it.

Tolliver stepped between them.

"Thanks, Mike," he said, with a warm, generous, slightly pitying smile. "Really. But we're not going to be constrained by *legacy thinking*."

He gently pushed the guide back into Mike's arms.

Master Quint looked at the guide, and at Tolliver, and at the single sheet of paper in his hand, and for a fleeting instant something human and weary crossed his polished face. Then the smile came back.

"Of course," he said. "Fresh thinking. Very wise."

Brannoc bought it on the spot.

He signed in front of the whole guild, with a flourish, on a long contract that Master Quint unrolled across the front counter. Nobody read it except Mike, who read it upside down over Brannoc's shoulder as he signed, and so was the only person in the hall who saw clause fourteen: a ten-year service agreement under which the OMNIFILE could only be maintained, repaired, adjusted, or removed by Quint & Daughters Automata, at Quint & Daughters' rates.

The payment line read: *On credit.*

Mike didn't need to ask whose.

He asked the skill something else instead.

**Estimated time to replace the Registrar: 2 days.**

**Estimated time to replace everything the Registrar does: unknown.**

**Undocumented.**

Mike looked down at the bundle of paper in his arms. Three hundred and twelve pages of exactly that, documented.

*Not undocumented,* he thought. *Unread.*

---

The roommates ran the migration.

Rupert called it *the migration*, which was a word he had found in the back of the Lean Six Sigil pamphlet, and he said it with relish. It meant moving every record from the old Registrar's great ledger into the OMNIFILE, so that the new golem would know everything the old one knew. Dex, Tolliver, and Bramble were put in charge of it, because, Rupert said, they had "real ownership" of the project.

They did it over two days, in the round back room, with the Registrar's ledger open on its huge lectern and the OMNIFILE standing beside it, gleaming, with its chest slot open like a mouth.

Mike offered to help. He was told that the team had it handled.

He offered, again, the guide. Tolliver did not even look up this time.

"That's *legacy documentation*, Mike," he said kindly, turning a page of the great ledger. "We're building for the future here."

Then he turned the ledger back several hundred pages, to the brittle brown section near the front, and frowned at it.

"What's all this old stuff?"

"Those are the guild's oldest records," said Mike. "Founding documents. Land. The seal—"

"Hmm." Tolliver tapped the page. "Some of these are *two hundred years old*. Nobody needs those." He turned to the others. "Lads, let's do a little data cleanup. Anything older than ten years, we'll skip. Stale data. Keeps the new system lean."

"*Lean*," said Dex approvingly, and touched his belt.

And so, while Mike watched, they flipped past the first hundred and ninety years of the ledger without copying a word. Past the founders. Past the deed with *(One demon, sealed.)* in parentheses. Past the records of the seal, and the warding of the front door, and the note that said *TEMPORARY*. Past every quirk and exception and workaround that two centuries of clerks had carefully written down, so the people who came after them would know.

Pip had come up from the Basement to watch. He stood in the doorway with his slate clutched to his chest, getting paler and paler, as page after page of two hundred years of records went by. When Tolliver casually flipped past the seal records, Pip made a small, high sound, like a whistle running out of air, and fainted.

He went down quite gently, all things considered. Mike caught him on the way.

The roommates looked at him with polite concern.

"Is he all right?" asked Bramble.

"He'll be fine," said Mike. "He just really cares about records."

"Huh," said Bramble, and played a sympathetic chord on his lute.

That night, after everyone had gone, Mike went back into the round room with a lantern. He opened the old Registrar's ledger to the brittle brown pages at the front. And one by one, by hand, in black ink, he made fresh copies of every record the roommates had skipped. He filed each copy with the old Registrar, which was still, for one more night, switched on: the founders, the deed, the seal, the door. He worked until nearly dawn.

The Registrar watched him do it, its blue eyes dim and steady. Every time the brass stamp came down, it came down very gently, as if it knew what was happening.

---

They switched it off the next morning.

They could not move it. The Registrar weighed, by Pip's estimate, about as much as a small barn, and the round room had been built around it, two hundred years ago; the door was not wide enough to get it out. Rupert had wanted it hauled away and broken up for scrap. Instead, it was decided, it would simply be switched off where it sat.

Nobody knew how to switch it off.

In the end, Mike did it. It was in the guide, on page 291, in a section he had written five years ago and never expected to use: *To put the Registrar to rest. Knock five times on the lectern. Say "Esteemed Registrar, you may rest." Wait. Do not leave until the eyes are dark.* He had learned it from Osric, who had learned it from Edwin, who had learned it from someone long dead. It had never been tested, because nobody had ever wanted the Registrar to rest.

He knocked five times. He said the words.

The Registrar's great stone head turned toward him. Its blue eyes looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, the quill arm lowered to the lectern and lay still. The stamp arm came to rest beside it. And the two blue lights dimmed, and dimmed, and went out.

The round room was very quiet.

Dex and Tolliver threw a large dust sheet over the golem, and the Registrar became a gray shape in the middle of the room, like furniture in a closed house.

Rupert had wanted it scrapped. Mike found out later what had stopped him. On the morning of the switch-off, before anyone else arrived, Durgan Flintmantle had climbed the ladder from the Basement, walked into Rupert's office, and stood in front of his desk.

He had not said very much. Rupert, afterward, could not quite remember what. Only that the dwarf had been very quiet, and very firm, and very close. At the end of it, he had heard himself agreeing that the old golem would be switched off, not scrapped, "as a heritage feature."

The roommates left. Durgan went back down the ladder.

Mike stayed for a moment, alone in the round room with the gray shape under the sheet.

Then, because he could not help himself, he stepped up to the lectern under its dust sheet, and knocked three times.

Nothing answered.

He stood there for a while longer. Then he went out, and closed the door behind him, quietly, the way you close a door on someone sleeping.

---

The OMNIFILE went live the next morning, at the stand-up, with a fanfare.

Or rather, with *a* fanfare.

Mike noticed it at once. The golem that Master Quint's daughters wheeled into the round room that morning was the OMNIFILE. It said so on its chest. But it was not, Mike thought, quite the same OMNIFILE. It seemed shorter, by about a head. Its bronze was a shade duller. It had six arms, but two of them didn't seem to match the others. And when Dex handed it the first contract of the morning, and it filed the contract, and the music played, it was not the bright, triumphant parade he remembered.

It was shorter. Three notes instead of four. And the last one was slightly flat.

*Ta-da-dwa.*

Nobody else seemed to notice. The hall applauded anyway. Rupert said *modern* again, and Brannoc rang the gong.

Mike went to the front counter, where the long contract with clause fourteen had been left in a drawer, and turned it over. On the back, in very small print at the very bottom, beneath Master Quint's signature and the Guildmaster's flourish, was a single line he had missed the first time.

*Demonstration unit shown for illustration purposes only. Production unit may vary.*

He read it twice. Then he put the contract back in the drawer.

In another life, at Synergex, he had once watched a salesman demonstrate a reporting tool that produced beautiful charts in two seconds. When the company bought it, they discovered that the beautiful charts had been drawn in advance by the salesman's team, and that the real tool, given real data, took eleven minutes to produce a chart, which was gray. Synergex had used it for six years.

And yet, for one glorious morning, everything worked.

It really did. That was the cruel part. The OMNIFILE took the lumber guild's contracts, in any ink. It took them on the left side and the right side. It took them without knocking and without anyone calling it Esteemed. It took them fast, very fast, *ta-da-dwa*, *ta-da-dwa*, and by noon it had filed more paper than the old Registrar used to file in a day.

Rupert walked around the guild hall all morning with his black belt on and a look of shining vindication, telling everyone who would listen that this was what *modernization* looked like.

Then, at eleven minutes to noon, Bramble filed a quest to clear seven rats from a bakery cellar.

The OMNIFILE took it. *Ta-da-dwa.*

Nothing happened.

Then, at noon exactly, every bounty in the guild's books doubled.

Not just the rat quest. Every one. The wolves, the goblins, the Thornwood, a lost cat, a haunted well, the Westmarch griffin. All of them, across every page of the OMNIFILE's records, at once. Mike found out when an adventurer named Corran came to the counter to collect his bounty for a minor errand and was handed twice what he'd been promised, and then went straight to the Rusty Flagon and told everyone.

Mike opened the guide to the page he knew it would be on. Page 47. *Rule 9. Never file anything involving the number seven before noon.* Underneath, in his own hand, the note he had made years ago: *Reason unknown. Osric says it's about money. Edwin, apparently, never said.*

It was about money.

By that afternoon, it was about something else as well.

The Duke's steward came in with a fresh letter for His Grace's file, a routine thing, boar-on-a-boar seal, capital letters. Dex took it and handed it to the OMNIFILE. The OMNIFILE did what it did with everything: it filed it, and stamped it.

It stamped the Duke's paperwork.

Mike, at the far end of the hall, heard the *tok* of the stamp. He went cold all over. He knew the rule. It had been one of the very first things he'd learned, on his very first day, from Priscilla: the Duke's paperwork could never be stamped. Not ever. *For reasons involving an old feud,* she had said, *and a seal that may or may not have been cursed.*

It had been cursed.

The first thing anybody noticed was a sound from the Guildmaster's office: a long, low, slithering hiss, like ink spilling across a great many pages at once. Then Brannoc's roar.

Mike got there first. The Guildmaster was standing in the middle of the office, staring at his wall.

The wall of ADEQUATEs. Two hundred and thirteen gilt frames, floor to ceiling. Overnight they had turned red. Now, as Mike watched, the red letters in every single frame were moving. They squirmed on the paper like worms, rearranging themselves, one by one, all across the wall, until each frame held a different word.

*MEDIOCRE.*

Two hundred and thirteen times. *MEDIOCRE. MEDIOCRE. MEDIOCRE.*

Brannoc stared at it. His big face went through a great many colors and settled on none of them.

"Don't tell the Duke," he whispered.

And that same afternoon, Tolliver filed a quest for a farmer whose geese had been stolen. Mike, passing the round room, saw it happen. He stopped in the doorway, rigid with dread.

Tolliver did not say *Your Honk*. Tolliver had no idea there was a rule about saying *Your Honk*. He handed the goose quest to the OMNIFILE, which took it, filed it, stamped it, and played its three flat notes.

Mike waited, holding his breath.

Nothing happened.

Nothing at all. No bounties doubled. No words squirmed. Nothing hissed. The goose quest sat in the OMNIFILE's records as quietly and harmlessly as a stone.

Rule 31 had really been a joke. Tobin Tully, a hundred and forty years ago, had simply wanted to see if anyone would notice. Malphus had been right.

And Mike, standing in the doorway, found it unexpectedly comforting. It meant the others had been *real*. It meant that every one of those strange old rules, the black ink, the left side, the sevens, the Duke's seal, had not been superstition, or habit, or a clerk's joke. Somebody, once, long ago, had learned each one the hard way, and written it down, so that nobody would ever have to learn it again.

All except one. And that one was about geese.

---

The bells rang that night as usual, at a quarter to three. Between them, the OMNIFILE went *ta-da-dwa* every time anything was filed, which, now that it had been switched on, appeared to be something it did at any hour, for any reason, including in the middle of the night, possibly to itself.

The guild hall now had four separate alarms: three bells and a fanfare. They went off at random, and sometimes together.

Durgan Flintmantle took his blanket and his pillow and moved down into the Basement, where it was quiet, and did not come back up to sleep again.

---

It was Dex who fed it.

Nobody ever established exactly why. Dex himself, when asked, could only say that he had been working late (which nobody had asked him to do, and which he had never done before), and that the OMNIFILE had looked *hungry*.

"Hungry?" Mike repeated.

"It had its mouth open," said Dex. "The slot. In its chest. Just standing there. And there was a big stack of blank parchment on the desk, and I thought, well, it eats paper, doesn't it? That's what it *does*." He looked at Mike with innocent eyes. "So I gave it some. A treat."

"What time was this?"

Dex considered. "After dark," he said. "Just after the lamps went on."

Mike closed his eyes.

He did not have to look in the guide. He knew this one by heart. It was the very first rule he'd ever been given in this building, on his very first day, written in a hurried, blotchy hand on a single sheet of paper under an inkpot. It was now the first line of page one of *The Registrar: A Guide*, copied in his own hand. Edwin's warning.

*Don't feed the Registrar after dusk.*

By morning, the OMNIFILE had filed everyone.

Mike came in at dawn and found the quest board covered. Not with quests. With people. The OMNIFILE had spent the night eating Dex's stack of blank parchment and, finding nothing written on it, had apparently decided to fill it in. It had produced a quest notice for every single person in the guild hall, all neatly written in its quick, characterless hand, all stamped, and all pinned to the board. There were so many that the board had overflowed, and the notices ran down the wall, across the floor, and out the new front door into the square, where several had blown into the fountain.

The quest board was twelve feet of people.

Mike walked along it slowly, reading.

*Gareth the Bold. Status: URGENT.*

*Priscilla, Head of Client Delight. Status: OVERDUE.*

*Dex. Status: UNDOCUMENTED.*

*Osric. Status: NOT FOUND.*

*Velka. Status: ON FIRE (INTERMITTENT).*

*Mikel Dobbins. Status: ASSIGNED (ALL).*

*Rupert Spindlewick, Chief Ink Officer. Status: UNASSIGNED.*

And at the very top of the board, in the place of honor, on a slightly larger sheet with a gold border, the OMNIFILE had filed the Guildmaster himself.

*Brannoc, Guildmaster. Status: COMPLETED.*

Brannoc came in an hour later and stood in front of it for a long time. Nobody said anything. Gareth tried to take his own notice down, and found it had been stamped in such a way that it couldn't be removed without filing a request with the OMNIFILE, which immediately stamped the request URGENT and pinned it beside the first one.

Rupert came in last. He read his own notice twice.

"*Unassigned*," he said, with a small, injured frown. "That can't be right. I have *several* assignments."

"Which ones?" asked Mike.

Rupert opened his mouth, and then, for the first time Mike had ever seen, seemed unable to find a word to put in it.

---

They woke the Registrar that night.

Mike, Durgan, and Pip, after the hall had emptied, with a lantern, in the round room. Durgan pulled the dust sheet off in one long, careful motion, the way you might uncover a sleeping horse, and folded it, and laid it on the floor.

The Registrar sat where it had always sat, dark and still, gray stone and old iron bands and two hundred years of patches. Beside it, the OMNIFILE gleamed in the lantern light with its chest slot still slightly open, humming a faint, satisfied little hum, as if pleased with its night's work.

Mike stepped up to the old lectern.

He had looked it up. Page 292, the page after *To put the Registrar to rest*. He had written that one, too, five years ago, from something Osric had once said. *To wake the Registrar, if it has been put to rest. Knock four times, as if filing for someone absent, and say "on behalf of."*

"On behalf of whom?" Pip had asked, when Mike showed him.

"I don't know," Mike had said. "Everyone, I suppose."

He knocked four times on the lectern.

"Esteemed Registrar," he said. "On behalf of."

For a long moment, nothing happened.

Then, deep inside the great stone body, something clicked. Then something else turned over, slowly, with a sound like a millstone beginning to move. The iron bands creaked. And in the dark, gray, featureless face, two faint points of blue light appeared, flickered, and grew, until they glowed steady and clear, exactly as they always had.

The Registrar was awake.

Pip made a small sound. Durgan put one broad hand flat on the golem's stone shoulder, and left it there.

The Registrar's eyes moved. They looked at Mike. They looked at Durgan's hand. They looked at the dust sheet folded on the floor. Then, slowly, they looked out through the open door of the round room, into the dark guild hall, at the quest board and the twelve feet of people.

The Registrar began to hum.

It was not the wary, listening hum of the last few weeks. It was the old hum, the working hum, low and steady and enormously patient. Its quill arm rose. Its stamp arm rose. And it began to work.

It worked all night. Mike sat on the floor and watched it, and so did Pip and Durgan, and none of them said very much. One by one, the Registrar un-filed the guild. It took down every person from the quest board, and every notice from the wall, and every sheet from the floor and the fountain. It un-stamped Brannoc's COMPLETED and Gareth's URGENT and Rupert's UNASSIGNED. It found the doubled bounties and halved them again. It quietly re-filed every record the roommates had skipped, from the copies Mike had made, in their proper places. It worked slowly, and carefully, and in the old order, knocking and stamping and humming, and it did not play any music at all.

It was nearly dawn when it finished.

The great brass stamp came down one last time, *BOOM*, and the Registrar sat back on its stone haunches, and its humming settled into the deep, contented note that Mike had listened to for eight years.

Then its blue eyes turned, slowly, to the right.

To the OMNIFILE.

The bronze golem stood beside it, gleaming, six arms neatly folded. Its white eyes were bright. Its slot was open. In the gray light from the window, it looked very new, and very shiny, and very small.

The Registrar studied it for a long time.

In eight years, Mike had heard the Registrar say exactly two words aloud. The first was *FILED*, which it said every day, every time, with every stamp. The second was *NOTED*, which it had said once, on the day the inquiry refused to read Mike's evidence.

Now, looking down at the OMNIFILE from its great stone height, in a voice like a millstone settling, the Registrar said a third.

"JUNIOR."

The OMNIFILE's white eyes flickered.

Pip clapped both hands over his mouth. Durgan made a sound that Mike had never heard from him before, low in his chest, which it took Mike a moment to recognize as laughter.

---

They could not send the OMNIFILE back.

Mike checked. He read clause fourteen four more times. He wrote to Quint & Daughters Automata, very politely, explaining that the guild would like to return the golem. He received a reply, very politely, explaining that under the terms of the ten-year service agreement, the OMNIFILE could only be removed by Quint & Daughters, at Quint & Daughters' rates, which were attached. He looked at the attached rates, and then he went down to the Registrar and looked at Malphus's ledger, where the OMNIFILE already had its own line, and its interest was already compounding, and he came to the conclusion that the guild could not afford to keep the golem, and could not afford to get rid of it.

So they kept both.

That was how it ended. That was how it always ended, Mike thought. He had seen it in every company he had ever worked for. You bought the shiny new system to replace the old one. The new one couldn't do everything the old one did. The old one couldn't be turned off, because only it knew how to do those things. And the new one couldn't be turned off either, because you'd paid for it, and signed for it, and announced it at a meeting with a fanfare.

So from that day on, every document filed at the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor went through two golems.

It went through the OMNIFILE first, for speed. *Ta-da-dwa.* And then, because the OMNIFILE got things wrong, it went through the Registrar, to be right. *Knock, knock, knock. Esteemed Registrar. BOOM. FILED.* Every contract, every quest, every receipt and request and complaint. Twice.

The paperwork doubled.

Mike asked the skill, because he already knew the answer and wanted to see it written down.

**Estimated time until the guild stops running both: never.**

---

Rupert reviewed the go-live at the next morning's stand-up.

He had prepared a report. It was signed *R. Spindlewick, Chief Ink Officer*, which was, Mike noted, back where it had started. He read it aloud to the circle with the grave, earnest, responsible air he had now perfected.

"The OMNIFILE go-live," said Rupert, "was, in many ways, a *tremendous success*. We modernized our core infrastructure. We increased filing speed by over four hundred percent. We had some challenges. And I want to be transparent about those challenges." He turned a page. "We've done a root cause analysis, and the root cause is clear. Inadequate migration documentation from the clerk's office."

He looked across the circle at Mike, with sorrow.

He was not alone. Master Quint, it emerged, had sent a letter. Rupert read that too. On behalf of Quint & Daughters Automata, Master Quint regretted any inconvenience. He was confident that the OMNIFILE had performed to specification. He noted, with respect, that the difficulties appeared to have been caused by *inadequate customer-side documentation*.

Mike, behind his pillar, looked at the guide under his arm. Three hundred and twelve pages. Every rule. The sevens. The Duke's seal. Edwin's note on page one.

"The guide," he said, "is three hundred and twelve pages long. It covers every one of those problems. It was offered to the vendor. It was offered to the migration team. It was declared legacy, and it was never opened." He looked around the circle. "By anyone."

Rupert's face lit up, as if Mike had at last understood him.

"*Exactly*," he said warmly. "*Legacy* documentation." He spread his hands. "That's on you, Mike."

That afternoon, a junior clerk came halfway down the Basement ladder, threw a scroll tied with a red ribbon, and fled.

Durgan opened the drawer marked *MIKE* and laid it inside, beside the other two.

"Three," said Durgan.

"Three," said Mike.

---

That night, alone at his desk behind Osric's stack, Mike asked the skill the question he had been carefully not asking for two weeks.

**Estimated time until the first payment to Malphus: 12 days.**

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 19 days.**

He looked at the second number for a long time.

Nineteen. Of course it was nineteen. It was always nineteen. He was beginning to think the universe had a favorite number, and was not going to stop using it until he admitted it was funny.

The stack beside him rustled. A small gray hand emerged and set something on the corner of his desk. It was another cookie. It was, if anything, older than the last one.

He ate it anyway.

He was still sitting there, chewing, when the trapdoor behind him banged open, and Velka came up the ladder from the Basement so fast that she nearly took the trapdoor off its hinges. She had clearly been down there looking for him. Her hair was wild, her cheeks were flushed, and there was a scorch mark on her sleeve, still smoking. In both hands she was clutching a sheet of paper covered from edge to edge in diagrams, runes, and frantic, triumphant scribbles.

"*Mike*," she said. Her eyes were shining. "I've done it. I've invented a brand-new spell. Nobody's ever cast anything like it." She slapped the paper down on his desk, on top of the guide. "And it's going to save the Contraption's field test."

Mike looked at the paper. He looked at the smoking sleeve.

"Have you tested it?"

Velka smiled at him, radiantly.

"It works perfectly," she said, "in my tower."
