## Chapter 18: The Last Archmage

The Archmage of Brindlemoor lived above the city gate.

Mike had walked under it ten thousand times, every day of his life in this world, without once looking up. It was a squat stone tower built right into the arch of the east gate, with a narrow stair winding up the inside of the wall and a round room at the top, from which you could see the whole city: the roofs, the river, the guild hall, and, if you knew where to look, the faint, pale shimmer in the air at the edge of the walls that was the barrier.

Pip had never been there either. He climbed the stair beside Mike in a state of suppressed, trembling excitement, clutching his slate to his chest, the way some people might approach a saint's shrine.

"He designed the ward board," Pip whispered, for the fifth time. "The *original* ward board. Footnote: I've been maintaining his work for nineteen years. Footnote: I've written to him six times. Footnote: he's never written back."

"Maybe he's busy."

"Footnote," said Pip, with feeling, "*nine years*."

The door at the top of the stair was open. Inside, in the round room, by the window, in a sagging armchair with a blanket over his knees, sat the oldest man Mike had ever seen.

He was very small, and very thin, and very white: white hair, white eyebrows, a long white beard that reached his lap and had a small bird's nest in it, which appeared to be empty. His skin was as wrinkled and soft as old paper. His eyes, when he turned them toward the door, were bright pale blue and full of a cheerful, untroubled light.

"*Visitors!*" said Thessaly, Archmage of Brindlemoor, delighted. "Oh, how lovely. Come in, come in. Sit anywhere. Not there, there's a lizard."

There was a lizard. Mike sat somewhere else.

Pip sat on the very edge of a stool, staring at the archmage with enormous eyes. He seemed unable to speak. Mike decided to begin.

"Archmage," he said. "We're from the Adventurers' Guild. We got your letter. About your retirement."

"Ah, yes!" Thessaly beamed. "Two hundred and six years. That's quite enough for anyone, I think. I'm going to go fishing."

"Before you do," said Mike carefully, "we were hoping you could tell us how the barrier works. So that someone else can keep it running."

"Oh, of course! It's quite simple." Thessaly leaned forward in his armchair with an air of great helpfulness. "You just sort of... *feel* it."

There was a pause.

Mike looked at Pip. Pip's stylus was poised over his slate, motionless.

"Feel it," said Mike.

"Mm." Thessaly nodded happily.

"Could you be a bit more specific?"

Thessaly considered this. He tilted his head. He seemed to be trying very hard. "Well," he said slowly, "you hum it. In the key of the third wall."

Pip wrote this down. Then he looked at it, with a small frown, as if it had come out wrong.

"What *is* the key of the third wall?" asked Mike.

Thessaly looked genuinely baffled by the question, the way you might look at someone who asked what color the color blue was. "Well," he said, "you do it the way anyone would."

"That's not obvious."

"Oh, but it is," said Thessaly, kindly. "It's been obvious for two hundred years."

Mike sat back in his chair.

He knew this man. He had worked with this man in another life, many times, in many forms. He had been a senior engineer at Synergex named Walt, who had written the whole payment system in a single month in 2011 and had never written a single comment, and who, whenever anyone asked how a part of it worked, had smiled the same kind, untroubled smile, and said the same thing.

"The code," Mike said slowly, "is *self-documenting*."

Thessaly's face lit up as if Mike had finally understood.

"*Exactly*," said the archmage warmly. "The barrier is *self-explanatory*."

Mike asked the skill. He did not expect much.

**Estimated time until Thessaly explains anything: unknown.**

It was the most honest answer he had heard all day.

---

The letters were in the corner.

Mike noticed them when he got up to look out of the window and found that he couldn't get to it, because there was a hill in the way. It was a hill made of paper: hundreds of folded, sealed, unopened letters, heaped against the wall of the round room from floor to waist height, sliding gently at the edges, some of the oldest so yellow and brittle they had begun to crumble.

"What are all these?" he asked.

Thessaly glanced at them vaguely. "Oh, letters. People write. I keep meaning to get to them."

"How long have they been here?"

Thessaly thought. "Nine years? Ten? It's hard to tell, after a while."

Pip had gone very still on his stool. Then, slowly, as if in a dream, he got up, walked over to the hill of letters, and knelt beside it. He began, very carefully, to sort through the top layer. He found the first one within a minute. Then the second. Then, digging deeper, three more. He laid them out in a row on the floor in front of him, in order, by date.

There were six of them, all addressed to the Archmage of Brindlemoor in small, neat, gnomish handwriting, and all sealed with the stamp of the guild's Ward Maintenance Department. None of them had been opened.

Pip looked at them for a long time.

"Footnote," he said at last, very quietly, to no one. "I knew it wasn't personal."

Mike found another letter while he was helping Pip put the pile back. It was near the middle, in thick, cream guild paper, sealed with a great blob of wax and a boar-tooth stamp. It was addressed in a huge, slanting hand that Mike would have known anywhere.

He turned it over. *From Guildmaster Brannoc. Seven years ago.*

"May I?" he asked.

Thessaly waved a hand. "Please. I'd never get to it."

Mike broke the seal. Inside was a single line.

*Archmage. Quick favor: can you extend the barrier to cover the new guild stables? Won't take a minute. — B.*

Mike looked out of the window, past the hill of letters, at the city. The barrier shimmered faintly at the edge of the walls. And just beyond it, outside the pale curve of light, sat the long, low roof of the guild's new stables, the ones that had been built seven years ago and warded by Durgan and Pip in nine days and two hours.

They had been outside the city barrier the whole time. Seven years. And nobody had noticed.

Mike folded the letter and put it in his pocket.

"Archmage," he said. "Didn't you ever have anyone to help you? An apprentice?"

"Oh, several!" said Thessaly fondly. "Lovely young people. Very bright."

"What happened to them?"

"They left." Thessaly sighed a little. "They all left, in the end. I never understood why. I gave them such interesting things to watch."

"To *watch*?"

"Well, I couldn't let them *do* it," said Thessaly, as if this were obvious. "Not the barrier. It's far too important. And it's so much faster if I just do it myself." He smiled. "So I did. Every day. For two hundred years. And I never needed to write anything down, you see, because I was always here."

Mike looked at the tiny, cheerful old man in the armchair, with a bird's nest in his beard and a hill of unopened letters in the corner, and understood, all at once, the full shape of the problem.

For two hundred years, every person in Brindlemoor had slept safe behind a barrier that existed entirely inside the head of one man. One man, who had never let anyone help him, never written anything down, and never opened his mail. And at the end of this month, he was going fishing.

In his old life, they had a name for it. They called it the *bus factor*: the number of people who would have to be hit by a bus before a project was doomed. Mike had worked on systems where the bus factor was one. He had been, at times, the one.

He had never seen a bus factor of one hold up a city.

---

Rupert had a solution by the next morning's stand-up.

"Team," he said, with his towel over his arm and his black belt around his doublet, "I've been thinking about this archmage situation, and I believe it's actually a tremendous opportunity." He paused for effect. "We're going to *automate the archmage*."

The circle by the window stared at him.

"We already have the OMNIFILE," Rupert went on, shining. "Modern. Efficient. Fast. Why not have it maintain the barrier? Then we'll never have to worry about archmages again."

The Duke raised the dragon tooth. "IS IT DONE YET?"

"Not yet, Your Grace, but it's on the roadmap."

Mike, behind his pillar, closed his eyes.

That afternoon, a pigeon arrived from the capital. It bore a small, glossy, folded brochure, printed in bronze ink, with a drawing on the front of a tall, polished golem standing heroically on a city wall, arms raised, surrounded by a dome of light.

*Quint & Daughters Automata proudly presents: the BARRIERMATIC Mark One! Your city's protection, fully automated! No archmage required! Now with optional fanfare!*

Rupert pinned it to the stand-up board. Brannoc stood in front of it for a long time, stroking his beard.

"I'd like to see a demo," he said.

Mike opened his mouth. But before he could speak, he heard a voice behind him, low and gravelly and very firm. And as he turned, he heard his own voice say the same word, at exactly the same moment.

"*No*," said Mike and Durgan together.

The Guildmaster blinked at them. So did everyone else. Mike and Durgan looked at each other. Durgan, who was still wearing his white belt, gave a short, sharp nod.

Brannoc cleared his throat. "Well. Perhaps not a demo. Not *yet*."

So Rupert, being Rupert, did the other thing.

He scheduled a *Knowledge Transfer Session*.

It was to be held at ten o'clock the following morning, in the council room. It was to last exactly one hour. Rupert had written the title on a slate at the front of the room in neat chalk: *KT SESSION: CITY BARRIER*. Underneath it was a checklist with a single empty box.

*Knowledge transferred.*

Thessaly came. He was carried down the stair from his tower in a sedan chair by two porters, wrapped in his blanket, with the lizard on his shoulder, beaming at everyone. He was installed in the best chair at the head of the council table. Rupert sat at his right hand with a quill. Mike, Pip, Durgan, Velka, and a scattering of curious clerks sat around the rest of the table.

"Archmage," said Rupert, with great respect, "thank you so much for joining us. Over to you. Please share your knowledge."

"Oh, certainly," said Thessaly. He settled into the chair, folded his hands on his blanket, and gazed out of the window at nothing in particular. "Well. I suppose it all started with the goat."

And for the next hour, Thessaly told them about a goat.

It was, Mike would later admit, a remarkable story. The goat's name had been Bettany, and she had belonged to Thessaly a hundred and eighty years ago, when he was a young man and the barrier was new. Bettany had followed him everywhere. She had followed him around the walls every morning. She had eaten things she shouldn't have. She had bleated at things that weren't there. She had fallen in love with a weathervane. She had once, memorably, got her head stuck in the third wall. The story wandered and doubled back and stopped in the middle for long, fond pauses, and it did not, at any point, appear to be about the barrier at all.

Rupert listened to all of it with an expression of fixed, nodding, professional attentiveness. Velka fell asleep about halfway through, upright, with her new hat tipped over her eyes. Durgan stared at the ceiling.

And Pip, out of pure habit, because he had never in his life been able to hear a story without footnoting it, wrote down every single word.

At eleven o'clock exactly, Thessaly finished the story of the goat. (She had lived to a great age, and died happy, in a field.) He looked around the table with a gentle, satisfied smile.

"And that's really all there is to it," he said.

Rupert rose. He walked to the slate. He picked up a piece of chalk.

And with a firm, decisive stroke, he ticked the box.

*Knowledge transferred.* ✓

"Wonderful session," said Rupert warmly. "Thank you all."

He rang the gong on his way out.

Mike sat in the emptying council room and looked at the tick on the slate for a long time. Then he asked the skill, because he wanted to see it in writing.

**Knowledge transferred: one goat.**

---

So Mike did what he should have done in the first place. He stopped asking Thessaly to explain, and started watching what he did.

They went up to the walls at dawn the next day: Mike, with a notebook, and Pip, with his slate. They found Thessaly already there, standing on the parapet of the east gate in the gray half-light, out of his sedan chair, a tiny figure in a long white robe with his blanket around his shoulders and the lizard tucked inside it for warmth.

He didn't explain anything. He just walked.

He walked the whole circuit of the city walls, slowly, at the pace of a very old man, which took the better part of three hours. And every so often, apparently at random, he stopped, and put out one thin, papery hand, and laid it flat against a particular stone in the wall. He held it there for a few seconds, humming something low and wordless under his breath. Then he walked on.

Mike counted. There were forty-seven stones.

He wrote down every one. Where it was. What it looked like. How long Thessaly touched it. What he hummed. Pip, beside him, drew a map, and footnoted the map.

They did it again the next morning, and the next. And by the third morning, Mike had noticed a few things.

Thessaly did not just touch the nineteenth stone. He took a small pinch of something white from a pouch at his belt and rubbed it into a crack, gently, before he laid his hand on it.

"Salt," said Pip, sniffing his fingers after Thessaly had moved on. "Footnote: it's salt."

"Why salt?"

Pip didn't know. Neither did Mike.

And on the third wall, the long north stretch that ran above the river, Thessaly's humming changed. It dropped, suddenly and noticeably, to a lower, rougher note, almost a groan, for the whole length of the wall, and then rose again as he turned the corner.

"The key of the third wall," said Mike slowly. "That's what he meant."

"But *why* is it lower?"

Nobody knew.

That evening, in the Basement, Pip sat at his desk going back through everything he had written down that week, looking for anything that might explain the salt or the low note. He went through the dawn notes. He went through the map. And then, almost absentmindedly, he turned back to the very first page of his slate, to the hour of footnotes he'd taken at the Knowledge Transfer Session. The goat.

He read for a minute. Then he sat bolt upright.

"*Mike*."

Mike came over. Pip was stabbing a finger at a line of tiny writing.

"*Bettany was very fond of salt*," Pip read aloud, from his own notes, in a shaking voice. "*She would lick it off the nineteenth stone every morning, where the masons had left a salt mark. I took to rubbing a pinch there myself, to keep her happy. The barrier rather got used to it.*"

Mike stared at him.

Pip was already scrolling down. "And here. *Whenever we walked the north wall, she would bleat at the river, very low and very sad, because she was frightened of water. I used to hum along with her, to keep her calm. I suppose I still do.*"

There was a long silence in the Basement.

"The goat," said Mike.

"The goat," said Pip.

"The whole story. It was all in the goat. The salt, the low note on the third wall. It was all *there*. He told us. He told us everything, for a full hour, and we just didn't recognize it as instructions." Mike sat down hard on a stool. "And Rupert ticked the box."

"He did transfer the knowledge," said Pip faintly. "Footnote: he really did." He looked down at his slate, at a hundred and eighty years of an archmage's craft, hidden in the rambling story of a goat who loved salt and was afraid of rivers. "Only nobody wrote it down. Except me."

From his chair, without lowering his pamphlet, Durgan said: "That's where it always is."

Mike looked at him.

"The real stuff," said Durgan. "Never in the manual. Always in some story about a goat."

---

There was one stone Thessaly never touched.

It was the thirty-first. Every morning, on every circuit, he walked up to it, looked at it fondly, and walked straight past.

On the fourth morning, Pip couldn't stand it anymore.

"Archmage," he said, scampering to catch up. "The thirty-first stone. Footnote: why don't you ever touch it?"

"Oh, that one?" said Thessaly cheerfully, over his shoulder. "That one's just for fun."

Pip stopped dead on the parapet.

Mike, who had been told by a contract devil only a few weeks ago that Rule 31 of the Registrar was a joke started by a clerk a hundred and forty years ago, had to stop too, and look out over the wall for a moment to collect himself.

Pip insisted on testing it. Of course he did. He waited until Thessaly had walked on, and then, with Mike watching, and with a face of fierce scientific resolve, he walked back to the thirty-first stone, took a deep breath, and laid his hand flat against it.

Nothing happened.

The barrier did not flicker. The walls did not shake. The stone was just a stone, cold and gray in the dawn light.

Pip took his hand away. He took out his slate. And in the margin of the barrier map, beside the thirty-first stone, in his neatest, smallest writing, he wrote:

*Stone 31: Joke. Confirmed.*

He was quietly satisfied for the rest of the day.

---

It was on that same fourth morning, near the end of the circuit, that Pip noticed the lines.

They were standing on the parapet above the river, and the sun had just come up, and in that low gold light the barrier was more visible than Mike had ever seen it: a faint, shimmering dome of pale light, rising from the forty-seven stones and arching over the whole city. And Pip, squinting at it through his enormous spectacles with his head on one side, made a small, puzzled sound.

"Mike," he said. "Look. Down there. Inside the walls."

Mike looked.

The barrier did not only rise from the walls. Faint as threads of spider silk, there were lines of the same pale light running *inward* from the stones, down into the ground, under the streets of the city. They ran from every direction, from all forty-seven stones, and they all converged on a single point, near the middle of town, near the square.

They all converged on the guild hall.

"The barrier," said Pip slowly, "is connected to the ward board." He swallowed. "It's connected to the *seal*."

They turned around together.

Thessaly was standing a little way along the parapet, wrapped in his blanket, watching them with mild interest. He followed their gaze down to the guild hall, and his face lit up with recognition, as if they'd pointed out an old friend in a crowd.

"Oh yes," said the archmage. "I did the seal on that demon. Two hundred years ago."

Mike went very still.

"*You* did it?"

"Mm-hm. The guild's founders asked me. They'd bought that plot of land, you see, terribly cheap, and they wanted to build on it, and they needed something to keep the demon down. They didn't have much money." He chuckled softly. "Or much time. They wanted it done by the end of the week, for the grand opening. So I did it in an afternoon. Wired it into the barrier for a bit of extra strength. Carved the door frame myself."

He smiled a little, reminiscently.

"Rush job," he said.

Mike could hardly breathe. "There's a note on it. Carved into the frame. *Temporary. Replace before winter.*"

"Ah, yes. That was me too." Thessaly looked fondly down at the distant roof of the guild hall, and for just a moment, something passed across his ancient face: something rueful, and a little sad, and very human. "I always meant to come back."

---

Mike wrote up the notes that night, at his desk behind Osric's stack: all forty-seven stones, the salt, the low note, the goat, the lines running inward to the guild. He wrote it all out properly, neatly, from the beginning, in a fresh book. At the top of the first page, he wrote its title.

*The City Barrier: A Guide.*

And somewhere in the middle of writing it, around the part about Thessaly's apprentices, he stopped, and put down his pen, and sat back in his chair.

*I never needed to write anything down, you see, because I was always here.*

Mike looked down at his own desk, and at the guide to the Registrar, three hundred and twelve pages. And at the forty other things he did every day that weren't in it. Which drawer the lumber guild's papers went in. How to calm the OMNIFILE when it played its fanfare at three in the morning. Which of the Duke's demands were real, and which were just loneliness. How the bells worked. How the gong worked. How everything worked.

*Mike knows.*

He had heard those two words a hundred times in the past month. He had been proud of them, a little, if he was honest. And he had been, he realized now, slowly and without ever meaning to, building himself into the exact same kind of tower.

The next morning, he found Tam.

Tam was the young clerk who had asked about the blue drawers on the day Mike first heard *Mike knows*. He was thirteen now, still small, still nervous, and he looked faintly alarmed when the senior clerk sat down beside his desk and said, "I'd like to teach you how to file with the Registrar."

"Me?"

"You."

It took a week. Mike showed him all of it: the black ink, the three knocks, the left side, the number seven, the Duke's seal, the four knocks and *on behalf of*. He made Tam do each one himself, not just watch. He made him do it again when he got it wrong. He gave him a copy of the guide and made him read it, and then made him find the three places where it was out of date and fix them himself.

Then he taught Osric.

Or rather, he tried to, and discovered on the first afternoon that Osric did not need teaching at all. Osric, from inside the stack, answered every question before Mike had finished asking it. He knew the Registrar's rules better than the guide. He knew the lumber guild's paperwork better than the lumber guild. He knew things about the founding of the guild that weren't written down anywhere. He had, it turned out, been at the guild for fifty-one years, and had simply never been asked anything except where Edwin was.

Mike sat beside the stack for a long time after that conversation, not saying anything. Then he got up and fetched a fresh notebook, and gave it to Osric, and said, "Would you write some of it down?"

There was a long pause from inside the stack.

Then a small, gray, wrinkled hand emerged, and took the notebook, and withdrew.

And Velka learned the whole knocking sequence in a single afternoon, because she had a mage's memory, and then she borrowed the guide, and brought it back the next day with the spelling of eleven rune names corrected in red ink in the margins.

"You spelled *Esteemed* wrong on page forty," she told him.

"I did not."

"Two *e*s, Mike."

He checked. She was right.

At the end of the week, Mike was at his desk when a brand-new clerk, a girl of twelve on her very first day, stood in the middle of the alcove holding a contract in blue ink and looking lost.

"Excuse me," she said to the room. "Does anyone know how to file this with the old golem? It won't take it."

And from the next desk over, without even looking up, someone answered.

"Tam knows."

Mike sat very still.

He had heard *Edwin knew*, on his first day, a lifetime ago. He had heard *Mike knows* a hundred times. Tam was already getting up, already walking the new girl toward the round room, already explaining about black ink.

Mike found that he could not remember the last time he had been so happy to hear a sentence.

---

They finished *The City Barrier: A Guide* on the last day of the month.

It was two hundred and four pages long. It had forty-seven stones in it, with a map, and footnotes on the map. It had the salt and the low note, explained. It had the goat, in full, with a summary at the top for people in a hurry. It had the lines that ran under the city to the guild hall, and the story of the rush-job seal, and the note that said *TEMPORARY*.

Mike and Pip carried it up the stair to the round room above the gate and gave it to Thessaly.

The archmage read every page. It took him most of the afternoon. He read slowly, the way very old people sometimes read, with his finger moving under each line. Now and then he chuckled. Once, at the part about the goat, he wiped his eyes. Near the end, he stopped, frowned, took a pen from somewhere in his beard, and corrected a single rune on page one hundred and sixty-two.

"Small thing," he said. "But it would have mattered."

Then he turned to the last page and signed it, in thin, spidery, very old letters.

*Read and approved. — Thessaly.*

He closed the book and laid it on his blanket, and looked up at them, and smiled.

"Splendid," said the Archmage of Brindlemoor. "Now I can go fishing."

Mike laughed. He couldn't help it. A man named Edwin had once said almost exactly the same thing, and fled to somewhere very far away where nobody could ask him anything, and left behind a single sheet of paper and a guild full of people who had to learn everything by guessing.

Thessaly was going fishing too. But this time, someone had written it down first.

As they were leaving, at the top of the stair, Thessaly called Mike back.

"That seal," said the archmage. His bright pale eyes had a thoughtful look. "The one on the demon. If anyone ever wants to fix it properly, I'll come back. For a day." He smiled. "I'd like to see it done right, before I go. I've been meaning to for two hundred winters."

"I'll remember," said Mike.

"I know," said Thessaly. "You write things down."

---

He left the next morning, in the sedan chair, with the lizard and a fishing rod and a small bag of salt, through the east gate and down the river road. The whole guild came out to see him go. Rupert made a short speech about legacy and transformation and rang the gong. Thessaly waved happily at everyone and did not appear to hear a word of it.

That afternoon, back at the guild, a new clerk wandered into the alcove with a puzzled expression and stopped by Osric's stack.

"Excuse me," she said. "Somebody said the archmage left. So who knows how the city barrier works now?"

There was a pause.

And from deep inside the stack, in a dry, quiet, papery voice that had said *Edwin knew* for fifty years, and then *Mike knows*, Osric answered.

"The guide knows."

Mike, at his desk, put down his pen.

Then he asked the skill, as he always did.

**Estimated time until the first payment to Malphus: 4 days.**

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 11 days.**

He had barely finished reading it when a small white pigeon with a pink ribbon landed on the corner of his desk and held out its leg.

The note was in violet ink. It smelled of perfume.

> *Mikey!! WONDERFUL news!! The Contraption has been resold to SIX new clients!! Each one has asked for just ONE small new feature!! Field test in THREE DAYS, at the river!! Everyone's coming!! XO, P. (Head of Client Delight)*

Mike read it twice.

Then he put his head down on his desk, very gently, beside the cookie that had just appeared on the corner of it.
