## Chapter 4: Nineteen Days

"First thing" in Mike's old life had meant nine o'clock, or whenever Chad finished his smoothie. In Brindlemoor, it meant dawn. So Mike was at the guild two hours before it, banging on the side door until the night watchman let him in, grumbling, with a candle and a look of deep personal betrayal.

The Sunken Crypt notice was at the bottom of the URGENT pile, where Mike had left it. He eased it out. The paper was the color of weak tea and so brittle that a corner snapped off in his fingers. Under the red URGENT stamp, in faded ink, it read:

*URGENT: Clear the Sunken Crypt of Varrenhold. Recover the Signet of Aldric Varrenhold, 3rd Duke. Reward: generous.*

He took it to the back room, knocked three times on the lectern, and asked the Esteemed Registrar for the history.

The golem considered this. Then its quill arm swept the great ledger open, flipped back through decades of pages with a sound like a long sigh, and stopped.

The entry had been made thirty-one years ago. Under it, in six different hands, were the records of six different parties who had taken the quest. In the column marked *Outcome*, the Registrar's neat, unforgiving script read:

*Party 1: Wet.*

*Party 2: Wetter.*

*Party 3: Wet. Lost boots.*

*Party 4: Did not return. (Returned eleven years later. Wet.)*

*Party 5: Wet.*

*Party 6: Resigned.*

Mike read the list twice. Thirty-one years. Six parties. A notice stamped URGENT at the bottom of a pile so old the pile had a trapdoor under it. He thought about the Synergex bug tracker, where a ticket titled *Login page sometimes logs you in as someone else* had been marked CRITICAL for four years, and had been reassigned so many times that its comment thread had outlasted two marriages.

Urgent, he was beginning to understand, was not a measure of time. It was a measure of volume.

---

He asked the skill.

He had never actually *asked* before. The skill had always just appeared, unbidden, the way a smoke alarm appears in your life. But this time he stood in front of the Registrar, looked at the crypt notice, and thought, as clearly as he could: *How long will it take to clear the Sunken Crypt?*

The answer came at once.

**Estimated time to clear the Sunken Crypt: 19 days.**

Then, before he could even feel relieved, a second line slid in beneath it, unasked.

**Estimated time until the crypt can be entered: 7 years, 11 months, 6 days.**

Mike stared at it.

*That,* he thought, *is what we call a dependency.*

---

He took the problem down the trapdoor. Durgan was asleep in his chair with a pamphlet over his face. Pip was awake and at his desk, as if he had never left it, which, Mike would later learn, he generally hadn't.

"The Sunken Crypt," said Pip, when Mike explained. His eyes went bright and greedy behind the spectacles. "Oh, *yes.* Footnote: it's under the river. Footnote: not near the river, *under* it. The old Varrenhold dukes built their family tomb on a dry riverbed during a drought, and then the drought ended, as droughts do, and nobody had thought about that, as nobody does. It only drains in a deep dry season."

"How often is that?"

"Once every nine or ten years. Footnote: the last one was two years ago. Footnote: nobody took the quest. Footnote: it was not on the top of the pile."

Together, they wrote the estimate.

It was, Mike thought, the best estimate he had ever written, in either life. It fit on one page. At the top, in large clear letters, was the number: *19 days.* Underneath were the assumptions. The crypt must be dry before work starts. The next dry season is expected in about eight years. The estimate covers a party of five, of reasonable competence, who do not drown. Then came the risks, ranked by likelihood. Pip had added footnotes, which Mike had gently negotiated down from thirty-one to six.

When they were done, Durgan lifted the pamphlet off his face, took the page, and read it with his small, sharp eyes.

"Good work," he said. He handed it back. "Nobody's going to read past the number."

---

Mike was standing outside Brannoc's office door at dawn, exactly as ordered.

The door was shut. Behind it, Brannoc was talking loudly to someone about a boar hunt.

Mike waited.

At midmorning, Priscilla passed with an armful of notices and said, "Oh, he'll be with you *any* minute," with total confidence.

At noon, a servant went in carrying a roast chicken.

At two, the servant came out carrying the chicken bones.

**Estimated time until the Guildmaster sees you: 9 hours, 40 minutes.**

Mike found a stool, sat on it, and used the time to recopy the Registrar guide in a fairer hand. At four o'clock, he started on page two.

At sundown, the office door opened, and Brannoc boomed, "CLERK! Why didn't you come in this morning?"

Mike decided that this was not a question that wanted an answer.

---

Brannoc's office looked exactly as it had two days before: weapons on the walls, the one-eyed dragon head, a strong smell of chicken. There was a visitor now, standing very upright beside the fireplace in a black coat with gold buttons. He had the narrow, patient face of a man who had spent many years waiting for other people to finish their dinners.

Mike knew him at once.

It was the steward. *Lord Ashcombe's* steward, from the Drowned Goose. The one who had come at midnight for pheasant and pie and lamb with mint, twice a night, until Hilde gave up and sold him the tavern.

The steward looked at Mike. A flicker crossed his face, the faint discomfort of a man who has seen a child somewhere before, scrubbing pots, but cannot imagine where.

"The Duke of Varrenhold's steward," Brannoc said, waving at him with a chicken leg he had apparently been saving. "Very important man."

"I thought you worked for Lord Ashcombe," Mike said, before he could stop himself.

The steward inclined his head a quarter inch. "I made a lateral move," he said. "His Grace's midnight demands pay better."

"Right!" Brannoc dropped into his chair and held out a huge hand. "The crypt. Let's have it."

Mike gave him the estimate.

Brannoc held the page at arm's length, squinted at the top, and read exactly one line. Mike watched his eyes. They did not move down. Not even an inch.

"*Nineteen?*" Brannoc said. "Nineteen days? That's too long."

"It's how long it takes, sir."

"Fourteen."

"Sir, that's not how—"

"Fourteen days. Two weeks. That's a good, solid number. People like two weeks."

"Sir, there's also the river. The crypt's under it. It can't be entered until the dry season, and that's not for about eight—"

"Water's a detail." Brannoc waved it away with the chicken leg. "Every dungeon has water. What's the best case?"

**Estimated time: 19 days.**

"Nineteen days," said Mike.

"That's the *best* case?"

"That's the case."

Brannoc narrowed his eyes. "You've padded it."

**Estimated time: 19 days.**

"I haven't, sir."

"Everybody pads. I used to pad. When I was hunting the Wyrm of Kessel Ridge, I told the old Guildmaster *six* weeks, and I did it in three." He leaned back, very satisfied. "Then I had three weeks off. Best holiday of my life."

"That's..." Mike paused. "That's actually a very good argument against what you're—"

"What if they worked weekends?"

**Estimated time: 19 days.**

"Sir, the nineteen days already includes—"

"What if we sent *more* adventurers? Twice as many! Half the time!"

**Estimated time: 19 days.**

**Estimated time with twice as many adventurers: 23 days.**

Mike opened his mouth to explain, and found that he did not have the strength. He had explained this to three different CEOs. He had once explained it with a pizza, with oven diagrams. It had not helped.

The door opened, and Priscilla leaned in with a beaming smile. "Are we talking about the crypt? Ooh! Can we say *ten?*"

"We are not saying ten," said Brannoc, with dignity. "We are saying fourteen. Ten would be ridiculous." He turned back to Mike. "Just put fourteen, lad, and we'll see how it goes."

Mike looked at the steward. The steward was examining a sword on the wall with the expression of a man who had attended this exact meeting in several different buildings.

**Estimated time: 19 days.**

Brannoc drummed his fingers on the desk. Then he leaned forward, lowered his voice to something he clearly believed was reasonable and fatherly, and said: "All right. Let's be grownups about this. What would it *take* to get it to fourteen?"

The skill considered the question. Then it answered, and for the first time Mike could have sworn its glowing letters had a tone.

**Estimated time if the Guildmaster stops asking: 19 days.**

**Estimated time if he keeps asking: 19 days, plus this meeting.**

---

They went with fourteen.

The guild's contract notary arrived a quarter of an hour later. Her name was Hesper Vane, and she was a tall, thin witch in a gray dress, with gray hair piled on her head and held in place by two quills. She carried a leather satchel, a silver pin, and an air of boredom so complete it seemed to lower the temperature of the room.

She unrolled a sheet of heavy vellum on Brannoc's desk and began to write. The ink was a deep, rusty red. It glistened.

"Blood contract," she said, to nobody in particular. "Binding on the guild and its officers. The Sunken Crypt of Varrenhold, cleared and the Signet recovered, within fourteen days *from commencement of work*." She glanced up at Mike, and for one moment something like sympathy crossed her face. "I added the commencement clause. Saves on lawsuits."

"Excellent!" Brannoc took the silver pin, pricked his thumb with a flourish, and pressed it to the page. The red ink glowed briefly, like a coal someone had breathed on.

Hesper blotted the signature. "Fourteen days," she murmured, writing a line in a small ledger of her own. Behind her, in the open satchel, Mike could see more ledgers: a whole row of them, labeled by year. "They always say fourteen."

"Do they?" said Mike.

"Always." She turned a page in the little ledger. It was full. Every line had a date and a number of days, and every line, Mike noticed, had a second date beside it, in a different ink, that was later than the first. "I've been keeping track. Forty years." She looked at him over the ledger. "Would you like to know how many came in on time?"

Mike looked at the ledger. "No," he said. "I think I know."

Hesper nodded, as if he had passed a test, and turned back to the contract. "And now," she said, "the estimator of record."

There was a pause.

Then, slowly, Brannoc, Priscilla, and the Duke's steward all turned to look at Mike.

"Me?" said Mike.

"You did the estimate," said Brannoc.

"You *changed* the estimate."

"And you agreed."

"I said nineteen days thirty-one times."

"That's agreeing," said Brannoc. "Eventually."

The steward cleared his throat delicately. "Forgive me," he said. "Is the boy of age to sign? He seems rather..." He searched for a polite word. "Compact."

Hesper Vane did not look up from her writing.

"Old enough to work," she said, "old enough to be blamed."

She held out the silver pin.

"It's a formality, lad," Brannoc said kindly. "Just a formality."

Mike looked at the pin. He looked at the contract, glowing faintly. He thought about the Synergex whiteboard, and the photo emailed to Hartwell with the subject line **COMMITTED!**, and the HR meeting about accountability gaps, and the coffee maker.

He was twelve years old. He had worked for this guild for three days. And there was nobody, in this room or this world, whose job it was to tell the Guildmaster no.

He pricked his thumb, and pressed it to the page.

As his skin touched the vellum, a line of glowing text that had been counting down for twelve years, since a beige waiting room in another life, slid gently into view one last time.

**Estimated time until you regret this: now.**

---

"*Commencement of work,*" Pip read aloud, by lantern light, an hour later. "*Being the first day on which the party of the guild shall set foot within the said crypt.* Footnote: that's the only good clause in it. Footnote: someone kind wrote that one."

"The notary," said Mike.

"Footnote: I'd like to meet her." Pip ran his finger down the copy Hesper had made for the guild's records. It was long, and almost entirely in the kind of language that hurts to look at. "*Fees, forfeitures, the jurisdiction of Varrenhold...* Ah." His finger stopped. His voice went quiet. "*Penalties.*"

"Read it," said Durgan.

"*Should the work exceed the term of fourteen days*," Pip read slowly, "*the Guild shall forfeit the full fee to the House of Varrenhold, and the Estimator...*" He paused. "*...shall answer for the delay.*"

The Basement was very quiet. Even the runes seemed to hum more softly.

"Answer how?" said Mike.

"It doesn't say." Pip turned the page over and back again. "Footnote: they never say. Footnote: that's what makes it frightening."

Mike looked at the copy. The red ink of the signatures was still faintly glossy. Without thinking, he touched Brannoc's thumbprint with one finger. It was warm. Not body-warm, but warm the way the reddish signature in the Registrar's oldest ledger had been warm, as if something somewhere was still keeping it alive.

He took his finger away quickly.

Durgan heaved himself out of his chair, which was itself a rare enough event that Pip stopped reading to watch. The dwarf crossed to the desk, cracked his knuckles, held one broad hand over the contract, and said a single word that sounded like a door closing.

The red ink flared like a struck match.

There was a sharp crackle and a smell of burning hair, and Durgan stepped back, patting briskly at the end of his braided beard, which was smoking.

"Contract's got wards on it," he said, when the beard was out. He examined the singed end with professional interest. "Good ones."

There was a pause.

"Better," he added, "than the ones on the building."

---

Mike walked home late, long after dark, across the old stone bridge over the river.

He stopped at the middle and leaned on the parapet. The water below was black and fast and very deep. Somewhere down there, under the current and the mud, a long-dead duke lay in a flooded tomb with a signet ring on his finger, and the ring had been URGENT for thirty-one years.

It would be urgent for eight more.

His father was waiting in the cobbler's doorway with a lamp, as he did every night now that Mike came home late. Hob's broad face split into a grin when he saw him.

"There's my clerk! How was the third day?"

Mike thought about it. "Da," he said, "have you ever promised somebody something you couldn't actually do?"

Hob laughed, a big, warm, cobbler's laugh. "Course I have. Every day. 'Your boots'll be ready Thursday.'" He ruffled Mike's hair. "A promise is just a hope with a date on it."

Mike stood very still on the doorstep. In two lifetimes, he thought, he had never heard anyone describe an estimate more accurately, and the man who'd done it made shoes.

"Oh!" Hob added, holding the door. "And that writing desk. Coming along. This weekend, for certain."

Inside, the lamp was warm and the stew smelled like something, roughly. Mike sat down at the table, took out a clean sheet of paper and his black ink, and began, at the top, a new list. *Things to learn before the river drops.*

It got long very quickly. The guild's ledgers. The Registrar's rules, all of them. Every quest and how long it had really taken. How the wards worked. How contracts worked. Which officers could be persuaded of things, and how. He had time, he thought. For once in either life, he had time.

The skill flickered in front of his eyes, with one last readout for the day.

**Estimated time until Brannoc remembers this contract: 7 years, 11 months, 5 days.**

Mike dipped his quill, and kept writing.
