# Chapter 2: Onboarding


The young woman at the counter wrapped up with the farmer by shaking his hand with both of hers and telling him the guild was "so excited about this partnership." The farmer left looking dazed and hopeful, the way people looked when they had been promised something enormous by someone very pleasant.

Then she turned her smile on Mike.

"Hello! Welcome to the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor, where every quest is our top priority! I'm Priscilla. Are you here to post a quest, take a quest, or complain about a quest?"

"I'm here about the clerk position."

"Oh, *wonderful*." Priscilla clasped her hands. "You're hired."

Mike blinked. "You haven't read my references."

"I'm sure they're lovely." She took the folded papers from his hand and set them on a stack without unfolding them. The stack was very tall. Several of the papers near the bottom looked like they had been waiting there since before Mike was born. "Can you start today?"

"I suppose so."

"Perfect! Technically you started yesterday. We were short a clerk, so I told the lumber guild their contracts would be filed by this morning." She leaned back and called across the hall, without lowering her smile. "GUILDMASTER! I've found a clerk!"

At the far end of the room, Guildmaster Brannoc was still standing on his table, now dictating something to a nervous adventurer who was writing it on the back of his own shield. He did not look over.

"HIRED!" he roared. "Tell him the Duke's escort needs filing by noon!"

The skill flickered in front of Mike's eyes.

**Estimated length of interview: 11 seconds.**

**Actual length of interview: 11 seconds.**

It was, Mike thought, the most efficient hiring process he had ever seen, in either world.

---

The clerk's desk was in an alcove behind the counter, half buried under paper. There were contracts, quest notices, receipts, complaints, a half-eaten loaf of bread that had gone hard as a brick, and one dried quill. In the middle of the desk, held down by an inkpot, was a single sheet of paper.

"That's from Edwin," Priscilla said. "Your predecessor. He left last week."

"Why did he leave?"

Priscilla's smile did not move, but something behind it did. "Personal growth."

Mike picked up the paper. It was written in a hurried hand, the ink blotched in places as if Edwin had been writing while somebody shouted at him.

> *To whoever's next:*
>
> *Don't feed the Registrar after dusk.*
>
> *Ignore the URGENT pile.*
>
> *Good luck.*
>
> *— E.*

Mike turned it over. The back was blank.

"Is there anything else?" he asked. "A procedures book? A list of what goes where? Notes on the Registrar?"

Priscilla laughed, a bright, musical laugh. "Oh, Edwin never wrote anything down. He just *knew*."

Over the next three hours, Mike learned that Edwin had known a great many things. He had known which contracts went in the blue drawer and which went in the other blue drawer. He had known that the lumber guild's paperwork had to be stamped twice, and that the Duke's could never be stamped at all, for reasons involving an old feud and a seal that may or may not have been cursed. He had known how to get the Registrar to accept a form on a damp day.

Mike learned all of this because every time he asked anyone a question, they gave him the same answer.

"Edwin knew."

"Ask Edwin."

"Edwin had a system."

The worst offender was the other clerk, a stooped, gray man named Osric who sat behind a stack of paper taller than he was. Mike never once saw his face. Every question he put to the stack produced the same two words, in the same flat voice: "Edwin knew."

"Where is Edwin now?" Mike finally asked a passing adventurer, a tired-looking woman with an arm in a sling.

She thought about it. "Fishing, I heard. Somewhere very far away. He said he wanted to be somewhere nobody could ask him anything."

Mike looked down at the scrawled page in his hand: one sheet of paper, holding everything an entire organization knew about its own operations. He had seen this before. At Synergex, it had been a man named Gary, who had built the billing system in 2009, never documented any of it, and retired to a sailboat. For two years afterward, every invoice the company sent had been a small act of archaeology.

He folded Edwin's note carefully and put it in his pocket. Something told him he would need it.

---

The Registrar lived in the back room.

Priscilla showed him the door, then stepped well back from it. "It processes every quest," she said. "Logs it, stamps it, files it, assigns the bounty. Nothing is official until the Registrar says so."

"How does it work?"

"Edwin knew."

Mike opened the door.

The room beyond was round and high-ceilinged, lit by a single dusty window. In the center sat a golem the size of a farm cart, built from stone blocks and iron bands and what looked like several different centuries of repairs. One arm ended in an enormous brass stamp. The other held a quill as long as a broom. In front of it, on a lectern built for something much larger than a human, lay a ledger so thick it had its own support beam.

The golem's eyes, two pale blue lights, swiveled toward Mike with a grinding sound.

"STATE. YOUR. BUSINESS."

Mike, still holding the stack of lumber guild contracts, stepped forward. "Um. Filing."

The Registrar did not move.

He tried holding out the contracts. Nothing. He tried saying "filing" more clearly. Nothing. He tried setting them on the lectern, and the golem's stamp arm swung around with a whoosh and swept them gently onto the floor.

He went back out to the counter.

"It won't take them."

Priscilla winced. "Did you knock?"

"I opened the door."

"No, you have to *knock*. Three times. On the lectern, not the door. And you have to call it 'Esteemed Registrar.' And" — she peered at his stack — "oh, those are in blue ink. It doesn't take blue ink."

"Why not?"

She shrugged. "It never has."

It took Mike most of the afternoon, but by trial and error and a great deal of asking around, he assembled a partial list of the Registrar's rules. Black ink only. Knock three times, on the lectern. Address it as "Esteemed Registrar." Never stand on its left side. Never file anything involving the number seven before noon. If it starts humming, step back and wait, and under no circumstances try to help.

Nobody could tell him why any of these were true. They had simply always been true. Some of them, Mike suspected, were real requirements. Some had probably been workarounds for a problem that no longer existed. And at least one was almost certainly superstition that someone had once started as a joke. There was no way to know which was which, and no one dared find out.

He went back in, recopied the contracts in black ink, knocked three times on the lectern, and said, "Esteemed Registrar. Filing, please."

The blue eyes swiveled. The quill scratched. The great brass stamp came down with a *BOOM* that rattled the window, and the contracts vanished into the ledger as if they had always been there.

"FILED," said the Registrar.

"Edwin used to do this," Mike said, mostly to himself.

At the name, the Registrar's blue eyes dimmed, just for a moment, and the great stamp arm sagged half an inch. Then the lights steadied, and it sat as still as stone, as if nothing had happened.

Mike stood there for a moment, smiling despite himself.

He knew this thing. Not this exact thing, but its cousin. Every company he had ever worked for had one somewhere in the basement: some ancient, groaning system that everyone complained about and nobody could replace, because it held decades of knowledge in its strange old bones. Ugly, cranky, undocumented, and still running, still doing the work, long after the people who built it were gone.

It was the guild's mainframe. And Mike, to his own surprise, found that he liked it.

He patted the edge of the lectern, carefully, on the right side.

"Good job," he told it.

The Registrar's eyes dimmed and brightened once, slowly, which Mike decided to interpret as a thank you. He turned to go, and as he did, he noticed that the ledger had fallen open to an older section. The paper there was brittle and brown. One page near the edge was signed in a strange reddish ink, a long, looping signature he couldn't read.

The ink, he would have sworn, was still faintly warm.

He decided that was a problem for another day.

---

The quest board stood just outside the main doors, and at the end of the afternoon Priscilla sent him out to "tidy it up a bit."

Mike stopped on the steps and stared.

It was not so much a board as a geological formation. Notices had been pinned over other notices, which had been pinned over still older notices, until the whole thing bulged outward like a paper mushroom. And every single one of them, without exception, was stamped in large red letters: **URGENT**.

*URGENT: Wolves harassing sheep, east pasture.*

*URGENT: Retrieve heirloom ring from well.*

*URGENT: Mysterious noise in cellar (probably nothing).*

*URGENT: Clear the Sunken Crypt.* That one was at the very bottom, so old that its paper had gone the color of weak tea and its edges were crumbling.

One notice near the top was different from the rest. It had a heavy gold seal, and instead of URGENT it read: *VARRENHOLD — ALWAYS FIRST.*

Mike had seen this before too. At Synergex, the ticket system had allowed five priority levels. Within six months, every ticket had been set to the highest one, because anything lower never got done. Then someone in management had added a sixth level, called "Critical Plus." Within a month, everything was Critical Plus.

The skill, uninvited as always, did the math.

**Estimated time to clear all posted quests with current staff: 4 years, 7 months, 12 days.**

While he was still reading the numbers, Priscilla came out of the hall with three new notices and pinned them on top, beaming.

**Revised estimate: 4 years, 7 months, 26 days.**

"Isn't it wonderful?" she said. "Business is booming!"

---

He was back at his desk, trying to sort the lumber guild's paperwork into something like an order, when the doors banged open hard enough to bounce off the wall.

A tall young man strode in. He had golden hair, a jaw like a cliff edge, and a cloak that billowed even though there was no wind indoors. A sword the size of a door hung across his back.

The hall erupted in cheers.

"GARETH!"

"Gareth the Bold!"

Gareth raised a gauntleted fist in salute, marched straight to the quest board outside, tore down the top three notices without looking at them, and marched back in.

"I'll take these!" he declared, to more cheering. Then he swept out again, cloak billowing, and was gone.

From the far end of the hall, Brannoc bellowed from his table, "THAT'S the kind of initiative I like to see!"

**Estimated time Gareth will spend on this paperwork: 0 seconds. Estimated time you will: 2 hours.**

Mike looked at the doorway. Then he looked at the Registrar's door. Then he looked at the three empty spaces on the board.

Gareth had not signed for the quests. He had not registered them with the Registrar. He had not told anyone which quests they were. Three people somewhere in Brindlemoor were now waiting for help that, as far as the guild's records were concerned, nobody was bringing. And if any other adventurer took those quests, there would be two parties fighting the same wolves while a ring sat at the bottom of a well forever.

It took Mike two hours to figure out which notices were missing, recopy them from the drafts in Priscilla's stack, register them under Gareth's name, and convince the Registrar to accept a quest filing without its taker present (knock four times, it turned out, and say "on behalf of").

When he finished, the guild hall was still buzzing about Gareth. How brave he was. How fast. How he never even hesitated.

Nobody mentioned the paperwork. Nobody ever would.

---

By closing time, Mike had a list.

He had written it on the back of a rejected blue-ink contract, in his best scribe's hand, and he had been thinking about it all afternoon. It was not a long list. It was not a radical list. It was, he thought, about as sensible as a list could possibly be.

He found Brannoc in his office, a cluttered room hung with old weapons and a stuffed dragon head that was missing one eye. The Guildmaster was eating a roast chicken leg and reading a letter from the Duke of Varrenhold, scowling.

"Guildmaster? Do you have a moment?"

Brannoc looked up. He seemed surprised to find someone so short in his office. "You're the new clerk."

"Yes, sir. Mikel. I had two ideas I wanted to suggest."

"Ideas." Brannoc said it the way some people said *rats*. But he waved the chicken leg. "Go on."

"The first is the Registrar. Nobody knows how it works anymore. Edwin knew, but Edwin's gone. I'd like to start writing down its rules, the ink, the knocking, all of it, in a book that stays with the guild. So the next clerk doesn't have to learn it all by guessing."

Brannoc chewed.

"The second is the quest board. Everything's marked URGENT, so we can't tell what actually is. If we put all the quests in one list, ranked by what matters most, the adventurers could work from the top down, and we'd know what's being done and what isn't."

Brannoc swallowed. For a moment, Mike thought he might actually be considering it.

Then the Guildmaster threw back his head and laughed. It was a huge laugh, the laugh of a man who had once killed a dragon and had never had to fill out a form about it.

"Write things DOWN? Boy, we haven't got time to write things down. We're far too busy!" He wiped his eyes. "A *ranked list*. Ha! Everything on that board is important, lad. That's why it's on the board."

"But if everything is—"

"Tell you what. Since you're so interested in the board, you can take the URGENT pile. All of it. Sort it out by the end of the week."

**Estimated time to sort the URGENT pile: 3 weeks, 4 days.**

"Yes, sir," Mike said.

Brannoc had already gone back to the Duke's letter. Mike let himself out.

He stood in the empty hall for a while. Then he sat down at his desk, took out a fresh sheet of paper, dipped his quill in black ink, and at the top, in neat letters, wrote:

*The Registrar: A Guide. Page 1.*

Under it, he copied Edwin's note, word for word. It seemed only fair to give him credit.

---

It was nearly dark when he finished. The other clerks had gone. Priscilla had gone, after cheerfully promising the night watchman's cousin a full escort to the coast, which was eleven days away and had pirates. The guild hall was quiet and smelled of candle wax and old paper.

Mike was locking up the front doors when he noticed the doorframe.

He had walked past it a dozen times that day without really seeing it, but in the low evening light it caught his eye. All around the frame, faint and worn almost smooth, someone long ago had carved a line of runes. They ran up one side, over the top, and down the other, and disappeared into the floor.

He ran his fingers over them. The stone was cold.

"Do you know what these are for?" he asked the night watchman.

The watchman squinted at the doorframe, then at Mike. "Decoration, I always figured."

Mike was not so sure. In his experience, nobody carved anything that carefully for decoration.

He turned the key in the lock. And as he did, from somewhere beneath his feet, beneath the floor of the guild hall itself, he heard a voice.

It was muffled, distant, and very precise. It seemed to be reading aloud.

"...*see also*, footnote fourteen, subsection b, which, *as I have said repeatedly*..."

Then a second voice, deeper, gruff, and deeply unimpressed.

"Have you tried dispelling it and casting it again?"

Mike stood very still, the key in his hand.

The skill chimed, almost cheerfully.

**Estimated time until you find out what's down there: tomorrow, 9:14 a.m.**
