# Chapter 21: Two Weeks' Notice

The letter took him until dawn.

It was not a long letter. Mike had written longer letters to the lumber guild about a single misfiled invoice. But every time he got to the end of a sentence, he stopped, and looked at it, and crossed it out, and started again, because it turned out there was no polite way to say *I'm leaving* to a building that was going to fall down in four days.

In the end, he wrote it the way he had been taught to write it, a lifetime ago, by an HR department in Raleigh, North Carolina, which had given every new hire a laminated card titled *Leaving Synergex the Right Way!* He had never used the card. He had died first. But he still remembered it.

> *Guildmaster Brannoc,*
>
> *Please accept this letter as formal notice of my resignation from the position of Senior Clerk. In keeping with professional practice, I will continue in my role for two weeks from today, to ensure an orderly transition.*
>
> *Thank you for the opportunity.*
>
> *Respectfully,*
>
> *Mike Dobbins*

He read it over. It was clean, short, and polite. It had no adjectives in it, which the laminated card had been very firm about.

Then, out of pure habit, he asked the skill.

**Estimated time until your notice period ends: 14 days.**

He looked up. Across the dark hall, on the front counter, Malphus's hourglass sat in its little pool of lamplight. Almost half the sand had run through. Four days left.

Mike looked back at the letter.

In fourteen days, his notice would be over, and he would hand in his keys, and shake hands, and leave. In four days, the guild would collapse. That left ten days in the middle, during which he would be, as far as he could tell, contractually obligated to work out his notice in a building that no longer existed, for an employer that was a hole in the ground, filing paperwork with a demon.

He picked up the pen. He crossed out *two weeks* and wrote *four days* above it.

He read it again.

*I will continue in my role for four days.*

It read like a threat. It read like something you'd find pinned to a door with a knife.

He crossed out *four days* and wrote *two weeks* again. Then he looked at the mess of crossings-out, sighed, took a fresh sheet, and copied the whole thing out a second time, neatly, with the two weeks in it. He would just have to work out the other ten days somehow. He'd figure it out. He always did.

The laminated card would have been proud.

---

Before he handed it in, he tried one more time.

He didn't plan to. He went up the stairs to Brannoc's office with the letter folded in his pocket, fully intending to put it on the desk and go. But on the landing he stopped, and took out a second sheet of paper, and wrote on it, in large, plain, ordinary letters, one single line. Then he knocked.

Brannoc was at his desk, behind the purple curtain, eating a pastry. Rupert was in the visitor's chair with his feet on a footstool he had brought from home.

"Mike!" said Brannoc. "Great timing. Can I get an estimate on the—"

Mike put the sheet of paper on the desk.

> *ESTIMATED TIME UNTIL THE GUILD COLLAPSES: 4 DAYS.*

Brannoc looked at it. He chewed slowly. He looked at it some more.

"Hm," he said. "That's just an estimate, though."

"I've given you a lot of estimates," said Mike. "Eight years of them. I said the crypt would take nineteen days. It took nineteen days."

"Exactly," said Brannoc, nodding. "And that was a *failure*."

There was a pause, during which Mike felt something inside him very quietly give up, like a wagon axle that had been creaking for years and had finally just stopped making the noise.

"I think," said Rupert thoughtfully, from the footstool, "the issue here is really the *framing*. 'Collapse' is a very negative word. People hear 'collapse,' and they panic." He made a little frame with his fingers and looked at Mike through it. "What if we called it a *four-day runway*?"

"A runway," said Brannoc, brightening. "I like that. That sounds like we're going somewhere."

"Off the end of it," said Mike.

"Let's not do *negativity*, Mike." Brannoc picked up his quill and pulled his calendar toward him. "Look. I hear you. It's important. Let's put some time on the calendar to really dig into it." He ran his finger down the page. "Today's no good. Tomorrow's the float. Then the banners. Then the—" He frowned. "There's a lot on." He turned the page. "Day eight. How's day eight? First thing."

Mike didn't need to ask. The skill told him anyway, because by now it had a sense of occasion.

**Estimated time until this meeting is relevant: never.**

"Day eight's fine," said Mike.

He took the letter out of his pocket and put it on the desk, on top of the warning.

Brannoc glanced at it. He didn't open it. He didn't read it. He picked up his quill and signed the bottom of it with the same large, confident flourish he used for everything, the same flourish he had once used, eight years ago, on a blood contract for a crypt, and then he put it on the pile with the other things he had signed that week, and set his empty mug on top to keep it from blowing away.

"Great," said Brannoc. "Thanks, Mike. Day eight."

---

It turned out that the guild could not process a demon, a barrier, a debt, or a Duke. But it could process a resignation. It processed it beautifully.

By nine o'clock, Rupert had heard, because Rupert had read the letter, which Brannoc had not. By nine fifteen, Tolliver had invented Form R-1. By nine thirty, Rupert was sitting across from Mike at the little table by the coat hooks, with the form in front of him and a fresh quill, conducting an exit interview.

"This is a safe space," said Rupert warmly. "I want you to feel totally comfortable. So." He read from the form. "Question one. On a scale of one to five, how likely are you to recommend the guild to a friend?"

Mike thought about all of his friends. Most of them worked in the Basement.

"I'd like to recommend it to someone I don't like," he said.

Rupert nodded, and wrote down *3*. "Question two. Please rate the Chief Ink Officer's leadership, where five is highest."

He turned the form around so Mike could see it. The five was already circled. It had been circled in ink, firmly, and then gone over a second time, to be sure.

"That's already filled in," said Mike.

"We pre-populated some fields," said Rupert, "to save you time. Question three." He looked up, and his face became serious and kind. "What could *you* have done better?"

Mike thought about it honestly, which was a mistake, because the honest answer was *leave eight years ago*.

"I could have used Form E-1," he said.

"*Thank* you," said Rupert, with real feeling, and wrote it down.

At ten o'clock came the counteroffer.

Rupert had prepared it on a single sheet of good paper, and he presented it with both hands, the way people present a check in a picture.

"Mike," he said. "We don't want to lose you. You're a huge part of the team. So I've put together a *retention package*." He cleared his throat. "Number one. Your old desk back."

Mike blinked.

"Not by the window," added Rupert quickly. "That's the Champions' area now. But very near it. You'd be able to see the window. Number two." He paused for effect. "A promotion. From white belt to *yellow* belt."

He produced a yellow belt from his pocket and laid it on the table with great ceremony.

"And number three. A new title." He smiled. "*Senior* Estimate Owner."

Mike looked at the yellow belt for a while. It was a very nice yellow. It was the yellow Gareth wore on his head.

"No, thank you," he said.

Rupert stared at him. For just a moment, his warm, confident expression flickered, and underneath it was something genuinely bewildered and a little hurt, like a man who has offered someone a very generous piece of cake and been told they are allergic to cake.

"It's a *yellow* belt, Mike," he said.

"I know."

Rupert looked at the belt. He looked at Mike. He slowly picked the belt back up and put it in his pocket, and he did not say anything for almost a minute, which was the longest Mike had ever known him to go.

At eleven o'clock came the knowledge transfer.

Rupert had decided that, since Mike was leaving, the Champions should learn everything Mike did, so that there would be *continuity*. Mike thought this was the first sensible thing Rupert had ever said, so he brought them everything. He brought them the Registrar guide, all three hundred and twelve pages. He brought them the guide to the barrier, the lumber guild file, the Duke's file, the bell schedule, the rules for the gong, the folder titled *THINGS THE OMNIFILE DOES AT NIGHT*, and the index he had made to all of it, which was itself fourteen pages long.

He put it all on the Champions' desk, by the window, in the good light. It made a stack nearly as tall as Dex.

Dex looked up at it.

"Is there a summary?" he asked.

"That *is* the summary," said Mike. "The index is the summary of the summary."

"Great," said Tolliver. "We'll do our own summary."

The three of them retired to the window with a fresh sheet of paper. There was a great deal of murmuring and pointing, and at one stage Bramble played a short, thoughtful chord on his lute. After twenty minutes, Tolliver came back and handed Mike the summary, so that he could check it for accuracy.

It was a single page. In the middle of the page, in Tolliver's large, neat letters, it said:

> *ASK MIKE.*

Mike looked at it for a long time.

"That's going to be a problem," he said, "in about two weeks."

"We've identified it as a risk," said Tolliver, and went back to the window.

And then, while the Champions were taking a break to admire their plaque, something happened that Mike almost missed.

A farmer came to the counter with a contract for a fence-mending job, and Tolliver was busy, and Dex was busy, and Bramble was tuning his lute. So Tam, the young clerk, the one who had once asked about the blue drawers, got up from his little desk at the end of the row, took the contract, and walked it back to the round room.

Mike followed, quietly, and watched from the doorway.

Tam knocked on the lectern, three times. He used black ink. He stood on the right side. He said "Esteemed Registrar," clearly, without his voice shaking. The old golem's blue eyes brightened, and its quill arm moved, and the contract was filed. Then Tam did two more, because there were two more in the tray, and he did them both exactly right, including the one with a seven in it, which he set aside to file after noon.

Then he went back to his desk and sat down and got on with his work, and did not seem to notice that anyone had been watching.

Mike stood in the doorway.

*Mike knows*, people had been saying, for years. And then, after the archmage, he had sat down next to a thirteen-year-old and started teaching, so that one day it wouldn't have to be Mike who knew. And it had worked. It had actually worked. Tam knew. Pip knew. Velka had borrowed the barrier guide and corrected it in red ink. If Mike walked out the front door tomorrow, the guild would not fall down because Mike was gone.

It would fall down anyway, on the same day. For different reasons. Because of everything else.

*Seven days, if I stay. Seven days, if I leave.*

He had thought, last night, that it meant he didn't matter. Now, watching Tam file the seven-contract into the after-noon tray, he thought that maybe it meant something more complicated than that. But he was too tired to work out what.

That afternoon, a junior clerk came halfway down the Basement ladder, threw a scroll tied with a red ribbon, and fled.

Mike happened to be down there, returning Pip's ruler. He watched Durgan pick it up, open the drawer marked *MIKE*, and lay it inside, next to the others.

"Five," said Durgan.

Mike untied this one. He wasn't sure why. It was short.

> *NOTICE OF CONCERN*
>
> *Senior Clerk M. Dobbins is hereby noted for contributing to ATTRITION, which is a key risk to guild stability.*
>
> *— Office of the C.I.O.*

"I'm the attrition," said Mike.

"Mm," said Durgan.

"They're blaming me for leaving."

Durgan looked at him for a long moment, from under his eyebrows. Then he went back to the pamphlet he was reading, which was, Mike noticed, the guide to the seal, and was covered in fresh chisel dust.

He didn't say anything else. But he didn't close the drawer, either.

---

At two o'clock, Priscilla came down the hall clapping her hands.

"Everyone! Everyone!" she cried. "Mike is *leaving* us! And we are going to give him a *proper* send-off! Party in the main hall at five!"

Mike, behind Osric's stack, closed his eyes. He didn't have to ask. The skill told him.

**Estimated time to organize a farewell party: 3 hours.**

It took exactly three hours.

Mike watched it happen, from behind the stack, with a sense of growing disbelief. He had never seen anything like it. Not in this life, and not in the last one either.

Priscilla made a list. On the list were the things that needed doing. Beside each thing she wrote a name, and only one name, and beside the name she wrote a time. The kitchens were told to make a cookie platter by four, and they did. Two adventurers were told to hang a banner by four thirty, and they did. Somebody was sent for punch. Somebody else was sent to borrow glasses from the Silver Pheasant. Nobody was asked to do two things at once. Nobody added a float. When Gareth wandered by and said the party should have a sword fight, Priscilla said, very pleasantly, "Not this time, Gareth," and he went away, and nobody seemed to find this remarkable.

At five o'clock, exactly, the doors of the main hall opened, and there it was.

There was a long table with a white cloth. There was a large platter of cookies, and a bowl of punch, and two dozen borrowed glasses. Across the back wall hung a banner, painted in bright, cheerful, slightly drippy letters: *GOOD LUCK, MIKE!* Under it, smaller, someone had added: *(WE'LL MISS YOU!)*

And there was a card.

It was a big card, folded from a sheet of the guild's best paper, and it had been passed around all afternoon, and nearly everyone had signed it. Priscilla presented it to him with a little curtsy. Mike opened it.

> *Good luck at your new guild!! — Gareth the Bold*
>
> *Let's do lunch!!! xo P*
>
> *Best wishes in your future endeavors. — R. Spindlewick, C.I.O. (on behalf of the Champions)*
>
> *Thank you for teaching me. — Tam*
>
> *Your departure has been noted in the attached footnote.¹ — Pip*
>
> *¹ I do not want you to go.*
>
> *Five. — D.*

Behind his stack, Osric had not signed the card. But when Mike went past it on his way to the punch, there was a small folded note sticking out of the side of the stack at about knee height. It said, in thin, shaky handwriting: *Edwin left too. It was worse after.*

Brannoc came down at a quarter past five, still holding his empty mug, and stood up on a chair to give a speech.

"Mike," he said, in his barn-collapsing voice, and the hall went quiet. "Mike has been with us for eight years. Eight years of—" He paused, and looked out over the room, and seemed to be searching for the right word. "—*initiative*."

Everyone clapped. Mike stood by the cookies and listened to it.

And somewhere in the middle of the clapping, he looked around the hall, at the banner and the table and the platter and the punch, all of it finished, all of it on time, all of it exactly what had been promised, and he understood something that he had not understood in eight years.

The guild could plan.

It could plan perfectly well. It could make a list, and give each thing one owner, and say no to Gareth, and hit a deadline to the minute. He had just watched it do all of that in one afternoon, with no meetings, no gong, and no chart.

It just never, ever planned anything that mattered.

He ate a cookie. It was a very good cookie. It was the best thing the guild had delivered in years.

---

He left a little after six, with a wooden box of his things under his arm: his good pen, his spare pens, his ruler, the card, and his mug, which was not empty. He walked out the front door, down the steps, past the board with the barrier notice still nailed to it, and into the square.

He did not really decide where he was going. His feet decided. They took him across the square, and down past the cooper's, and left at the well, and down a narrow street he had not walked down in nine years, until he heard the sound of it before he saw it: a hammer on steel, slow and even, ringing like a bell that knew exactly when it wanted to ring.

The forge on Anvil Lane looked almost exactly the same. The same soot-black doorway. The same orange glow inside. The same smell of hot iron and coal smoke and leather. Brunhild Ashgrove stood at the anvil with her sleeves rolled up, and her forearms were still like hams, and her hair had gone gray at the sides, and she looked up when his shadow fell across the doorway.

She looked at him. She looked at the box under his arm. She did not ask a single question.

She jerked her head at the bellows.

Mike put the box down by the wall, rolled up his sleeves, and took hold of the handles. They fit his hands differently now. He was taller. But the rhythm was the same, and his arms remembered it before he did: in, and out, and in, and out, and the coals flaring white with every breath.

They worked for a while without saying anything. It was the most peaceful he had felt in a week.

Then a shadow fell across the doorway, and a voice said, "Brunhild, my *dear*!"

Mike looked up.

It was Fennick. He was older, and a little rounder, but he had the same velvet hat, or one exactly like it, and the same bright, beaming confidence of a man who had already spent money that did not belong to him.

"Wonderful news!" said Fennick. "I've secured you an order! The Margrave of Thelling, coming in for the harvest festival, wants twenty swords for his honor guard. Fine steel, gold inlay, the works. Ready by Friday!"

Mike's arms stopped on the bellows.

He knew what came next. He had seen it when he was eleven. The ten days without sleep, the steel without folding, the drunk cousin, the sack of broken blades. He waited for the skill to tell him how long until the swords broke.

Brunhild set down her hammer. She looked at the forge. She looked at the little slate on the wall beside it, where she kept, Mike now saw, a neat list of every job she had promised, and when. She looked back at Fennick.

"No," she said.

Fennick's smile stayed exactly where it was, the way a smile does when the face behind it hasn't caught up yet.

"I'm sorry?"

"No," said Brunhild. "I make one sword a week. I've got four booked. I can do you twenty by the spring."

"But he's already paid me the deposit," said Fennick, "so—"

"Then you'd better give it back," said Brunhild, and picked up her hammer again.

Fennick stood in the doorway for a moment longer, opening and closing his mouth. Then he turned around and went away down Anvil Lane, and the velvet hat bobbed out of sight around the corner.

Mike stared at Brunhild.

"You can say that, you know," she said, without looking up. "It's allowed." She turned the blade over on the anvil. "Took me a while to learn it. After the Baron."

She worked for a while. Mike pumped. Then she said, as if she were continuing a conversation they had been having for nine years:

"Fennick never listened. The Baron never listened. Most of them don't. You asked me once if it ever gets better, and I told you it does, sometimes, when someone in charge listens to the person who does the work." She glanced at him sideways. "I wrote you a reference, when you went to the guild. You remember what I put?"

"*He listens*," said Mike.

"Two words." She brought the hammer down. "Did you think I meant you'd be good at taking orders?"

Mike's arms stopped on the bellows again.

He had, in fact, thought exactly that. For eight years, he had thought exactly that. He had thought she meant he was a good worker, a good clerk, a good pair of hands, the kind of person who heard what he was told and did it, and kept his head down, and didn't make trouble. He had been proud of it. He had been, in his quiet way, living up to it.

"Pump," said Brunhild.

He pumped.

"I didn't write it for whoever hired you," she said. "I wrote it for whoever you'd end up being." She turned the blade. "Somebody's got to be the one in charge who listens. Doesn't have to be the one with the biggest desk."

They didn't say anything for a long time after that.

Mike asked the skill how long the blade would take. He wasn't sure why. Maybe he just wanted to hear it say something true about something that wasn't on fire.

**Estimated time to finish this blade: 2 hours.**

It took exactly two hours. Not because the skill made it so, but because Brunhild had known, when she started, exactly how long it would take, and she had not let anyone tell her otherwise.

---

Velka found him at dusk.

He heard her before he saw her, which was usual with Velka, because she walked like she was late for something even when she wasn't. She came down Anvil Lane in her mage's coat, with soot on one sleeve, and the plain brown hat pulled low. She stopped in the doorway of the forge and looked at him, at the bellows, at the box of his things by the wall.

"Pip footnoted it," she said.

"Of course he did."

"Footnote one said you were leaving. Footnote two said where Tanner's Row was. Footnote three said he didn't think you'd go there first." She came in and sat down on the upturned bucket by the wall, the one he had sat on when he was eleven. "Footnote four said you'd go somewhere with bellows."

Brunhild looked at Velka, and at Mike, and at Velka again. Then she put down her hammer and announced that she was going to see a man about some coal, and left, which Mike was fairly sure was not a real errand.

Velka waited until she'd gone.

"So," she said. "Seven days."

"Four now."

"Pip said you asked the skill if it would collapse if you quit."

"It said seven. Same as if I stay." Mike let go of the bellows and sat down on the edge of the coal bin. "So either I don't matter, or I'm free to go. And I've spent all day trying to work out which, and I can't."

Velka didn't say anything for a while. She was frowning, but not at him. She was frowning the way she frowned at a spell draft on her tower wall, when it had worked fine on paper and blown out the windows in the field. Mike had seen that frown a great many times, at a great many horse ponds. It was the frown of someone who was about to find the bug.

"You tested a hat," she said.

"What?"

"For me. After the wolf." She tapped the brim. "You held it over a candle for ten minutes. You borrowed a dog from the stables and let it chew on it for an hour. You wrote it down." She looked at him. "You tested a *hat* more carefully than you tested this."

"I asked twice."

"You asked the same thing twice," said Velka. "That's not testing. That's just running it again and hoping." She leaned forward on the bucket. "You asked what happens if you leave. And you asked what happens if you stay and Brannoc's plan goes ahead, with the float and the parade and the moving the demon. Both of those are the same branch, Mike. Both of them are *Brannoc's plan*." She spread her hands. "You never tested the other branch. You never asked what happens if you stay, and you do it properly. Your way."

Mike opened his mouth to say that there wasn't a *his way*, that he was a clerk, that it wasn't his guild, that nobody would listen.

And then he heard Brunhild, an hour ago, saying *Did you think I meant you'd be good at taking orders?*

He closed his mouth.

He asked the skill.

*Estimated time until the guild collapses if we make a real plan?*

The skill paused.

It had never paused before. In twenty years, across two lives, it had never once paused. It had answered every question he had ever put to it, instantly, flatly, correctly, the way a sum answers. Now there was a gap, like a breath being held. Mike felt it, the way you feel a step that isn't there in the dark.

**Unknown.**

Mike stared at it.

It had said *unknown* only once before, ever, and that had been about whether Thessaly would ever explain anything, and he had assumed it was the skill's idea of a joke. This was not a joke. He could feel that it wasn't. It wasn't *unknown* because the answer was hidden. It was *unknown* because the answer hadn't happened yet. Because it depended on what they did.

"What did it say?" said Velka.

"Unknown."

Velka thought about that. Then she nodded, firmly, as if a test had come back clean.

"Unknown is better than four," she said.

It was. It really was. Mike sat on the coal bin and looked at the word, and for the first time in a week, the knot in his chest loosened a little.

Velka stood up and brushed the soot off her coat, which only moved it around. She looked down at him from under the brim of the hat.

"I've got a question too," she said. "How long until you come back?"

He didn't need the skill for that one.

He stood up and picked up his box.

---

It was very late by the time he got back to the guild. The hall was dark. The banner was still up: *GOOD LUCK, MIKE! (WE'LL MISS YOU!)* There were three cookies left on the platter, and somebody had put a cloth over them, which he thought was nice.

He went upstairs to Brannoc's office. The door was open. Brannoc had gone home.

The letter was still there. It was on the pile of things Brannoc had signed that week, under the empty mug, exactly where he had left it. Rupert had taken the copy for Form R-1 and then, it seemed, not been able to file it, because there was a note pinned to the corner in Tolliver's writing that said *R-1: NO FILING CODE YET. ESCALATE?*

Mike lifted the mug, took the letter, and put the mug back.

Then he went down to his desk behind Osric's stack, which nobody had cleared out yet, because nobody had been told to, and he lit the lamp and sat down.

There was no form for withdrawing a resignation. He knew every form in the building. There was a form for requesting a form. There was not a form for changing your mind, because nobody at the guild had ever thought that was something people did.

He took a fresh sheet of paper. He ruled a margin down the left side, one thumb's width. At the top, in clear, ordinary letters, he wrote:

> *FORM R-2: WITHDRAWAL OF RESIGNATION*

He filled it in. Name, date, reason. Under *reason*, he wrote: *Asked the wrong question.* He signed it and blotted it and filed it with the letter, in the right drawer, under the right code, which he made up.

E-1. E-2. R-2. That was three forms he'd invented in one week. He was starting to think that might be the whole job.

Then he opened his desk drawer and took out Wren Halloway's offer.

He looked at it for a while. *Steady hours. Fifteen-minute stand-up. No night bell. Estimates are estimates.* It was a good offer. It was the best offer anyone had ever made him, in either life. He didn't tear it up. He folded it carefully and put it back in the drawer.

Then he took a small card and wrote two words on it, and addressed it to Tanner's Row, and put it on the corner of the desk for the morning post.

> *Not yet.*

There was one more thing.

He took the lamp and climbed the stairs to the second floor, to the war room. It was dark and cold, and the long table was still covered in chalk dust, and on the far wall, the chalkboard was exactly as they'd left it.

*MALPHUS AUDIT. DEMON. HARVEST FESTIVAL.* *TOP PRIORITY. TOP PRIORITY. TOP PRIORITY.* *MOVE DEMON?* An armory roof. A feast. Six small gestures of goodwill. *CAPE (GARETH).* And at the very top, squeezed into the last space on the board, in Bramble's tidy little letters, above everything else: *PRIORITY ZERO: FLOAT (CONTRAPTION).*

Mike set the lamp down on the table.

He picked up the rag. And starting at the top, with the float, he wiped the whole board clean. All of it. Every word, every priority, every arrow, every corner, until there was nothing left on it but a faint gray smear and the lamplight.

Then he picked up a piece of chalk, and at the very top of the empty board, he wrote:

> *WHAT ACTUALLY MATTERS*

He stood back and looked at it.

Downstairs, on the front counter, the sand in Malphus's hourglass kept falling. A little over three days now. Slow, and steady, and not getting any faster.

He asked the skill one more time, just to be sure.

**Estimated time until the guild collapses: unknown.**

Mike blew out the lamp and went home to sleep for four hours.

---

The next morning, he was at his desk at seven, behind Osric's stack, with a cup of tea.

At a quarter past, Brannoc came through the hall on his way upstairs, holding his empty mug, and stopped beside the stack.

"Mike!" he said. "There you are. Can I get an estimate on the float?"

Mike looked at him.

Brannoc looked back, cheerfully, with no idea at all. He had signed the letter without reading it. He had stood on a chair and given a speech about *initiative* without, Mike now realized, the faintest idea what the party had been for. He seemed to think it had been an anniversary party. Eight years, after all. Of initiative. As far as the Guildmaster of the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor was concerned, his senior clerk had never left at all.

Only the Basement had known. And Velka.

"I'll have something for you this afternoon," said Mike.

"Great," said Brannoc, and went upstairs.

At noon, a runner came from Tanner's Row with a small, plain card, addressed to Mike in small, careful handwriting. It said only:

> *Understood. The window will keep.*
>
> *— W.H.*

Mike put it in the drawer, on top of the offer, and went upstairs to the war room, where the board was still clean, and waiting.
