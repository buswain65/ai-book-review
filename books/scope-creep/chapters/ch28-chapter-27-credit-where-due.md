# Chapter 27: Credit Where Due

On the morning of the harvest festival, the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor had promised nobody anything.

Mike checked. He went through the front counter ledger twice, and Priscilla's pink notebook once, and the board in the war room, and the bell schedule, which was now very short. There was nothing. No escort. No float. No wyvern by Friday. No small gesture of goodwill. For the first time in the two hundred years of the guild's history, as far as anyone could tell, there was not a single thing that anybody, anywhere, was waiting for the guild to do today.

Nobody knew what to do with themselves.

The adventurers stood around the main hall in their best clothes, holding their swords, looking at each other. The clerks sat at their desks with nothing on them. Tolliver kept picking up a pen and putting it down again. Bramble tuned his lute four times. Dex asked if there was a form he could fill out, and when Tam said no, looked genuinely alarmed.

"It's a holiday," Mike told them.

"But what are we *for*?" said Dex.

Mike thought about it. "Today," he said, "we're for watching."

---

At eight o'clock, Pip came up from the Basement carrying the Duke's banner.

It was rolled up on a long pole, and he unrolled it on the floor of the main hall with great ceremony, and everybody crowded around to look. Mike had asked the skill about it in the Duke's hall, the day he said no. **Estimated time for a banner with a golem on it: 2 hours.** Pip had started it at six. It was eight o'clock exactly.

It was beautiful. It was a golem, painted in gray and blue and bronze on a long white field: the old Registrar, round and lumpy and wise, with its two blue eyes glowing and its quill arm raised, as if in greeting. Pip had painted it from life, sitting on the floor of the round room with his paints in his lap, while the Registrar watched him.

And around the border, in tiny, careful lettering, there were footnotes.

> *¹ Eyes: accurate.*
>
> *² Quill arm: accurate, though in life it is usually holding a contract.*
>
> *³ Expression: approximate. The Registrar does not, strictly speaking, have an expression. This is my best guess.*
>
> *⁴ Lumps: accurate. All of them.*

"It's perfect," said Mike.

Pip went pink to the tips of his ears and rolled it back up.

At a quarter past eight, the Basement hatch opened again, and something happened that nobody at the guild had ever seen.

Durgan came up.

He came up the ladder slowly, as if he were climbing out of a well into the light, and stood in the main hall blinking. He had washed. His beard had been combed, which nobody had known was possible. He was wearing a clean shirt that still had the creases in it from wherever it had been folded for a very long time. And on his chest, polished bright, was the medal from the crypt that Gareth had given him the day before.

"Festival," said Durgan. He said it the way another man might say *ocean*, or *dragon*, or *Tuesday in Spain*: like a word for something he'd heard of, but never actually seen.

"First one?" said Mike.

Durgan nodded. "Nineteen years."

He walked to the front door, the one that was back where it belonged, with *LOAD-BEARING* carved into the frame, and he put one big hand flat against it for a moment, the way he'd put his hand on the stone beside the seal. Then he opened it and went out into the sunshine.

At half past eight, Gareth the Bold reported for duty.

He was supposed to walk beside the Duke's carriage for the length of the parade route. It was the job Mike had offered the Duke, the alternative to a full guild escort, and Gareth had taken it more seriously than anything Mike had ever seen him take. He had polished his armor. He had polished his sword. He had polished his boots and his belt buckle and, Mike suspected, his teeth.

And he was wearing a cape.

It was a magnificent cape. It was enormous, and deep, rich purple, and it fell from his shoulders all the way to his heels, and it swept along the floor behind him as he walked, and it rippled. It was the most impressive thing Gareth had ever worn, by a very long way.

Mike looked at it. He looked at it for a while.

"Gareth," he said slowly. "Where did you get that cape?"

"Found it," said Gareth proudly. He swished it. "It was just hanging there. In the Guildmaster's office. On a rail. Over a wall." He frowned slightly. "It wasn't *doing* anything."

It was the purple curtain. The eleven-silver-crown curtain. *Office Improvements (Urgent).* The curtain that had been hanging over the wall of frames in Brannoc's office for weeks, so that nobody, not Brannoc, not the clerks, and above all not the Duke, would ever have to see what those frames said.

Mike opened his mouth to say something.

Then he closed it again.

"It looks great, Gareth," he said.

Gareth swished it again, and went out to find the Duke.

Nobody stopped him. Nobody told Brannoc.

---

The parade began at ten.

The Duke of Varrenhold's carriage came down the hill from his hall and through the east gate and into the city, slowly, pulled by four white horses with gold ribbons in their manes. Behind it came the Duke's honor guard, all twenty of them, who had spent the last week carrying stones and holding ladders and guarding a barrier and were looking much happier and more muscular than before. Behind them came the festival bands, and the dancers, and the cart with the giant harvest pumpkin on it, and a long, cheerful, disorganized tail of everyone in Brindlemoor who had felt like walking.

And beside the carriage, the whole way, marched Gareth the Bold in his purple cape, with his sword held upright in front of him, beaming like a lighthouse.

The Duke sat in the open carriage, holding the dragon tooth, with the golem banner flying from a pole behind him. He looked out at the crowds lining the street. And everywhere he went, people came up to the carriage, the way they always did, to ask the Duke for things.

The Duke had discovered his new favorite word. He used it on everything.

A juggler ran alongside the carriage, juggling five flaming torches. "Your Grace! A private performance? For your guests? Tonight?"

"NO," said the Duke happily.

A man pushed a cart up beside the wheels. "Soup, Your Grace? Fresh soup! Hot! Leek and—"

"ABSOLUTELY NOT," said the Duke.

A small girl in a straw hat stepped out from the crowd, holding up a single yellow flower. She didn't ask anything. She just held it out.

The Duke looked at it for a long moment.

"...YES," he said, and took it, and put it behind his ear.

The crowd cheered.

And moving through the crowd, working his way from carriage to carriage along the parade route with a leather satchel over one shoulder, was a familiar figure in an unfamiliar tunic. It was green and bronze, with a small embroidered golem on the breast, and the words *QUINT & DAUGHTERS AUTOMATA* stitched across the back. Rupert Spindlewick had started his new job early. He was handing out pamphlets.

Mike, in the crowd, caught one as it fluttered past. It was glossy and bronze and smelled faintly of new ink.

> *Introducing OMNIFILE 2.0!*
>
> *Now with INK STRATEGY™!*
>
> *Legacy-informed. Synergy-driven. Convergence-ready.*
>
> *(Service agreement required. See clause fourteen.)*

Rupert reached the Duke's carriage. He fell into step beside it, on the other side from Gareth, and held up a pamphlet with a bright, warm, confident smile.

"Your Grace," he said, "have you considered a legacy-informed—"

"NO," said the Duke.

Rupert didn't even break stride. He smiled, nodded, tucked the pamphlet back into his satchel, and peeled away smoothly toward the next carriage, where the Countess of Lyle was sitting, already reaching for another pamphlet.

Mike folded the pamphlet carefully and put it in his pocket. He would file it later.

The guild did not march. For the first time in its history, the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor stood in the crowd on the festival route and *watched*. Mike stood with Velka and Pip and Tam on the steps of the guild hall. Sister Agatha had brought a picnic. Sir Roderick had brought a chair. Osric had brought his green ledger and was sitting on the bottom step, making small notes, possibly about the parade, possibly about something else entirely. Nobody asked.

Durgan stood at the back, a little apart, still blinking in the sunlight. Someone, at some point, had handed him a toffee apple on a stick, and he was holding it the way he might hold an unfamiliar tool. He watched the Duke's carriage go by, and the honor guard, and the dancers, and the pumpkin, and the bands.

"Nineteen years," he said, to nobody in particular. He took a bite of the toffee apple, and chewed, and thought about it. "Should've come up sooner."

At the far edge of the square, a farmer in a muddy coat had cornered Priscilla. Mike could see it happening from the steps. He could see the farmer gesturing toward the north, and the word *field*, and the word *Monday*. He could see Priscilla's face light up, the way it always did, and her mouth start to open, the way it always did.

He held his breath.

Priscilla's mouth closed.

She looked down at her hands. She took a breath. And then she said something to the farmer, and turned around, and came across the square through the crowd, toward the guild hall steps, and up them, to Mike.

"A north field," she said, a little breathlessly. "Cleared of rocks and stumps. Twelve acres. He wants it by Monday." She looked at him hopefully. "Could you... check the estimate?"

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time to clear a twelve-acre field: 9 days.**

"Nine days," said Mike. "Not Monday. The Thursday after next."

Priscilla nodded firmly. She went back across the square, through the crowd, and said it to the farmer. Mike watched the farmer's face. It went through surprise, and confusion, and something like suspicion, and he looked Priscilla up and down as though she had suddenly grown a second head and he wasn't sure which one was in charge.

Then he shook her hand.

---

The parade ended at the guild hall, as the Duke had said it would.

The carriage drew up in front of the steps. The honor guard formed two lines. The bands finished their tune with a flourish, a little out of time with each other. And the Duke of Varrenhold climbed down from his carriage, with the yellow flower behind his ear, and offered his arm to the Countess of Lyle, and beckoned to the Margrave of Thelling, and swept up the steps of the guild hall and in through the front door, past *LOAD-BEARING* and *DO NOT MOVE*, as if he owned the place.

"MY WALL," he announced to the hall at large. "I HAVE COME TO SEE MY WALL."

Brannoc had been waiting for him.

He had been waiting at the foot of the stairs in his best tunic, with his full mug in one hand, and when he saw the Duke he put on his largest, warmest, most confident smile, and bowed deeply, and led the way.

"Your Grace! Countess! Margrave! This way, this way. Right up these stairs. Mind the step." He was talking fast. "Two hundred and thirteen, Your Grace, every one of them a treasure, I tell everyone—"

They went up the stairs to the Guildmaster's office. Mike followed, a little way behind. So did Velka. So, Mike noticed, did most of the guild, at a polite, curious, safe distance.

Brannoc threw open the office door and strode in ahead of his guests, toward the far wall, and reached up with a grand, practiced sweep of his arm for the edge of the purple curtain, ready to pull it back with a flourish at exactly the right moment, the way he must have rehearsed it a hundred times in his head.

His hand closed on nothing.

There was no curtain.

There was only the rail it had hung on, empty. And behind it, floor to ceiling, covering the whole of the far wall, two hundred and thirteen gilt frames. Every single one held a single word, in red, in the Duke's own seal.

*MEDIOCRE.*

The office went absolutely silent.

The Countess of Lyle raised her lorgnette. The Margrave of Thelling raised his eyebrows. The Duke of Varrenhold, Lord of the Hill, holder of the dragon tooth, stood in the doorway and looked at his famous wall, and said nothing at all.

Brannoc stood beside it with his hand still raised in the air, closed around nothing, and went slowly, steadily gray, from the beard upward.

And then, in the silence, Mike watched him reach for the only tool he had ever seen work.

"It's a..." said Brannoc. His voice came out high and strange. "It's a, uh... a *refresh*, Your Grace. A heritage-informed..." He swallowed. "Wall 2.0."

The Duke didn't look at him.

He didn't seem to have heard him. He was looking at the wall. He looked at it for a long, long time: up and down, from one side to the other, frame by frame, the way Malphus read a ledger. The Countess glanced at the Margrave. The Margrave glanced at the door.

And then the Duke laughed.

It was the laugh from his hall: the great, booming, rolling laugh that shook the tapestries. It shook the frames on the wall. It shook Brannoc. It went on for a very long time, and when it was done, the Duke wiped his eyes with the back of his hand, and the yellow flower fell out from behind his ear, and he didn't notice.

"MEDIOCRE," he said, with enormous satisfaction. "I *KNEW* IT."

"Your Grace," began Brannoc weakly. "I can explain—"

"YOU DON'T NEED TO," said the Duke cheerfully. "*I* CAN." He turned to his guests. "MY SEAL, YOU SEE, IS CURSED. HAS BEEN FOR GENERATIONS. OLD FAMILY BUSINESS. WHATEVER IT IS STAMPED UPON, IT TELLS THE TRUTH ABOUT. THAT'S WHY IT MUST NEVER, EVER BE STAMPED." He pointed the dragon tooth at the wall. "SOMEBODY STAMPED IT. AND IT TOLD THE TRUTH ABOUT THIS GUILD. THAT NIGHT. TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTEEN TIMES." He beamed. "THAT," said the Duke of Varrenhold, "IS THE MOST HONEST THING ON THIS WALL."

Mike, in the doorway, thought about the old rule. *The Duke's paperwork must never be stamped. For reasons involving an old feud, and a seal that may or may not have been cursed.* He had always assumed it was superstition. It turned out it was a curse. It turned out the curse was *honesty*. He thought, not for the first time that week, that he was going to need a much larger filing cabinet.

Then the Duke turned around, and looked past Brannoc, past the Countess and the Margrave, to the doorway, to Mike.

"MIKE," he said. "WHAT'S THE WORD TODAY?"

Everyone in the doorway turned to look at Mike.

Mike didn't ask the skill. He didn't need to. He looked at the Duke, and thought about the week. About the board, and the door, and the seal, and the audit, and the people who had done the work.

He didn't answer. He just shrugged, very slightly.

The Duke nodded, as if that were answer enough. He walked to the wall. He looked along the frames to the very last one, the newest, the frame nearest the window. And he reached up with the dragon tooth and touched its tip, very lightly, to the glass.

The red letters inside the frame began to move. They squirmed across the paper, the way they had that night, like worms, rearranging themselves, letter by letter, while the whole room held its breath.

They settled.

*ADEQUATE.*

One frame. One frame, out of two hundred and thirteen. The other two hundred and twelve stayed exactly as they were. *MEDIOCRE, MEDIOCRE, MEDIOCRE,* all the way up to the ceiling. And in the bottom corner, by the window, in red, one *ADEQUATE*.

"HISTORY," said the Duke of Varrenhold, "DOESN'T CHANGE." He tapped the last frame gently with the tooth. "BUT TODAY DOES."

There was a long, quiet moment.

Then Brannoc cleared his throat.

"Your Grace," he said. Some of the color had come back into his face. "Could we, perhaps, move that one to the *middle*? Where people can see it?"

"NO," said the Duke.

---

On the way out, as the Duke's party went back down the stairs, the Margrave of Thelling dropped back and fell into step beside Mike.

He was a lean, hawkish man with a short gray beard and an air of having been disappointed by a great many things. He looked at Mike sideways.

"You're the clerk," he said. "The one who estimates."

"Yes, my lord."

"I'm told you know everyone in this city who does honest work." The Margrave cleared his throat. "I had an order. Twenty swords. For my honor guard. Fine steel, gold inlay. I wanted them for the festival, by Friday. My agent tells me the smith turned him down." He frowned, as though this were a new and unpleasant experience. "Do you know a smith who could do twenty swords? Quickly?"

Mike thought about a forge on Anvil Lane. A slate on the wall with every promise on it. A velvet hat going away around a corner.

"I know one who could do them by spring," he said.

The Margrave stopped on the stairs. He looked at Mike. Mike could see him getting ready to say *by spring?* in a tone of outrage, the way men like him had always said it, in every workshop, in every world.

And then, to his own visible surprise, the Margrave said:

"...Fair enough."

He seemed puzzled by it himself. He went down the stairs shaking his head slightly, as if he had just discovered a new word, and wasn't sure yet whether he liked it.

---

The speech was at noon, in the square.

It was a festival tradition. Every year, at noon, the most important person in Brindlemoor who wasn't the Duke climbed up onto the rim of the big stone fountain in the middle of the square and said something to the crowd. Usually it was the mayor. This year, it was Brannoc.

He climbed up onto the fountain in his best tunic, holding his full mug, and the crowd gathered around him in a great ring, and the bands stopped playing, and he spread his arms wide.

"PEOPLE OF BRINDLEMOOR!" roared Brannoc, in his barn-collapsing voice, and it echoed off every wall in the square.

Mike stood near the back of the crowd, between Velka and the Duke. The Duke had insisted on standing with them. He had refused his chair, his canopy, and three separate offers of soup, and was now standing among the common people with the yellow flower back behind his ear, looking extremely pleased with himself.

"This week," cried Brannoc, "our guild faced the greatest crisis in its history! A demon! A debt! A seal that was failing! And in the darkest hour, when all seemed lost, *someone* had to step up. Someone had to make a *plan*."

The crowd murmured expectantly.

"And as I always say," said Brannoc, putting one hand on his chest, "communication isn't a cause! It's a word you put on a cause, so you don't have to say what it is!"

There was a pause while the crowd tried to work out whether this was profound. Then, on balance, they decided it was, and cheered.

Mike's mouth moved.

He didn't mean it to. It just happened. He had said that sentence himself, the day before, in the war room, peeling yellow squares off a wall. And as Brannoc said it, from the top of the fountain, word for word, Mike found himself saying it along with him, very quietly, under his breath.

Velka turned and looked at him.

"When the demon came," Brannoc went on, "I looked my people in the eye, and I told them: *this is the same thing. It just has a demon in it!*"

*It just has a demon in it,* mouthed Mike, perfectly in time.

Velka's face, which had been tight and red and furious since the word *someone*, began to do something complicated.

"And I said to myself," cried Brannoc, "what does this guild need? It needs *Fridays*! Fridays for paying down debt! That was my idea!"

*Malphus's idea*, mouthed Mike.

"And most of all," said Brannoc, and his voice dropped, and he looked out over the crowd with deep, noble, heartfelt sincerity, "I always say: *nobody asks the Basement*." He thumped his chest. "Well. *I* ask the Basement."

The crowd roared.

Velka snorted. She tried to stop it and couldn't. She clapped a hand over her mouth and her shoulders shook, and she turned away, and turned back, and looked at Mike, still silently mouthing along to the Guildmaster's speech like a man singing along to a song he'd written himself, and she laughed so hard she had to hold on to the Duke's arm to stay upright.

At the very back of the crowd, where he was standing a little apart with his toffee apple, Durgan said, "Hm."

"I can't believe you're not angry," Velka managed, eventually, wiping her eyes.

"It's on the record," said Mike. "Plan owner, bottom corner of the board. In his own words. *I want it on the record that this is your plan.*" He shrugged. "He can say whatever he likes from a fountain. The board's still there."

Beside them, the Duke leaned down toward Mike. He lowered his voice, which meant that only about half the square could hear him.

"HE'S LYING," said the Duke.

"I know, Your Grace," said Mike.

"I THOUGHT YOU'D WANT TO KNOW."

"Thank you, Your Grace."

Up on the fountain, Brannoc had moved on to the door, and was explaining how he had personally carried it across the guild hall, which was, to be fair, true.

Mike felt someone watching him. He looked across the ring of the crowd, and there, on the other side of the fountain, stood a woman in a plain dark coat with her sleeves rolled up, holding a large mug. It was full. Steam was rising from it. It smelled, even from across the square, of strong coffee.

It was Wren Halloway, Guildmistress of Tanner's Row.

She was watching Brannoc's speech with one eyebrow raised, the way you'd watch a dog walking on its hind legs: not with scorn, exactly, but with a certain professional curiosity. Then her eyes moved across the crowd and found Mike's, and she lifted her mug to him, very slightly, an inch.

"Not yet?" she said. She said it quietly, but somehow it carried.

"Not yet," said Mike.

She nodded, as if that were a perfectly reasonable answer, and went back to watching the speech.

Up on the fountain, Brannoc had caught sight of her too. Mike saw it happen. He saw Brannoc's eyes find Wren in the crowd, and find her mug, and stay there for a moment. He saw Brannoc look down at his own mug, in his own hand. For years, it had been empty. He had carried it empty everywhere, because Wren Halloway carried a mug, and Wren Halloway was very successful.

It was full now. Of tea. Malphus's tea.

Brannoc looked at it, and then back at Wren's, and something in his face settled. Mike had seen that expression on Brannoc's face exactly never. It was, he decided after a moment, *contentment*. Not envy. Not wanting. Just a man looking at his own mug, and finding it full, and being satisfied with that.

Brannoc took a sip, and went on with his speech.

A runner came pushing through the crowd, looking for someone, and found Mike. He handed over a small card. It was heavy and cream-colored, and the writing on it was in red ink, neat and precise. The ink was faintly, unmistakably warm.

> *Plan owner: M. Dobbins.*
>
> *I have it in writing.*
>
> *— M.*

Mike read it twice. Then he put it carefully inside his jacket, next to the Rupert pamphlet, to file later.

On the fountain, Brannoc finished his speech with a great sweeping bow, and the crowd cheered, and the bands struck up again, and the festival went on.

---

That evening, the guild hall was nearly empty.

Mike went up to Brannoc's office because there was a note on his desk, in Brannoc's handwriting, that said only: *Office. Six o'clock.* It was the first thing Brannoc had written to him since the retrospective. It did not say why.

The office was dim and quiet. The light from the window fell across the wall: two hundred and twelve red *MEDIOCRE*s, and one red *ADEQUATE*, in the bottom corner, nearest the glass. Brannoc sat behind his desk with his full mug in front of him. He was looking at the one frame. He didn't look up when Mike came in.

It had been, Mike realized, almost exactly one day since the retrospective. Since Brannoc had stood up in the war room and walked to the front and torn six scrolls in half and walked back to his chair, without a word. He had talked all day since, of course: to the Duke, to his guests, to the crowd from the top of a fountain. But none of that, Mike suspected, counted. That had all been for an audience. That had been the old Brannoc, the one on the fountain. He hadn't said a single thing yet to anyone who had been in that room.

This was the one behind the desk.

Mike stood in the doorway and asked the skill.

**Estimated time until Brannoc says something: 4 seconds.**

Mike counted, in his head.

*Three.*

*Two.*

*One.*

"Mike," said Brannoc.

He said it quietly. He still didn't look up from the frame.

"Next year's festival," said Brannoc. "I promised His Grace. The whole guild, marching. Properly. Banners. Drums. Everyone in step." He turned the mug slowly in his hands. "How long would it take to get ready? To do it right?"

Mike asked the skill.

"Six weeks," he said. "Of practice. Twice a week. Not on Fridays."

Brannoc nodded slowly. And then Mike watched his mouth open, and watched the old words come up, the way they always did, fully formed, from wherever they lived in him.

"Can we do it in—"

He stopped.

He stopped, the way he had stopped at the bottom of the crypt, with the rest of the sentence still in his mouth. And he looked at the wall. He looked at the frame in the bottom corner, by the window, at the one word in red that was different from all the others.

"Six weeks," said Brannoc.

He pulled his calendar toward him. He found a date in the spring. He picked up his quill, and wrote it down, carefully, not in a flourish, but in ordinary letters that anyone could read. *Festival practice begins. Six weeks.*

Then he put the quill down. He sat for a while.

"Mike," he said, without looking up. "Why did Edwin leave?"

Mike had not expected that. He stood very still.

"Nobody listened," he said.

Brannoc nodded slowly. He looked at the wall, at the one ADEQUATE, for a long time.

"I'm listening," he said.

Mike didn't say anything. He didn't think there was anything to say. He just stood in the doorway of the office, and looked at the Guildmaster, and nodded.

There was a long pause.

"I'm still giving the speech next year, though," said Brannoc.

And Mike laughed. He couldn't help it. It came out of him all at once, in the quiet office, and after a moment, to Mike's enormous surprise, Brannoc laughed too.

---

The place by the river was very small.

It had four tables, one cook, and a waiter who was also the cook's son, and it sat right at the bottom of Cooper's Lane, by the dock, where the water ran slow and brown and deep in the dark. Mike had walked past it a thousand times in his life and never once gone in. Velka had picked it. She said it was because it was quiet. Mike suspected it was because, from the table by the window, you could see the river, and on the river, out in the middle, floating gently upside down in the moonlight, with its wheels in the air and the scope creeper still trailing from its axles, was the Contraption.

"It's still floating," said Mike.

"It was *tested*," said Velka, with dignity.

They sat down. There was no board on the wall. There was no list. Nobody had a quest. Nobody needed an estimate for anything. Mike had brought nothing with him at all: no pen, no paper, no chalk. He kept reaching for his pocket and finding nothing in it.

They talked about nothing in particular. It was harder than Mike expected. In all the time they'd known each other, they had talked a great deal, about spells, about tests, about horse ponds and wagons and crypts and demons and hats. They had never once talked about nothing. It took a while to remember how. Velka asked him what Raleigh was like, and he tried to explain about highways, and pine trees, and barbecue, and she laughed at him. He asked her why she'd become a mage, and she told him about a candle she'd set on fire when she was six, on purpose, to see if she could, and how her mother had been so proud and so angry at the same time that it had seemed like the best possible thing to be.

The waiter came. He was a cheerful, gangly young man with flour on his apron.

"The fish is very good tonight," he said. "And the kitchen says twenty minutes."

Mike, out of pure reflex, out of twenty years of habit, without even meaning to, checked.

**Estimated time until your food arrives: 20 minutes.**

He stared at the words.

Twenty minutes. The waiter had said twenty minutes. And the skill said twenty minutes. And the skill was never, ever wrong.

He looked up at the waiter. The waiter looked back at him cheerfully, with flour on his apron, with no idea that anything had happened at all.

"Thanks," said Mike faintly.

Velka was watching him across the table.

"What?" she said.

"He said twenty minutes," said Mike.

"Yes?"

"It's going to be twenty minutes."

Velka looked at him. "Is that... unusual?"

Mike opened his mouth to explain, and found that he couldn't. Not properly. He wanted to tell her about a lifetime of estimates, two lifetimes. About every *quick win* and *won't take a minute* and *by Friday* he had ever heard, from every manager and client and salesman and Guildmaster in two worlds. About how he had grown up, twice, inside a world where everyone told you a number, and the number was always, always wrong. About how he had died at his desk at two in the morning because of a number that was wrong.

And now a boy with flour on his apron had told him a number, and it was right. Just like that. Without a skill. Without a goddess. Without anything but a cook in a kitchen who knew how long it took to cook a fish, and a waiter who had bothered to ask her.

"It's the first time," he said, "that somebody else's estimate has been right."

He said it, and he was surprised to find that his eyes were stinging.

Velka looked at him for a long moment. Then she began to laugh. She laughed until she had to put her head down on the table, and the other three tables turned to look at them, and the waiter came back out of the kitchen to see what was wrong.

"*That's* what gets you?" she said, wiping her eyes. "Demons. Debt. A wall that says *mediocre* two hundred times. And *that's* what gets you? *Fish*?"

"It's a very good estimate," said Mike, with dignity.

The fish came. It was twenty minutes, exactly. It was very good.

They ate it, and talked about nothing, and watched the Contraption float in the dark. And somewhere in the middle of the second glass of wine, Mike looked across the table at Velka, at the candlelight on her face and the soot she had never quite managed to get off her left sleeve, and he felt the old, familiar shape of a question start to form in his head, all by itself, the way they always did.

*Estimated time until—*

He stopped.

Velka was watching him. She had seen it. She always saw it. She had been watching him ask the skill things for years, and she knew exactly what it looked like, the small blank moment behind his eyes when he went somewhere else for an answer.

"Don't," said Velka.

"I wasn't going to," said Mike.

It was true. He hadn't been going to. He'd caught himself. He had, for the first time in either of his lives, started to ask the skill something, and then decided that he didn't want to know. That he'd rather wait, and see, and find out the long way, the way everybody else did.

Velka looked at him for a moment longer. Then she reached up, and took off the brown hat. The plain one. The one he'd tested over a candle for ten minutes, and let a stable dog chew for an hour. The one she hadn't taken off in weeks.

She set it down on the table between them, next to the candle.

Outside, the river went by. The Contraption floated. Somewhere up on the hill, the Duke was asleep, and nobody was standing outside his window.

Mike didn't ask the skill anything else that night.

He found he didn't want to.
