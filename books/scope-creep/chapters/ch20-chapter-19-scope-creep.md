# Chapter 19: Scope Creep

Priscilla brought the list to the stand-up the next morning, on a long strip of pink paper that she unrolled with a flourish, the way a herald unrolls a proclamation.

"Six new clients!" she announced. "Six! For the Contraption! And each of them has asked for just *one small thing*."

She began to read.

"Master Thrum of the River Merchants' Consortium. You remember Master Thrum! He'd like it to *fly*."

The circle murmured. Mike, behind his pillar, felt the cold drop in his stomach begin.

"Lord Ashcombe, of the manor on the hill. He'd like a small oven built into the back. For pies. Midnight pies, specifically."

Mike knew that name. He had scrubbed pots at the Drowned Goose until one in the morning for that name, when he was ten.

"The Baron of Westmarch," read Priscilla. "He'd like a sword rack on the side. Fully stocked." She squinted at her own handwriting. "*With swords that don't break.* He was very particular about that."

Mike knew that name too. He had pumped the bellows for that name, at eleven, for ten days and ten nights, and three weeks and two days later a rider in Westmarch colors had arrived with a sack of broken blades.

"The Hollins family, for their daughter's second wedding! They'd like it to play music."

"Second wedding?" said someone.

"The first one didn't have music," said Priscilla. "They felt it didn't take."

The Duke of Varrenhold, by the window, raised the dragon tooth. Priscilla curtsied to him.

"And His Grace, of course! A padded seat, for travel. And a priority bell."

"ADEQUATE," said the Duke.

"And finally," said Priscilla, and her smile grew even brighter, "our newest client! Local 47 of the Amalgamated Goblins of the Brindlemoor Hills!"

The circle went silent.

"They've settled their strike!" Priscilla explained happily. "Very amicably. And as part of the settlement, they've bought a Contraption, to carry porridge between the farms. They'd like a porridge pot."

She rolled up the pink paper.

"Six small things! Field test is in three days at the river. Everyone's coming!"

The circle applauded. Brannoc rang the gong.

Mike, behind his pillar, asked the skill about each one.

**Estimated time to add flight: 1 day.**

**Estimated time to add a pie oven: 6 hours.**

**Estimated time to add a sword rack: 2 hours.**

**Estimated time to add music: 4 hours.**

**Estimated time to add a seat and bell: 3 hours.**

**Estimated time to add a porridge pot: 1 hour.**

Each one was small. Each one, on its own, was entirely reasonable. Mike looked at the six lines and almost felt relieved.

Then a seventh line appeared below them.

**Estimated time to add all six together: unknown.**

**They interact.**

---

He told Velka about it that night. They were sitting on the edge of the stableyard horse pond, where the Contraption had been parked for the build, silver and humming in the lantern light. They were not there for any particular reason. They had both simply ended up there after the guild closed, and neither of them had said anything about it.

"In my world," said Mike, "we had a name for this."

Velka looked at him. He had told her about his world, a little, over the past few weeks. Not everything. Enough. She had listened to all of it without laughing, which was more than he could have said for himself.

"What?"

"Scope creep." He waved a hand at the wagon. "You start with one thing. Then somebody asks for one small extra thing. Then another. Each one's tiny. Nobody ever says no, because each one's tiny. And it grows and it grows until one day you look up and the thing you were building is gone, and in its place there's something enormous that nobody asked for all at once."

Velka considered this, frowning. "*Scope creep*," she repeated slowly. "Sounds like a monster."

Mike looked at the Contraption: the twine, the parasol, the bell, the sheep asleep on the back, and the empty space on the roof where, by Thursday, they would have to bolt on something that let it fly.

"It is," he said.

---

At the next morning's stand-up, Brannoc declared all six features Priority One.

"Every one of them!" he boomed. "Every client is our *most important* client!"

Rupert, beside him, beamed. "And to make sure we deliver, team, we're applying Lean Six Sigil. Standard takt time." He wrote on the slate. "Each feature: forty minutes."

"Forty minutes," said Mike. "For flight."

"Standardization, Mike."

The roommates were given what Rupert called *integration ownership*, which meant that Dex, Tolliver, and Bramble would oversee the bolting of each feature onto the Contraption, and the Basement would do the actual work. The Basement had three days.

That afternoon, Mike went down the ladder with a fresh slate. He set it on Pip's desk and drew a grid on it: six features down the side, six across the top. Then he crossed out the diagonal, because a feature could not interact with itself, and the lower half, because the pairs repeated.

Fifteen squares were left.

"Fifteen," he said. "Six features. Fifteen ways for any two of them to get in each other's way. And that's just pairs."

Pip looked at the grid for a long time. "Footnote," he said faintly, "this is the same arithmetic as the reinforcements."

"It's always the same arithmetic," said Mike.

The Contraption grew.

It grew in the most literal way. By the end of the first day, with the porridge pot and the padded seat installed, it was too heavy for its two horses, and needed four. By the end of the second day, with the oven and the sword rack bolted on either side, it was three wagons wide and needed eight. The stableyard had to take down a fence. Duchess the sheep, who had previously ridden on a small step at the back, now had her own deck, with a rail, and a little roof, and a water trough. She seemed to find this adequate.

And on the first day, Pip found the vine.

He was crouched by the Contraption's front wheel, checking the ward-twine, when he noticed it: a small, pale-green tendril, no longer than his finger, curling out of a gap in the silver thread. It had tiny, round leaves. It was growing out of the Contraption itself, out of the enchantment, as if the magic had leaked.

By that evening, after the porridge pot went on, it was a foot long.

By the next morning, after the oven, it was two.

Pip watched it with fascination. He measured it every time a new feature was installed. He kept a careful chart. And he was, as far as Mike could tell, completely delighted.

"It's feeding on leftover enchantment," he explained, glowing, "from the overlapping wards. Every new feature adds more overlap. Every overlap leaks a little magic. And the vine grows." He wrote in his notes, in his best and smallest hand, and showed it to Mike with enormous pride.

*Scope creeper (Contraptio crescens). Grows approx. one foot per new requirement. Harmless. Probably.*

Mike looked at the vine. It had wound itself three times around the porridge pot and was reaching, in a small, determined, leafy way, toward the oven.

"Of course it is," he said.

---

The interactions began on the first afternoon, and they did not stop.

The pie oven, installed at the back for Lord Ashcombe, got hot. That was what ovens did. And as it got hot, it heated the ward-twine wrapped around the wagon, which was spun with protective runes that had not been designed to be warmed, and which began, slowly and with a smell like singed hair, to fray.

Mike went to the slate and, in neat handwriting, filled in the first square.

*Oven + twine: twine frays.*

The Hollins family's music box played a gentle, sweet wedding tune, very well. Unfortunately, underneath the front axle, Pip's tiny promise rune had been carved to resonate with belief, and it turned out it resonated with music too. Every time the music box played, the rune began to hum along, slightly off-key, and its glow flickered.

*Music + promise rune: rune hums off-key. Holds less well.*

The Duke's priority bell was wired, by the roommates, with tremendous confidence, directly into the fire ward. Durgan learned about it when the ward board downstairs groaned like a dying cow. He climbed up the ladder, looked at the bell, and said nothing for a full minute.

*Bell + fire ward: again.*

The Baron of Westmarch's swords arrived on the second morning, in a long wooden crate: twenty of them, enchanted at enormous expense to be unbreakable. They were. They were also, because unbreakable enchantments were made of very dense magic, extraordinarily heavy. When they were racked on the side of the Contraption, the whole wagon tipped over onto its side, and three roommates and a sheep had to push it back.

*Swords + float: too heavy. Float 1.0 not tested for this weight.*

And Local 47's porridge pot, which was large and iron and had been installed under the folded parasol to keep the rain out, came to the boil on its first trial, rose up in a great creamy wave, and flooded the parasol mechanism with hot porridge. The parasol sprang open by itself and would not close. It smelled faintly of oats for the rest of the book.

*Porridge + parasol: parasol now porridge.*

By the third morning, all fifteen squares on Mike's grid were full. Each one held a small, careful disaster, in his neat hand. Pip studied the slate for a long time. Then he took his chalk, and in the margin beside the grid, added a sixteenth square, all by itself.

*Duchess + everything.*

Mike looked at it. Then he looked across the stableyard to where Duchess was standing on her private deck, chewing the scope creeper with calm determination. It was now six feet long.

"Fair," said Mike.

Meanwhile, the *Known Issues* list grew.

It had been three pages long after the dragon-proof escort. Now, with six features and fifteen interactions and a sheep, it was eleven. Mike wrote every page himself, carefully, by lantern light, in the Basement. Item one was still, after everything, *Not dragon-proof.* Item three was still, word for word, exactly as he and Pip had written it in the very first version: *Promise rune fails if the client reads the contract carefully.*

He did three things with it.

He filed it with the Registrar. *Knock, knock, knock. BOOM. FILED.*

He raised it at the stand-up, out loud, item by item, for six minutes, until Brannoc said "Let's take that offline."

And he sent a copy, all eleven pages, by hand, to Rupert's desk.

Rupert's reply came back the same afternoon, on a small card, in Rupert's flowing hand.

*Love the detail! — R.*

Mike went and looked. The eleven pages were on the corner of Rupert's desk, exactly where the junior clerk had left them, still tied with string. The knot had not been touched.

---

Flight came on the third day.

It came in a crate from the capital, stamped *QUINT & DAUGHTERS AUTOMATA*, with a brass plaque on the lid: *LEVITATION MODULE, MARK ONE. Simply attach to any vehicle! Up, up, and away!* The roommates had ordered it. They had ordered it, Mike discovered, without asking anyone, on credit, two days before, as soon as Priscilla had said the word *fly*.

It was a bronze drum about the size of a barrel, with a polished dome on top and a small brass lever on the side marked *ASCEND*. Dex, Tolliver, and Bramble hoisted it onto the roof of the Contraption with a pulley and bolted it down. That night, the scope creeper grew two feet.

"Nobody's tested it," said Mike, to Rupert, at the next morning's stand-up.

"It's from Quint and Daughters, Mike," said Rupert. "It's *modern*."

Then Rupert turned to Velka.

"Velka! Our superstar. Here's what I'm thinking. You already have the float spell. Couldn't you just... add flying to it? A small tweak? Just to give the module a little boost?"

The circle turned to look at her.

Velka was standing at her chalk mark, wearing the plain brown hat. She had not taken it off in a week. She looked at Rupert for a long moment. Then she looked across the circle at Mike, briefly, and back.

"No," she said.

Rupert blinked. "No?"

"Float one point oh," said Velka, "was tested for one thing. A wagon floating on a river. That's what it does. Thirty-one tests, ten in a row. It wasn't tested for flying. It wasn't tested for swords, or ovens, or porridge, or whatever that *vine* is." She folded her arms. "I'll cast Float. Exactly as tested. Nothing more."

Rupert smiled at her with warm, patient encouragement. "Velka, I *love* the caution. I really do. But right now, we need you to be a *team player*."

Velka did not move.

"I *am* on a team," she said. "It's the one that tests things."

There was a silence around the circle. Somebody coughed.

Mike, behind his pillar, discovered that he was grinning. He could not stop. He tried, for professional reasons, and failed completely. He had worked with a great many clever people in two lifetimes. He had never, in either of them, been prouder of anyone.

---

The field test was held on the third afternoon, at the river, and the whole town came.

Priscilla had made it an event. There was bunting along the riverbank, and a band, and a pie stall, and a stand of benches for the six clients. Master Thrum sat in the front row, glowing with anticipation. Lord Ashcombe sat beside him, older and stouter than Mike remembered, already eating a pie. The Baron of Westmarch, a lean, sour man in a black cloak, sat with his arms folded. The Hollins family took up an entire bench, with the bride in the middle. The Duke of Varrenhold had a chair of his own, in his dressing gown, with the dragon tooth.

And at the end of the row, in his neat brown waistcoat and his small round spectacles, with his folder on his knees, sat Nettle, shop steward of Local 47.

The Contraption stood at the top of the long ramp down to the water.

Mike had stopped thinking of it as a wagon. It was three wagons wide. It took eight horses to bring it down to the river. It had a pie oven at the back, smoking gently, and a rack of twenty very heavy swords on one side, and an iron porridge pot under a parasol that was permanently open and smelled of oats. There was a padded seat on the front with a priority bell beside it, and a music box, and a polished bronze drum bolted to the roof with a brass lever marked *ASCEND*. There was a sheep on a private deck. And over all of it, wound through the wheels and around the oven and up across the roof in great leafy green loops, was the scope creeper, now eleven feet long and still, Pip swore, growing.

Behind it, attached by a long iron chain, waited the caravan: six loaded wagons, one for each client, full of wool and wine and pies and porridge.

Mike stood at the edge of the crowd and looked at it all and thought that it was, in its way, the most honest thing he had ever seen. It looked exactly like what it was: six small requests that nobody had ever said no to.

Velka stepped up to the head of the ramp. She did not look at the bronze drum on the roof. She did not look at the clients. She closed her eyes, and spoke her grandmother's spell, slowly and plainly, word by word, exactly as she had tested it thirty-one times at a muddy horse pond.

The Contraption rolled down the ramp into the river, and floated.

It floated beautifully. It floated steadily, with the six wagons floating behind it like ducklings. The crowd applauded. The band played. Priscilla rang a small handbell she had brought specially, since the gong was too heavy to carry.

Then came the speeches.

Brannoc gave one. Rupert gave one, about synergy. Priscilla gave a long one, thanking each client by name and describing their feature in loving detail. The music box played under her voice. The Contraption floated. Everything, for a full quarter of an hour, was perfect.

And while the speeches went on, at the end of the clients' bench, Nettle opened his folder.

Mike saw it happen. He would remember it for a long time.

The little goblin took out a long document. He unfolded his small round spectacles and put them on. He licked one finger. And then, with the calm, careful, unhurried thoroughness of someone who has never once in his life signed anything without understanding it, Nettle began to read his contract.

All of it.

Every clause. Every subsection. Every footnote. He read the part about the porridge pot, and the part about delivery, and the part about liability, and the part in very small print at the bottom of page four, which said that the Contraption was *dragon-proof, fireproof, waterproof, and bandit-resistant (premium tier)*, and was guaranteed to perform all features as advertised.

Nettle read that part twice.

Then he frowned. He looked out at the Contraption, floating on the river with an oven smoking on one end and a vine wound around the other. He looked back down at the contract.

And Mike, standing at the edge of the crowd, felt something under the Contraption go *click*.

It was tiny. Nobody else heard it. But down under the front axle, under the water, a small carved circle with a line and a knot through it, a promise rune that had held only as long as the client believed, had just met a client who had read the contract carefully, and stopped believing.

Nettle closed his folder. He took off his spectacles. And he said, to nobody in particular, with genuine regret:

"I'm afraid I read it."

Then everything happened at once.

Without the promise rune, nothing was holding the features together anymore. Nothing was keeping them in line, or making them wait their turn. They all simply switched on.

The pie oven roared up like a forge. The music box leapt from a gentle wedding tune into something loud, fast, and frantic. The porridge pot boiled over again. On the padded seat, the Duke's priority bell began to ring, *DING DING DING*, all by itself, wired as it was into the fire ward three streets away.

And on the roof, the bronze drum of the Quint & Daughters Levitation Module, Mark One, which nobody had tested, whirred, and hummed, and glowed, and the little brass lever marked *ASCEND* flipped up all on its own.

The Contraption rose out of the river.

It rose slowly at first, streaming water, three wagons wide, with its oven roaring and its music blaring and its bell ringing and its sheep standing calmly on her deck. Then it rose faster. It rose ten feet into the air above the river, and twenty, and the iron chain behind it pulled taut, and then the first of the six wagons of the caravan lifted off the water. Then the second. Then all six, swinging beneath it on the chain like the tail of a kite.

On the bank, the crowd screamed.

The Contraption swung, enormous and roaring, out over the riverbank. The chain of wagons swept across the bank behind it, and swept up, as it went, the band, three pie stalls, a great deal of bunting, and Gareth the Bold, who had stepped forward with his sword drawn to give a heroic speech of his own and found himself caught up in the chain by his cloak.

From his chair on the bank, the Duke of Varrenhold watched his priority bell sail past overhead, still ringing.

"IS IT DONE YET?" he called up.

Nobody answered.

Then the Levitation Module, overloaded, gave one last, high, despairing whine, a little like the OMNIFILE's fanfare, and went out.

The Contraption fell.

It fell, with six wagons and a band and three pie stalls and Gareth, straight down into the middle of the river, with a splash that soaked the first four rows of the crowd. The oven hissed out. The music box gargled into silence. The bell stopped. For one long moment, there was nothing on the river but a vast, spreading ring of white foam and floating pies.

Then the Contraption came back up.

It came up upside down, wheels in the air, vine trailing in the water. But it came up. And it stayed up. It floated, steadily and a little clumsily, perfectly level on the slow brown water, exactly as it had been tested to float, thirty-one times, at a muddy horse pond, by a mage in a plain brown hat.

Float 1.0 was still holding. It was the only thing that was.

One by one, coughing and sputtering, the band members surfaced and grabbed onto it. Then the pie sellers, and two of the caravan drivers. Then everyone else. Within a minute, every single person who had gone into the river was clinging to the floating, upside-down Contraption, soaked to the skin, alive, and unhurt.

Duchess swam calmly to shore by herself, climbed out onto the bank, shook herself, and went to stand beside Bertram.

And last of all, Gareth the Bold burst up out of the water beside the wreck, gasping, his magnificent crimson cloak plastered to his head. He looked around. He saw the crowd on the bank, every face turned toward him. And Mike watched, with something that was almost admiration, as Gareth understood the situation, and decided.

He raised one arm out of the river.

"*I steered it into the water!*" bellowed Gareth the Bold. "*To save the town!*"

The crowd went wild.

Velka, standing beside Mike on the bank, said nothing at all. She had her arms folded and her hat pulled low, and she was watching the Contraption float. Mike looked at her, and then at the floating wagon, and then back at her.

"Thirty-one tests," he said quietly.

"Thirty-one," said Velka.

She didn't smile. But she leaned, very slightly, against his shoulder, and stayed there.

At the end of the clients' bench, Nettle was dripping wet, having been splashed along with the first four rows. He was mopping his spectacles with a handkerchief. He put them back on, reopened his folder, turned to a page near the end, and cleared his throat.

"I should just mention," he said politely, "clause eleven. *The Guild shall be liable for the full value of any wagon, cargo, or client property lost or damaged in the course of the service.*" He looked out at the six wagons bobbing on the river, and the pies drifting downstream. "I believe that's six."

---

Priscilla rose to the occasion. Priscilla always rose to the occasion.

She climbed onto a bench, soaked from the knees down, her feather drooping, and spread her arms wide to the six clients, who were standing on the bank in various states of wetness and outrage, and smiled the brightest smile Mike had ever seen.

"*Well!*" she said. "What a *learning*!"

---

Rupert gave the root cause the next morning, at the stand-up, by the window.

"Team," he said gravely. "Yesterday's field test did not achieve all of its outcomes. And I want to be transparent about that." He looked across the circle, sorrowfully, at Mike. "We've done a root cause analysis. And the root cause is clear. The failure of the promise rune was a *known issue*. It was on the clerk's own list. Item three." He paused for effect. "Mike *knew*. And Mike didn't *escalate*."

Mike stood very still behind his pillar.

"I sent it to you," he said. "All eleven pages. You wrote back *love the detail*."

"And I did love the detail."

"I raised it here. At this stand-up. Item by item, for six minutes."

"And we took it offline."

"And I filed it with the Registrar."

Rupert looked at him with gentle, patient sympathy, as if Mike were a child who had not yet understood a simple and important rule.

"Mike," he said. "I hear you. But did you escalate it *properly*?" He leaned in. "Using *Form E-1*?"

There was a pause.

Mike had been the senior clerk for eight years. He had handled, filed, sorted, or invented every form in the building. He knew them all by number. There was a lumber guild form and a bounty form and a form for requesting a form.

There was no Form E-1.

"There's no Form E-1," he said.

Rupert turned to Tolliver. Tolliver, who had clearly been briefed, already had a quill out. He took a fresh sheet of paper, wrote across the top in large, neat letters, *FORM E-1: ESCALATION OF KNOWN ISSUES*, ruled three lines underneath it, and handed it to Rupert. The ink was still wet.

Rupert held it up for the circle.

"Form E-1," he said. He looked at it. Then he looked at Mike, with deep regret. "Which, I'm sorry to say, you never filled in."

The circle nodded slowly. Brannoc rang the gong.

That afternoon, a junior clerk came halfway down the Basement ladder, threw a scroll tied with a red ribbon, and fled. Durgan opened the drawer marked *MIKE* and laid it inside, beside the other three.

"Four," he said.

Then he looked at the scroll for a moment longer.

"Form E-1," said Durgan. There was a long pause. "Didn't exist."

"I know," said Mike.

"Wrote it after."

"I know."

Durgan grunted. It was, Mike had learned over eight years, the grunt reserved for things that mattered. Then he went back to his pamphlet.

In a clay pot on the corner of Pip's desk, a small cutting of the scope creeper, which Pip had rescued from the riverbank, uncurled a new leaf.

---

Mike went back up the ladder that night, late, to his desk behind Osric's stack. He lit the lamp. And there, squared neatly to the edge of the desk, was a small folded card.

It was heavy cream paper. It had no seal. The writing on it was in red ink, neat and precise, and the ink was faintly, unmistakably warm.

> *Tomorrow. Bring the records.*
>
> *— M.*

Mike read it twice. He set it down very carefully.

Then he looked at the shelves behind his desk. The Registrar guide, three hundred and twelve pages. The City Barrier guide, two hundred and four. The crypt estimate, eight years old. The work order. The incident record. The formal objection, with its two signatures. The inquiry findings, dated the day before. The eleven-page *Known Issues* list. The test log with thirty-one tests in it.

He had been writing things down for eight years. Every single day.

He asked the skill. He knew what it would say.

**Estimated time until the first payment to Malphus: 1 day.**

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 8 days.**

Mike turned the lamp up, pulled the first box of records off the shelf, and began to put them in order.
