# Chapter 24: The Audit

They set up before dawn.

Mike had carried the boxes up from the archive himself, one at a time, in the order they would be needed, and stacked them along the wall of the main hall like the bricks of a very boring castle. There were forty-one of them. Every one was labeled on two sides, in his clearest handwriting, with what was inside it and the dates it covered and the name of whoever had filed it, which was nearly always *M. Dobbins*.

Tam had a long table in the middle of the hall, with a clean blotter, three pens, a pot of black ink, and a ruler. He had been there since four. He kept straightening the ruler.

"You'll be fine," said Mike.

"I know," said Tam, and straightened the ruler.

At the front counter, in its little pool of lamplight, Malphus's hourglass had almost run out. There was a thin trickle of sand still falling, slow as ever, and a small heap of it at the bottom that had taken seven days to build. Mike had stopped looking at it. Everybody else kept looking at it. It was the kind of object that made you look at it.

Then, at a quarter to five, Osric's stack moved.

It did not fall over. It *shifted*, the way a hedge shifts when something is coming through it. A few sheets of paper slid off the top and drifted down. And then, from somewhere in the middle of the stack, at about knee height, a door opened that Mike had never known was there, and out came Osric.

The whole hall stopped.

He was very small. Smaller than Pip, nearly. He was very, very old, older than Thessaly, possibly, in the way that some people are old not because of the number of years but because of the amount of paper they have been near. He had wisps of white hair, and round spectacles, and his sleeves were rolled up to the elbow, and his arms were blue-black with ink from the fingertips to the elbows, as if he had been dipped. Under one arm he carried a ledger, thick and battered and bound in faded green.

He looked around the hall, blinking, the way a man looks around when he hasn't seen a room in a long time and isn't sure it's the same room.

From his post by the door, Gareth the Bold leaned over to Mike and whispered, "Who's *that*?"

"That's Osric," said Mike.

"Osric *who*?"

Mike opened his mouth, and found that he did not know. He looked at Pip. Pip looked at Durgan, who had come up from the Basement for the start. Durgan looked at the ceiling. Nobody in the guild knew Osric's last name. Nobody, Mike suspected, had ever asked.

Tam, at the long table, stared at the old man with his mouth slightly open.

"He's *real*?" said Tam.

Osric walked slowly across the hall, past the boxes, past the counter, past the hourglass. He pulled out the chair beside Tam's, very carefully, as if it might be a trick. He sat down in it. He opened the green ledger on the table in front of him, took a pen from behind his ear, and looked at the door.

He did not say anything. He didn't need to.

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated audit length: 6 hours.**

At five o'clock, the Champions arrived. Rupert came first, with Tolliver and Dex and Bramble behind him, carrying between the three of them a large easel and a new chart under a cloth. They came up the steps in a little procession, and at the top of the steps, Gareth stepped out in front of the door and held up one large hand.

"Halt," said Gareth. "No belts."

Rupert stopped. He looked down at the black belt with the gold thread. He looked at the door, and through it, at the long table, and the boxes, and Osric, and the hourglass on the counter. He looked at it the way a man looks at a stage where a prize is about to be handed out.

Then, to everyone's astonishment, Rupert Spindlewick unbuckled the belt, folded it neatly in half, and handed it to Gareth.

"Hold that for me," he said.

Gareth looked at the belt in his hands as if it were a live animal. He was still holding it, very carefully, at arm's length, when the first bell of the morning rang across the city.

On the front counter, the last grain of sand fell.

---

Malphus came through the front door exactly as the bell finished ringing, ducking his horns politely under the lintel.

"Good morning," he said pleasantly. "Exactly on time."

He was wearing the same dark coat, and carrying the same red ledger. He looked around the hall, at the forty-one boxes along the wall, at the long table, at Tam with his ruler, at Mike standing beside him, and at Osric. When his eyes reached Osric, they stopped for a moment, and something passed across his face, a flicker of something that might, in a less pleasant creature, have been surprise.

Then Rupert stepped forward with the easel.

"Master Malphus! Before we dive in, I'd love to just set the scene." He nodded, and Tolliver whipped the cloth off the chart. *INK STRATEGY: AUDIT READINESS.* It had boxes and arrows, and a wagon wheel in one corner, and was, as far as Mike could tell, the same chart as before with a new title painted over the old one.

Malphus looked at it.

"We've met," he said.

And he walked past it, to the long table, and sat down opposite Tam, and opened the first box.

---

He read everything.

Mike had known he would. It was still something to watch. Malphus took each box in turn, and opened it, and took out every single sheet, and read it, front and back, and put it in a neat pile, and made a small note in his red ledger, and moved on to the next. He did not skim. He did not skip. He read the way Mike had always wished people would read his work: as if every word had been put there on purpose, by someone who meant it.

And as he read, he talked. Not to anyone in particular. He read the line items out loud, pleasantly, in his calm, warm voice, the way a doctor reads out an X-ray, naming each thing he finds as he finds it, so that everyone in the room can hear exactly how bad it is.

"The Big Rewrite," said Malphus. "Replacement of the guild's records golem with a new model. Cost: considerable. Records migrated: some." He turned a page. "The OMNIFILE. Ten-year service agreement. Clause fourteen." He read clause fourteen. He made a small sound, like a man watching someone step on a rake. "Removable only by the manufacturer, at the manufacturer's rates, which are attached." He turned the page. "They are attached."

He made a note.

"Seventy-two colored belts," said Malphus. "White, yellow, green. One black belt, with gold thread. Three weeks' fees for a Sigil Sensei, traveling from out of town. Room and board for the same." He turned the page. "A tasting menu at the Silver Pheasant. Eleven courses. To celebrate the elimination of waste."

There was a small, polite cough from the direction of the easel.

"If I could just add some context," said Rupert. "Those were *investments in culture*."

Malphus looked up at him. He looked for a while. Then he looked back down, uncapped his silver pen, and wrote something in his ledger in red ink, and the ink smoked faintly.

"Debt," he said.

He kept going. Box after box. The crypt. The door. The Contraption. A sheep in a hat, for which there was, to Mike's lasting regret, a receipt. The roof of the armory, which had leaked for six years and been patched four times with a different kind of tar each time. All of it. Every shortcut the guild had ever taken, every quick win, every *just this once*, laid out on the table, in order, with dates.

And then he got to the end of the most recent box, and turned over one more sheet, and stopped.

"Cookies," said Malphus. "One platter. Punch, one bowl. Two dozen glasses, borrowed from the Silver Pheasant. A banner, painted, reading *GOOD LUCK, MIKE*, with a smaller banner beneath it reading *WE'LL MISS YOU*." He turned the sheet over, read the back, and turned it over again. "For a resignation," he said, "withdrawn the same evening."

He looked up. He looked directly at Mike.

"Debt?" said Malphus.

Mike looked back at him with a perfectly straight face.

"Investment in culture," he said.

There was a short silence.

And then Malphus laughed.

It was not a loud laugh. It was a dry, surprised sound, like a door that hasn't been opened in a long time, and it seemed to take him as much by surprise as anyone. He put his pen down. He put one long hand over his eyes. His shoulders shook. Across the hall, Pip grabbed Durgan's arm. Nobody, in three visits and two hundred years of guild history, had ever heard Malphus laugh. Mike suspected nobody in Malphus's own office ever had either.

Over by the easel, Rupert was laughing too, a beat late, loudly, the way people laugh when they think the joke might have been about them and want to be on the right side of it.

Malphus wiped his eyes, picked up his pen, and wrote, in red: *Culture.* Then he crossed it out and wrote *Debt* underneath, and moved on.

---

Then he came to the forms.

Mike had put them together, in a slim folder of their own, because they were the newest and because he had thought, privately, that Malphus would like them. He had been right.

"Form E-1," said Malphus, holding it up to the light. "*Escalation of Known Issues*. Invented..." He looked at the note Mike had clipped to the corner. "...after the failure it describes. With a note to that effect." He nodded slowly. "Honest."

"Form E-2. *Request for Extension of Payment.*" He smiled at that one. It was his own. He looked at the margins again, the way another man might look at a photograph of his grandchildren.

"Form R-2. *Withdrawal of Resignation.*" He read it through. He read the line under *reason*. *Asked the wrong question.* He looked at Mike over the top of it for a moment, and didn't say anything, and put it down very gently.

Then he took Form E-2 and slid it into his own coat.

"For my own office," he said. "If you don't mind."

"There's a copy in the file," said Mike.

"I'm sure there is," said Malphus.

It was going well. Mike could feel it. Everybody in the hall could feel it. Box after box, sheet after sheet, Malphus kept nodding, and making small approving notes, and once, at the inquiry findings from the crypt, saying "*Good*" quite firmly to nobody. The room had changed. People had stopped holding their breath.

And Rupert had noticed.

He stepped forward from beside the easel, smiling his warm, confident smile, the one he wore whenever a room started going well and he wanted it to be known that he had been in it.

"I think what we're seeing here," he said, "is really the payoff of the Ink Office's *records-first strategy*. From day one, we've been all about documentation. Ink is literally in the title." He spread his hands modestly. "It's been a real team effort."

Malphus looked at him with polite interest. Then he looked down at the table, at the stack of records he had already read. He picked up the top sheet. He looked at the margin. He picked up the next. He looked at that margin too. He went through twelve sheets, at random, from twelve different boxes, from twelve different years.

Every margin note was in the same handwriting. Small, plain, neat, ordinary letters. *M.D.*

"Master Spindlewick," said Malphus courteously. "Would you show me one record that you wrote yourself?"

Rupert didn't hesitate. His face lit up. He snapped his fingers, and Tolliver came running with a leather folder, and Rupert opened it with a flourish and laid out its contents on the table, one by one, the way a man lays down a winning hand of cards.

*GUILD BULLETIN: CONVERGENCE CHAMPIONS.*

*GUILD BULLETIN: UNSANCTIONED WORKSTREAM.*

*GUILD BULLETIN: HISTORIC DUKE ALIGNMENT.*

Malphus read them all. Slowly. Carefully. Front and back, the way he read everything.

Then he took a fresh, empty folder from his own coat. He opened it. He slid all three bulletins neatly inside. He closed it. He uncapped his silver pen, and on the front of the folder, in red ink that smoked faintly, he wrote one word:

*FICTION.*

He set it on the corner of the table, squared neatly to the edge, and went back to the next box.

Rupert stood looking at the folder for some time.

Across the hall, Gareth, still holding the black belt at arm's length, said, "Is that bad?"

Nobody answered him. Pip was writing a footnote so fast his pen squeaked.

---

At ten o'clock, Malphus reached the bottom of the stack, and took out the loan.

It was not one document. It was the thickest thing on the table: the guild's debt to Malphus, as it currently stood, every advance and every amendment, bound together in red leather with a clasp. He opened it to the last page and turned it around so that everyone could see.

> *In the event of default, the guild and its premises shall pass into the keeping of the creditor. A bell shall be installed in every room. The bells shall be rung at random. Forever.*

Below it was a signature: a large, confident flourish that everyone in the guild knew by sight.

"This," said Malphus pleasantly, "is the binding contract. As amended."

The hall was very quiet. Everyone looked at Mike.

And Mike, who had been waiting all morning for this moment, and all week, and in a sense ever since he was a boy working in a scribe's back room, asked one question.

"Which version?"

Malphus stopped.

He looked at Mike. And then, slowly, for the first time that day, he smiled: a real smile, wide and slow and full of something that, in anyone else, Mike would have called delight.

"Go on," said Malphus.

Mike went to the wall of boxes and took down the oldest one, from the very bottom, from his first year at the guild. He brought it to the table and opened it. And one by one, he laid out the loan: not the bound copy, but every separate draft of it that had ever passed through the guild's hands, every version of every page, in the order they had arrived, each one with a small, neat label in the top right-hand corner, in a handwriting that had gotten steadily smaller and neater over the years, the way he had labeled everything since he'd worked for Master Quillon:

*Final.*

*Final 2.*

*Final (Brannoc approved).*

*FINAL — ACTUALLY.*

They made a row across the table, eight pages wide.

Malphus looked at them for a long time.

Then he did something that nobody expected. He reached into his coat. And from somewhere inside it, he drew out a thin bundle of papers, tied with a red ribbon. He untied the ribbon. And he laid out his own drafts of the same loan, one by one, in a row beside Mike's.

Each one was labeled in the corner. In red ink.

*Final.*

*Final (Infernal Legal reviewed).*

*Final — DO NOT EDIT.*

*FINAL FINAL — THIS ONE.*

The two rows lay side by side on the table. Mike looked at them. He looked at Malphus. Malphus looked back at him, with an expression that Mike had seen before, many times, on the faces of tired people in break rooms, late at night, in another world.

"Your people too?" said Mike.

"Everyone's people," said Malphus.

They looked at each other for a moment longer, in perfect understanding.

Then they went through it. Together. Page by page, version by version, Mike's row against Malphus's, matching each draft to its twin, checking each signature against each date, the way you'd check two halves of a torn map against each other. It took forty minutes. Nobody in the hall said a word.

And at the end of it, they both saw it, at the same moment.

The amendment. The one with the bells. The one that said *forever*. In Malphus's row, it was there: *FINAL FINAL — THIS ONE*. In Mike's row, it was there too, with its own label: *FINAL — ACTUALLY*. And on neither copy, in either row, was there a signature on the amendment itself.

There was a signature on the page *in front* of it. A large, confident, sweeping flourish. On a cover sheet. A cover sheet that said, at the top, in plain letters:

> *Please sign to acknowledge receipt.*

"Guildmaster," said Malphus, without looking up. "Would you come here a moment?"

Brannoc came over from the side of the hall, where he had been standing all morning holding his empty mug, looking as if he would rather be anywhere else.

Malphus pointed at the cover sheet. "Is this your signature?"

Brannoc peered at it. "Yes."

Malphus pointed at the page behind it. The amendment. The bells.

"Did you read this?"

Brannoc looked at the amendment. He looked at it with honest, total, untroubled blankness, the way a man looks at a page of a language he does not speak.

"I never read anything," said Brannoc proudly.

There was a silence.

It was, Mike thought, possibly the worst habit of the worst manager he had ever worked for, in either life. It was the habit that had signed the crypt contract. It was the habit that had installed the night bell and hired Rupert and bought the OMNIFILE and wired the Duke's bell into the fire ward. It was a habit that had cost the guild nearly everything it had.

And for once, just this once, it was true, and it helped.

Malphus looked at the cover sheet for a long time. Then he took his silver pen, and in his own red ledger, beside the line for the amendment, he wrote something short. Mike leaned over and read it upside down.

*Not executed.*

Behind them, at the corner of the hall, Pip had stopped writing his footnote. He had tears in his eyes.

---

There was one more thing.

The amendment was gone. But the loan itself was still there: the original loan, the very first one, the one Brannoc had taken out years ago, when he was new and the guild was struggling, back when the clerk at the front desk had been a man named Edwin. And that loan, Malphus explained apologetically, had its own terms. Its own interest. Its own default. And its terms said that the debt could not be restructured. Not reduced, not extended, not renegotiated. Only paid, or defaulted on.

He turned his copy around to show them. It was very old. The clause was very clear.

*This debt shall not be restructured.*

The hall went quiet again.

Mike reached into his jacket. He took out the brittle brown page that had come out of Osric's stack three days before, folded inside a card that said *Edwin would have come*. He had carried it next to his heart ever since, without knowing why.

He unfolded it and laid it on the table, beside Malphus's copy.

It was the same loan. The same date. The same terms. It was Edwin's own copy, in Edwin's own small, neat hand, initialed in the corner: *E.W.* And it had one more line than Malphus's did. One clause, near the bottom, that had been in the original, and that had been dropped, somewhere, by someone, in one of the many later versions that nobody had read.

> *Debt may be restructured upon presentation of complete and honest records.*

Malphus read it.

He read it a second time. Then he picked up his own copy and held them both side by side, up to the light, and compared them, line by line, the way he and Mike had just compared the drafts. And when he lowered them, he did not look at Mike.

He looked at Osric.

Osric had not moved all morning. He had sat at the end of the long table beside Tam, with his green ledger open in front of him and his pen in his blue-black hand, and he had not said a single word. Now he looked up at Malphus. And for the first time in eight years, for the first time since Mike had first walked into the hall at twelve years old and heard a voice from behind a stack of paper say *Edwin knew*, Osric spoke in a full sentence.

"Edwin knew," said Osric.

His voice was thin and dry, like paper being turned. Everyone in the hall leaned in to hear it.

"Edwin knew this day would come," said Osric. "He kept it. He didn't trust the later copies. He said someone would need it, one day. He kept it for whoever kept the records."

He looked down the table, past Malphus, past Tam, at Mike.

"That's you," said Osric.

Then he looked back down at his green ledger, and didn't say anything else for the rest of the day.

Mike stood very still. All those years, all those times he'd heard *Edwin knew* from behind the stack and thought it was a joke, or a complaint, or the only two words an old man had left. All those years, it had been a fact. Edwin had known. And he had written it down. And somebody had kept it. And it had waited, for eight years, folded in the dark, for somebody to need it.

Malphus set the two copies down on the table, side by side.

"Complete," he said. He touched the forty-one boxes along the wall with a glance. "And honest." He touched Mike's row of labeled drafts with another. Then he sat back in his chair.

"Very well," said Malphus. "Let's restructure."

---

The new terms took an hour to write and fit on a single page.

The guild would pay back the debt. All of it. But it would pay it slowly, over years, at a rate that it could actually afford, which Malphus calculated in his head and Tam checked with a pencil, and which came out the same. There would be no bells. There would be no *forever*.

There was one condition.

"Every Friday," said Malphus, writing it down, "the guild takes on no new work."

Priscilla, who had been hovering at the edge of the table all morning, made a sound like a kettle.

"Fridays," said Malphus, "are for paying down debt. Not mine. Yours. The debt in your walls and your wards and your doors. Every Friday, you fix one old thing. You mend what's cracked. You write down what nobody wrote down. You don't start anything new." He looked up. "If you do that, every week, the interest goes down. If you don't, it goes up. It's very simple." He smiled. "Debt always is. It's people that are complicated."

Durgan, at the back of the hall, made a noise that Mike had never heard him make before. It took him a moment to realize it was a dwarf trying not to cry.

Priscilla had opened her pink notebook. She was looking at it. She had gone white.

"I have three things," she said faintly. "On Friday. That I promised."

"Then," said Malphus kindly, "you will have to un-promise them."

Priscilla looked at him. She looked at Mike. She looked, Mike thought, exactly the way he must have looked yesterday morning, standing in front of Velka in a big chair with a wooden spoon.

He went over to her.

"It's all right," he said. "Come on. Front counter. I'll stand with you."

The first person in line at the counter that morning was, as it happened, a farmer. He had come in about his wyvern. The wyvern Priscilla had promised to have removed, eight years ago, by Friday, and then by the next Friday, and the one after that, and every Friday since. He was not angry. He had stopped being angry years ago. He just came in, every now and then, to see.

Priscilla stood at the counter. She opened her mouth.

"About the wyvern," she said. "I'm afraid it won't be... it can't be done... *not on a Friday*." She paused. Her eyes went bright and hopeful. "*Unless*—"

Mike put a hand very gently on her shoulder.

Priscilla closed her mouth. She took a breath. She tried again.

"Not on a Friday," she said.

The farmer looked at her. He thought about it. Then he nodded.

"Fair enough," he said. "Tuesday, then?"

Mike asked the skill. **Estimated time to remove a wyvern: 2 days.**

"Not Tuesday," said Mike. "Thursday week."

"Thursday week," said the farmer, and put on his hat, and went out, looking more satisfied than Mike had ever seen him.

Priscilla stared after him.

"He said *fair enough*," she whispered. "Mike. He said *fair enough*."

"They usually do," said Mike.

She did the other two by herself.

---

Back at the long table, Hesper Vane had arrived.

She was the guild's contract notary, a tall, thin witch in a gray shawl who had been at the guild even longer than Durgan, and who looked, at all times, as though she had seen every possible way a contract could go wrong, and been bored by all of them. She kept a ledger of late contracts that went back two hundred years. Mike was in it. He had been in it since he was twelve years old, on his third day, for a blood contract he had never signed.

She sat down at the end of the table, took out a small stamp and a pot of wax, and began to file her nails while she waited.

Malphus slid the new terms across the table to Brannoc.

Brannoc picked up his quill. He reached for the bottom of the page, for the line where his signature went, with the same easy, confident, unreading swoop of the hand that he had used for every contract he had ever signed.

Malphus put one long finger on the paper.

"Read it," he said.

Brannoc stopped. He looked at Malphus. He looked at the paper.

"*All* of it?" said Brannoc.

"All of it."

Brannoc looked around the hall, as if hoping for rescue. Nobody rescued him. So he put down the quill, and pulled the page toward him, and began, for what Mike was fairly sure was the first time in his entire life, to read a contract.

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time until Brannoc finishes reading: 40 minutes.**

It was one page.

Brannoc read it slowly, with his finger under each word, the way children read. His lips moved. Every so often he stopped and frowned and went back to the start of a sentence. Twice he looked up and asked what a word meant, and Tam told him. Nobody else in the hall moved. They just watched the Guildmaster of the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor read, and it was, Mike thought, one of the most remarkable things he had ever seen.

About halfway down the page, Brannoc stopped.

He looked up. His face had gone a strange color.

"Wait," he said slowly. "Wait. Did I sign the *crypt contract* like this?"

Hesper Vane did not look up from her nails.

"Yes," she said.

Brannoc stared at her. He looked down at the page. He looked back up at her. For a long moment, something moved behind his face, something heavy, slowly turning over, like a stone being lifted after eight years to see what had been living underneath it.

He didn't say anything. He went back to reading.

It took exactly forty minutes. When he reached the bottom, he picked up the quill, and signed his name. Not with a flourish this time. Carefully. In ordinary letters.

Hesper Vane put down her nail file. She dripped a small pool of red wax onto the corner of the page and pressed her stamp into it. Then she looked up at Brannoc, for the first time all morning, with an expression of very slight, very dry, very bored approval.

"Old enough to read it," said Hesper Vane, "old enough to be bound by it."

She blew on the wax, and the contract was done.

---

There was only one box left.

It was the last box, the newest one, and it was the one Tam had made. It was the reconciliation: every record from every other box, matched and counted and checked against every other, every number cross-filed, every date confirmed, so that if anyone ever asked *was this paid*, or *who signed this*, or *when did this happen*, the answer was there, on one sheet, in one place. Tam had built it over three days, with Osric beside him and Mike checking. It was the thing on the board that said *TAM + OSRIC: records*. It was his.

Malphus opened it. He took out the reconciliation, and laid it flat on the table, and looked at it.

He looked at it for a long time. Longer than he had looked at anything all day.

Then he looked up, past Mike, at Tam.

"Nice margins," said Malphus.

Tam went bright red, to the ears. He opened his mouth and closed it again. He looked at Mike with an expression of pure, helpless joy.

And Mike, standing beside him, felt something that he didn't expect. It was very small. It lasted only a second. It was a little pang, right in the middle of his chest: the knowledge that someone had looked at a page and said the thing that had always been said to *him*, and that this time it had been said to someone else.

And then the pang went away, and something better came in behind it. Something warm and steady and quiet, that he did not have a name for, and did not need one. It was the feeling, he supposed, of watching something you'd built work without you.

He went upstairs to the war room. On the board, beside *MALPHUS AUDIT*, under *DONE MEANS*, it said: *Malphus says "nice margins."* He picked up the chalk and wrote *DONE*.

Then, after a moment, he added: *(to Tam)*.

---

He came back down at noon.

He knew it was noon, because Malphus had just closed the last box, and the skill had said six hours, and it had been six hours, to the minute. Malphus sat back in his chair. He looked at the forty-one boxes, and at the restructured terms, and at the folder marked *FICTION*. He picked up his brass hourglass from the front counter, and turned it over.

Nothing happened.

No sand fell. The glass was empty. Every grain had drained to the bottom, and there it stayed. The hourglass sat on the counter with all its sand at one end, and none at the other, and no time at all left in it.

Malphus looked at it with an expression of great contentment.

"No deadline," he said. "That's what a restructured debt looks like."

He stood up, and buttoned his coat, and collected his red ledger and his folders, and turned toward the door.

And the floor shuddered.

It wasn't large. It was a single, deep, slow tremor, like something very heavy turning over in its sleep, very far down. The boxes along the wall rattled. Tam's ruler slid off the table. Every candle in the hall, all at once, leaned the same way, toward the Basement hatch, as if a wind were pulling at them from below. And then they straightened.

Nobody moved.

From the Basement hatch came the sound of feet on the ladder, very fast. Pip came up through it at a run. He was covered in stone dust from head to toe, white as a ghost, and he had a slate in one hand, and his spectacles were crooked.

"The rune," said Pip. His voice cracked. "Mike. The rune. It isn't red anymore."

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time until the demon wakes: 47 minutes.**

Forty-seven minutes. Not tonight. Not tomorrow. Forty-seven minutes. Earlier than the skill had said four days ago, and earlier than the plan had allowed, by almost exactly the hour of slack they'd had. The hour was gone. He'd known it would get used. He hadn't known it would get used all at once.

"What color is it?" said Mike.

Pip looked at him.

"It's black," said Pip.

There was a long silence in the hall.

At the door, Malphus had paused, with one hand on the frame. He looked back over his shoulder at the Basement hatch. He looked at Pip, and at Mike, and at the forty-one boxes, and at the war room stairs, and the board that he could not see from here but somehow seemed to know was there.

"I'll wait," said Malphus pleasantly. "I'd like to see how this one ends."

He walked back across the hall. He took a chair from the end of the long table, carried it over to the Basement hatch, and set it down beside it, facing the hole, the way someone sets a chair at the edge of a stage. He sat down. He crossed his legs.

"Might I trouble someone," said Malphus, "for a cup of tea?"

Gareth the Bold, who had been standing by the door all day, and who was still, Mike noticed, holding Rupert's black belt very carefully at arm's length, hung the belt neatly on the hat stand, went to the kitchens, and came back with a cup of tea on a saucer. He handed it to Malphus with enormous dignity.

Malphus took it. He looked down at the saucer for a moment, at the neat white rim around the cup.

"Nice margins," he said.

And at the back of the hall, quietly, without any fuss at all, Sister Agatha stood up from the bench where she had been sitting all day with her hands folded, picked up her small cloth bag, and checked to make sure her lunch was still inside it.

Mike looked at her.

He asked the skill one last question, though he already knew.

**Estimated time until you need Sister Agatha: now.**
