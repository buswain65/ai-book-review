## Chapter 8: Day Nineteen

Bertram took inventory by lantern light on the evening of day sixteen, and it did not take long.

The twelve reinforcements had been inside the crypt for less than a day. In that time, they had eaten eleven days of rations for five people, drunk most of the water, and used the last of the lamp oil to warm a pot of stew they had then knocked over. What remained, laid out on a flat tomb lid in the Great Vault, was: one wheel of cheese, half a sack of hard biscuits, two skins of water, a jar of pickled onions that nobody had touched because nobody liked pickled onions, and a harp.

The harp had been left behind in the confusion. Nobody knew how.

"Three days," said Bertram, dividing the cheese into precise wedges with a knife. It was the longest sentence Mike had heard him say.

"We need three days anyway," said Mike. "Nineteen days. It's day sixteen."

"And then," said Sir Roderick, "we're still in a sealed tomb."

This was, Mike had to admit, a fair point. He had been thinking about it since the ceiling came down. The skill had been helpfully silent on the subject. It would tell him how long it would take to clear the crypt. It would not, apparently, tell him how to leave.

They did, at least, have a way to talk to the outside. On the morning of day seventeen, the pigeons came.

They came through the crack in the rubble, the same narrow gap the last one had squeezed through: first the Duke's, in the gray dawn, with its boar-on-a-boar seal and its familiar question.

**IS IT DONE YET?**

Then, at noon, Brannoc's.

**WHY NOT?**

Then, at teatime, a small white one with a pink ribbon. The note was in violet ink and smelled of perfume.

*Mikey!! Don't WORRY!! Gareth is leading a HEROIC RESCUE!! We've sold 200 tickets!! Front row is 3 silver!! XO, P. (Head of Client Delight)*

Mike read it twice. Then he took out a scrap of paper and wrote, in his smallest, clearest hand:

*Sealed in. All five alive. Need: rope (100 ft), food, water, lamp oil. Entrance blocked; do NOT dig at the main tunnel, it's unstable. — M.*

He tied it to the leg of Brannoc's pigeon, which seemed to know its way home, and released it through the crack. It squeezed out into the daylight and was gone.

The reply came the next morning, on the leg of the same pigeon, which looked unimpressed with the whole arrangement.

It was a form. A real form, printed on guild stationery, with boxes. Across the top, in red, was the stamp of the Guildmaster's office: **RETURNED**. Under it, filled in by a clerk in neat handwriting, it said:

> *Request for supplies: not processed.*
>
> *Reason: All requests must be submitted through the proper channel.*
>
> *Proper channel: Senior Clerk.*

Mike read it three times.

Then he sat down on the lid of a minor Varrenhold's tomb and looked at the form for a long time, in silence, while the lantern flickered and Sister Agatha hummed a hymn and somewhere above them, a river flowed.

"What does it say?" asked Velka.

"It says," said Mike, "that my request for help has been routed to the proper person for approval."

"Who's that?"

"Me."

Velka took the form from him and read it. Her eyebrows climbed. "You're the—"

"I'm the senior clerk. I process supply requests. I'm sealed in a tomb under a river. So my request is waiting for me to get back to my desk and approve it." He took the form back, folded it carefully, and put it in his satchel next to the original crypt estimate. Some documents, he felt, belonged together. "I've been in this exact situation before. Different world. Same form."

Roderick, who had been studying the ceiling with deep professional concern, turned. "Then how do we get out?"

Mike pulled the leather-bound book out of his satchel. Fifty-six pages now. Every room, every trap, every bricked-up door and pointless staircase, drawn and labeled in charcoal. He turned back past the vault, past the corridor of stone faces, and stopped on day eleven.

The page showed a low, round tunnel in the lowest level of the crypt, choked with silt, sloping down and away toward the east. He had found it while mapping the mule. Beside it, in his own handwriting, was a note.

*Drainage culvert. Old. Probably where Party 4's mule came in. Leads toward river. Not explored.*

"There," said Mike.

---

But first, there was the sanctum.

They reached its gate on the morning of day eighteen, at exactly the hour the skill had told Mike to expect it eight years ago, when he was twelve. It was a tall gate of black iron, rusted almost red, set in an arch carved with boars. Beyond it lay a short passage and the inner tomb.

Between them and the gate was the floor.

It was tiled in large, square, gray stones. Three of them, Mike saw, were very slightly lower than the others. Following the cracks with his lantern, he could see the thin copper wires running beneath the edges, linking each sunken stone to the next, and the next to the iron gate, and the gate, as far as he could tell, to the ceiling. It was the trap from day six, three linked pressure plates, but bigger. Much bigger. Someone two hundred years ago had liked this design very much and decided to do it again, at scale.

Mike drew it. All three plates. The wires. The gate. The ceiling. An arrow, and the note he'd written on day six, copied over:

*DO NOT DISARM ONE WITHOUT THE OTHERS.*

Roderick studied the page for a long time. Then he looked at Mike.

"All three," he said. "At once."

"On a count of three."

There were only two of them who could do it. Agatha's hands were too unsteady, and Velka had looked at the copper wire and said something about conduction that Mike had decided not to think about. So Mike took the left plate, Roderick took the right, and for the middle one, Bertram silently set down his three packs and knelt.

They each found the catch beneath their stone. Mike's palms were slick. Roderick's face was gray but steady. Bertram looked bored.

"One," said Mike. "Two."

"Wait," said Roderick.

Mike froze.

The paladin looked down at the map in the lantern light. Then he looked at Mike. "I just want to say," he said quietly, "thank you. For writing it down."

Mike swallowed. "Three."

Three catches clicked together.

Something deep in the walls went *thunk*. The copper wires went slack. Above them, very faintly, something in the ceiling settled back into the place it had been for two hundred years and stayed there.

The iron gate swung open by itself, with a long, rusty groan.

Beyond it, in a small square chamber carved with boars, the Third Duke of Varrenhold lay on a stone bier under a dusty stone canopy. He was mostly bones now, in the remains of a burgundy robe. On the bony middle finger of his right hand, glinting in the lantern light, was a heavy gold signet ring set with a boar.

Carved into the stone canopy above him, in tall, deep, unmistakable letters, all capitals, was his epitaph.

> HERE LIES ALDRIC
>
> THIRD DUKE OF VARRENHOLD
>
> ADEQUATE

The five of them stood and looked at it for a long moment.

"It runs in the family," said Agatha finally.

"Two hundred years," said Velka.

Mike, very gently, slipped the ring off the Third Duke's finger. It came away easily. He wrapped it in a clean cloth and put it in his satchel, next to the estimate and the form.

"Thank you, Your Grace," he said, because it seemed polite.

The Third Duke, being dead, did not say anything. But Mike could not shake the feeling that, somewhere, he was finding the service adequate.

---

They heard the rescue from inside.

It had been going on, as far as they could tell, since day sixteen. It reached them as a series of sounds through the rubble: speeches, mostly, in Gareth's warm, ringing voice. Cheering. A band, briefly. At one point, quite clearly, a vendor shouting, "Pies! Rescue pies! Get your rescue pies!"

On the evening of day eighteen, the sounds changed.

"MEN!" came Gareth's voice, swelling with heroic urgency. "The time for speeches is over! The time for DIGGING has begun! *Boldly*, men! Dig *boldly*!"

There was a ragged cheer. Then, faintly, through the stone, came the sound of twelve people attacking a pile of unstable rubble with shovels, picks, and in at least one case, a shield.

And under it, swelling, a harp.

"That's not possible," said Velka. "The harp's *here*."

They all looked at the harp on the tomb lid.

"He bought a new one," said Bertram, which was the second-longest sentence Mike had heard him say.

The music swelled. It was something stirring and martial, in a heroic key. The digging got faster. The harp built toward a crescendo, and the shovels rose to match it, and Mike looked up at the crack in the rubble and felt every hair on his arms rise.

"Everyone," he said, "step back from the—"

On the crescendo, the pile shifted.

It was a long, grinding, settling roar, smaller than the first collapse but somehow worse, because it was so completely avoidable. Dust poured from the ceiling. The crack in the rubble, the narrow gap through which every pigeon had come, filled with stone and vanished.

The harp stopped.

In the silence, from far away, Gareth's voice rang out, magnificently undaunted: "A *setback*, men! But heroes do not *yield*!"

The skill glowed gently in front of Mike's eyes.

**Estimated time for Gareth's rescue to reach you: 3 weeks.**

Mike looked at the cheese. Bertram looked at the cheese. The cheese, which had been divided with great care into three days' worth of wedges, looked very small.

"Culvert," said Mike.

---

They went at dawn on day nineteen.

The culvert was in the lowest level of the crypt, past the vault where they had found the mule: a round, black tunnel no taller than a crouching man, slick with river silt and smelling of two centuries of water that had been somewhere it didn't want to be. It sloped down into darkness.

They stood in front of it with their packs. Nobody moved.

It was, technically, part of the crypt. It was old. Nobody alive knew who had built it, or why, or where exactly it came out. It was, by every possible definition, legacy.

Sir Roderick stepped forward.

He held out his hand, and Mike, understanding, opened the map and gave it to him. The paladin held the page close to the lantern and read it: Mike's handwriting from day eleven, the drawing of the tunnel, the slope, the note. *Drainage culvert. Old. Leads toward river.*

He read it twice. Then he nodded, once, to himself, folded the page closed, handed the book back to Mike, and got down on his hands and knees in his clean silver armor.

"I'll go first," said Sir Roderick Ashvale, of the Order of the Lantern, who did not go into legacy dungeons. And he crawled into the dark.

It took an hour. It was cold and wet and miserable and narrow, and at one point Agatha got stuck and had to be pushed, and at another point Velka lit the tunnel with Illumination 1.0 again and made Mike swear on the map not to tell anyone. But the culvert sloped steadily down, then level, then up, and the air began to change, getting fresher, colder, and smelling of reeds.

Then Roderick, at the front, said, "Light."

They came out one at a time through a rusted grate in the bank of the dry riverbed, half a mile downstream from the crypt, into the gray, cold, beautiful dawn.

Mike climbed out last. He stood on the riverbank, filthy, exhausted, with a two-hundred-year-old signet ring in his satchel and silt in places he did not want to think about, and looked up at a sky he had not seen in four days.

The skill flickered into view.

**Estimated time to clear the Sunken Crypt: 19 days.**

**Elapsed: 19 days, 0 hours.**

It was the first time it had ever shown him both.

---

They walked back upstream along the riverbank in a ragged line, and they heard the crowd long before they saw it.

There were hundreds of them, gathered on the bank above the crypt mouth: townsfolk, merchants, children on their parents' shoulders, a pie vendor, a ticket booth with a pink banner reading *RESCUE! FRONT ROW 3 SILVER*. Below them, at the crypt mouth, the twelve reinforcements leaned on their shovels, filthy and exhausted, around a great pile of rubble that was very slightly bigger than it had been a day ago.

On top of the rubble pile stood Gareth the Bold.

He stood with one boot planted on the highest stone, his new crimson cloak billowing in the dawn wind, his sword raised to the sky, his jaw set heroically toward the rising sun. In front of him, on a small folding stool at the foot of the pile, sat a painter with an easel, working fast.

Off to one side, on her own folding stool, under her parasol, Hesper Vane sat with a ledger open on her knee. She had clearly been there all night. She looked up as the five of them came walking along the bank, and something happened to her face that Mike had never seen before. It might have been the beginning of a smile.

Gareth saw them a moment later.

Mike would remember it for the rest of his life. He saw the realization hit Gareth: five filthy people walking up the riverbank, from the wrong direction, completely unrescued. He saw Gareth's eyes widen. He saw, for one tiny instant, Gareth the Bold not know what to do.

Then he saw him decide.

Gareth flung his arms wide on top of the rubble, sword in one hand, and his voice rang out over the riverbank like a trumpet.

"*I HAVE RESCUED THEM!*"

The crowd went wild.

They roared. They cheered. They threw their hats in the air. Children screamed with joy. The pie vendor wept openly. And the painter, without missing a stroke, began quickly sketching five small figures emerging from somewhere behind Gareth's magnificent cloak.

Mike stood on the riverbank and looked at the rubble pile, and at the grate half a mile downstream, and at Gareth, and at the cheering crowd.

The skill offered no estimate. Some things, he supposed, it simply didn't measure.

On her stool, Hesper Vane dipped her quill and wrote in her ledger, in a neat, dry hand: *Day 19. Complete.*

---

She convened the tribunal right there, at the crypt mouth, at a quarter past seven in the morning, before Gareth had finished climbing down from the rubble.

Brannoc had arrived with the crowd, and he came striding down the bank, beaming, roaring congratulations, clapping Gareth on the back. Then he saw Hesper, sitting at the folding table Bertram had carried down from town, and his face changed.

"The tribunal reconvenes," said Hesper Vane. "The work is complete. The delay can now be measured." She turned a page. "The contract stated fourteen days from commencement. The work was completed in nineteen. The delay is five days."

"*Five days!*" Brannoc rounded on Mike, recovering instantly. "There it is! Five days late! The Estimator shall answer for the delay!"

Hesper looked at Mike over her ledger. "The Estimator," she said, "may answer."

Mike stepped forward. He was filthy. His hair was full of silt. He was holding a satchel containing a two-hundred-year-old ring, an eight-year-old estimate, and a form telling him to approve his own rescue.

"The delay is five days," he said. "It's the difference between the guild's promise and my estimate." He paused. "That's my answer."

There was a silence on the riverbank.

Brannoc opened his mouth. Then closed it.

Hesper Vane picked up the blood contract, unrolled it on the table, and read the penalty clause again, slowly, with her finger under each word. *The Estimator shall answer for the delay.* She looked at it for a long time.

Then she set it down.

"The contract requires the Estimator to answer for the delay," she said. "The Estimator has answered." She dipped her quill. "The tribunal finds the penalty clause satisfied."

"*Satisfied?*" Brannoc's face went the color of a ripe plum. "He *explained* it!"

"Yes," said Hesper. "That's what *answer* means." She glanced up. "I wrote it."

Brannoc sputtered. He sputtered for some time. Then Hesper went on, in exactly the same dry voice, as if nothing had happened.

"However, the contract also states that should the work exceed fourteen days, the Guild shall forfeit the full fee to the House of Varrenhold." She turned the page. "The work exceeded fourteen days. The fee is forfeit."

The Guildmaster stopped sputtering. He looked as if he'd been hit by the ceiling.

Before he could recover, Hesper took out a smaller ledger: the one Mike had seen eight years ago, in her satchel, with a line for every contract she had ever witnessed. She opened it to a fresh page and began to write the crypt contract's entry. *Date. Term promised: 14 days.* And in the next column, where every other contract in forty years had a later date written in a different ink: *Actual: 19 days.*

Then she paused. Mike watched her quill hover over a third column, one he hadn't noticed before. It was narrow and mostly empty, headed *Estimate*.

She wrote: *19.*

Then she looked at the two numbers side by side, *Estimate: 19. Actual: 19*, for a long, long time. Mike could see the rest of the page over her shoulder, and the pages before it. Column after column. Forty years. And in the narrow third column, where it was filled in at all, the numbers never, ever matched.

Until now.

Hesper Vane looked at the page for what felt like a full minute. Then she said, very quietly, to herself:

"Huh."

That was all. She closed the ledger, put it away in her satchel, and stood. But as she passed Mike on her way up the riverbank, she paused, and inclined her head to him a quarter of an inch, the way the Duke's steward did. Mike had the strong impression that, by Hesper Vane's standards, it was a standing ovation.

---

The Duke received his ring that afternoon, in the great hall of Varrenhold House, from the hands of Guildmaster Brannoc, who presented it with a long speech about heroism and did not once mention the five people who had actually fetched it.

The Duke took the ring. He looked at it. He slipped it onto his finger.

It did not fit. It was much too big. It slid all the way down to his knuckle and hung there, swinging slightly.

The Duke looked at the ring. He looked at Brannoc.

"ADEQUATE," he said. And then: "IT DOES NOT FIT. I REQUIRE IT RESIZED. THIS IS URGENT."

By evening, a new notice had been pinned to the quest board, stamped with a red URGENT, and Priscilla had pinned it at the very bottom, under the others, where it would stay for a very long time.

---

The medal ceremony was the next day.

The whole guild gathered in the main hall: adventurers, clerks, Priscilla in a new hat, the twelve reinforcements in clean cloaks, and a crowd of townsfolk who had followed the rescue so closely they felt they ought to see the end of it. At the front of the hall, on a new easel, under a velvet cloth, stood the painting.

Brannoc whipped off the cloth.

The hall gasped.

It was enormous: six feet tall, in oils, in a gilt frame. It showed the mouth of the Sunken Crypt at dawn, with the rubble glowing gold in the sunrise. Striding out of the crypt, cloak billowing, jaw set, was Gareth the Bold. On his broad shoulders he carried five exhausted figures: a cleric, a paladin, a mage, a porter, and a clerk. In his raised right hand, flashing in the sunlight, was the Signet of Aldric.

Gareth had never set foot inside the crypt.

The hall applauded. Gareth bowed modestly.

Then Brannoc unrolled a scroll and read the citation for the Medal of Valor.

"*To Gareth the Bold, Lead Adventurer*," he read, "*for courage beyond measure in the matter of the Sunken Crypt of Varrenhold.* For locating the hidden culvert, by which the trapped party was brought to safety. For securing the Signet of Aldric, Third Duke. For keeping the party on schedule through great adversity. For his constant communication with the guild throughout..."

Mike, at the back of the hall, stood very still.

He knew those words. *Hidden culvert. Signet secured. On schedule.* He had written them himself, on the slate at the crypt mouth, every afternoon for fifteen days, for the pigeons. Someone, somewhere between the slate and the scroll, had carefully copied out every one of his status reports, and changed the name at the top.

The hero's record, he thought, was just the worker's notes with the name changed. It had been that way at Synergex too. He'd just never seen it in a gilt frame before.

Brannoc pinned the medal on Gareth's chest. The hall roared. Then the Guildmaster raised his hands for quiet and made a short speech.

"The crypt," he said, "ran late. Five days late. And that cost the guild dearly." He let that sink in, and his eyes found Mike at the back of the room. "But heroism," he said, turning to Gareth, his voice swelling, "*heroism* saved the day!"

More roaring.

Afterward, as the crowd broke up, a junior clerk found Mike and handed him a scroll, tied with an official red ribbon, sealed with the guild seal. Mike didn't need to open it. He opened it anyway.

*Reprimand. For the delay in the matter of the Sunken Crypt.*

He read it once. Then he rolled it up, tucked it under his arm, walked to the alcove behind his desk, and lifted the trapdoor.

---

The Basement was warm and dim and smelled of tea, hot stone, and faintly of the stable drain. The runes glowed on the long wall. Pip was on his ladder. Durgan was in his chair, boots on the desk, pamphlet in hand.

Mike came down the ladder and held up the scroll.

Durgan lowered his pamphlet. He looked at the red ribbon. He looked at Mike. He did not ask what it was. He did not need to.

Mike walked over to Durgan's desk and reached for the reprimand drawer, the one that no longer shut. It was even fuller than it had been eight years ago. Scrolls spilled from it in a red-ribboned avalanche. He started trying to wedge his own in among them.

"Not that one," said Durgan.

Mike stopped.

The dwarf swung his boots off the desk, leaned over, and pulled open a second drawer, lower down, on the other side. Mike had never noticed it before. It was completely empty and very clean, as if someone had been dusting it.

Carved into the front of the drawer, in neat, square, dwarven letters, was a single word.

*MIKE*

The carving was old. The edges of the letters were smooth and dark with eight years of lamp smoke and hand-oil. It had not been made yesterday, or last week, or this year.

Mike stared at it.

"When did you..."

"Week you found the trapdoor," said Durgan, settling back into his chair and lifting his pamphlet. "Been expecting you."

On his ladder, Pip made a small sound and became extremely interested in a rune.

Mike set the scroll in the drawer, very carefully, by itself. Then he slid the drawer shut. It closed easily. It had plenty of room.

And that was the moment, as he was standing there with his hand on the drawer, that the grate in the wall boomed with Brannoc's voice, coming from somewhere in the hall overhead.

"...on time, I'm telling you! *Every* job! The Tanner's Row guild hasn't missed a deadline all year! And do you know why?" A dramatic pause. "Because every single morning, they hold a *daily stand-up*!"

Down in the Basement, Durgan lowered his pamphlet again.

Pip turned around on his ladder.

Mike closed his eyes.

The skill, ever helpful, chimed.

**Estimated time until the guild has a daily stand-up: tomorrow, 8:00 a.m.**
