# Chapter 3: The Basement

Mike arrived at the guild at eight the next morning, sat down at his desk, and faced the URGENT pile.

Overnight, it had grown. He was fairly sure of this, though he had no proof, because nobody had ever counted it. It now filled the alcove behind his desk from floor to shoulder height, a leaning, rustling heap of notices, complaints, and reminders about earlier complaints, all stamped in red. Somewhere near the middle, a mouse had built a nest. The nest was also stamped URGENT.

**Estimated time to sort the URGENT pile: 3 weeks, 3 days, 23 hours.**

One hour of progress overnight, Mike noted. Priscilla must have gone home early.

He began at the top, sorting notices into three stacks: things that were actually urgent, things that had stopped being urgent sometime during the previous reign, and things that had never been urgent but had been stamped anyway by someone who liked the stamp. The third stack grew fastest.

At 9:13 he reached the bottom layer, a compressed mass of paper so old it had fused into something like slate. He braced his feet and heaved.

At 9:14 exactly, the slab came up, and underneath it was a trapdoor.

It was made of dark oak, with an iron ring for a handle. Faint light leaked around its edges, and with it came a smell of hot metal, old stone, and, unmistakably, tea.

Mike looked at the trapdoor. He looked at the slab of fused URGENT notices in his hands. He thought about how many years it must have taken for the pile to bury it, one urgent thing at a time, until nobody upstairs remembered there was anything underneath.

*Of course,* he thought. *Of course it was under the urgent work. That's where they always put the maintenance.*

He pulled the ring.

---

A ladder led down into a long, low room with a vaulted stone ceiling. It was lit entirely by runes.

They covered the far wall in glowing lines, thousands of them, blue and gold and a deep amber, branching and rejoining like the roots of an enormous tree. Some pulsed slowly. Some flickered. Here and there, small brass plates had been screwed into the stone beside a cluster of runes, labeled in tiny handwriting: *FIRE. FLOOD. ROOF (NORTH). ROOF (SOUTH, DO NOT TRUST). CHANDELIER (WHY).*

Nailed to a post at the foot of the ladder was a wooden sign:

**WARD MAINTENANCE.**

**KNOCK.**

**(DON'T.)**

There were two desks. At the nearer one sat a dwarf, broad as a barrel, with a beard braided into a long rope and his boots up on the desk, crossed at the ankle. He was reading a pamphlet titled *Ale Through the Ages, Vol. 4*. He did not look up.

At the far end of the room, on a ladder much taller than he was, stood a gnome in spectacles so thick they made his eyes look like two blue plates. He was holding a lantern in one hand and a slate in the other, and he was squinting at a single flickering rune with the expression of a man reading a very personal insult.

"Hello?" said Mike.

The dwarf turned a page. "Have you tried dispelling it and casting it again?"

"I... haven't cast anything. I'm the new clerk."

"Then that's your problem right there." The dwarf turned another page.

The gnome scrambled down the ladder with startling speed, crossed the room, and stopped in front of Mike, peering up at him through the enormous spectacles. He was about the same height as Mike, which at twelve years old was a novelty Mike hadn't experienced since he'd been eight.

"A visitor!" said the gnome. "We don't get visitors. Footnote: we have never had a visitor. Footnote: Edwin came down once, but he was lost, and he left immediately. Footnote: he did not close the trapdoor."

"Pip," said the dwarf, without looking up. "Rule."

"It's not *lunch* yet, I know, I *know*, I'm aware of the rule—"

"No footnotes before lunch," said the dwarf to Mike, by way of explanation. "He breaks it every day."

"I am Pipwick Fizzlethorn," said the gnome, "Senior Ward Analyst, Ward Maintenance Department. That's Durgan Flintmantle, Rune-Tender, also Ward Maintenance Department. The department is us. Footnote one: nobody up there ever listens to anything we say."

"Two," said Durgan.

"That was *one*."

"Before lunch. That's the fourth."

Pip opened his mouth, closed it, and wrote something on his slate with great dignity.

Durgan finally lowered the pamphlet and looked Mike over. His eyes were small and dark and much sharper than his posture. "Clerk, eh? They sent you down?"

"No. I found the trapdoor. It was under the URGENT pile."

Durgan and Pip exchanged a long look.

"Under it," said Durgan.

"At the very bottom."

"Well," said Durgan, swinging his boots off the desk. "That explains a great deal. Tea?"

He produced a dented iron kettle from a hook beside the rune wall and held it up against a fat cluster of amber runes labeled *FIRE (MAIN)*. The kettle began to steam almost at once.

"Is that safe?" Mike asked.

"It's spare capacity," said Durgan. "Fire ward's built for a dragon attack. Hall's had one dragon attack in two hundred years. Shame to waste it."

The tea was black, bitter, and slightly smoky, and it tasted faintly of copper. Mike drank all of it. It was, he thought, the best cup of anything he had been handed in two lifetimes, because for the first time, somebody had handed it to him without asking him for something first.

---

They were on their second cup when the voice came down the grate.

There was an iron grille set high in the far wall, leading up to the street, and through it, Mike realized, came the noise of the square: cartwheels, gulls, a vendor selling eel pies. And then, very loud and very close, a bell, followed by a voice built for shouting across marketplaces.

"HEAR YE, HEAR YE! The Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor is PROUD to announce that it will extend its protective wards to cover the NEW GUILD STABLES, completed by this FRIDAY! HEAR YE!"

The bell rang again and moved off.

In the basement, nobody spoke.

Pip had gone pale. He set down his teacup very carefully. "The new stables," he said.

"Didn't know there *were* new stables," said Durgan.

"By *Friday*."

"Didn't know there was a Friday," said Durgan. "Not one with my name on it."

Mike looked from one to the other. "Nobody told you?"

"Nobody tells us anything," said Pip. "We find out from the crier, mostly. Footnote: and the gulls, occasionally, but they're less reliable. Footnote: they mostly announce fish."

"Kept in the dark," said Durgan, "and fed manure."

Mike nodded sympathetically. He had worked at three companies where the engineers learned about new product launches from the press release.

Then he noticed the smell.

It had been there since he came down, under the tea, but he'd taken it for damp. Now that he paid attention, it was unmistakable, and it was coming from a large drainpipe that ran along the near wall and disappeared into the floor.

"Stable drain," said Durgan, following his gaze. "The old one. Runs right past us." He sipped his tea. "I wasn't speaking figuratively."

As if it had been listening, the bell came back.

"HEAR YE, HEAR YE! The Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor is PROUD to announce EXTENDED HOURS for its WARD MAINTENANCE DEPARTMENT, now serving the public from DAWN until MIDNIGHT! HEAR YE!"

Pip made a high, thin noise. "*Midnight?* We're available until *midnight?* Since *when?* How are we supposed to—"

"Have you tried dispelling it and casting it again?" said Durgan.

"You can't dispel a *schedule*."

"Not with that attitude."

---

"So," Mike said, when he had moved his stool upwind. "What do you actually do down here?"

Pip lit up like one of his runes. "Everything," he said. He took Mike by the sleeve and led him to the glowing wall. "*This* is the ward board. Every protective enchantment in the guild hall runs through here. Fire wards, so the building doesn't burn. Flood wards, so the cellar doesn't drown. Load wards, so the roof stays up. Footnote: the south roof is held up almost entirely by enchantment and spite."

"Mostly spite," said Durgan.

"If any of these fail," Pip went on, "the hall burns, or floods, or falls down. If all of them work, nothing happens at all, and nobody upstairs ever notices we exist." He beamed. "So, mostly, nobody upstairs ever notices we exist."

As if on cue, a cluster of blue runes near the ceiling began to stutter, flicking on and off with a faint, irritable buzz.

Pip spun around. "*No.* Not the flood ward. Not again. That cluster has been unstable for *weeks*, I've written three separate analyses, I've traced the fault through four separate junctions, I think it's a harmonic interference pattern originating from the—"

Durgan, still seated, still holding his tea, raised his free hand toward the wall, made a small, lazy gesture, said a single word that sounded like a door closing, and then a second word that sounded like it opening again.

The flickering runes went dark. Then they came back on, steady and bright, humming contentedly.

Pip stared at them. Then at Durgan. Then at his slate, which was covered in tiny, furious calculations.

"Dispelled it," said Durgan. "Cast it again."

"That shouldn't *work*," said Pip.

"Works every time."

"There's no *theory* behind it."

"There's eleven years behind it." Durgan blew on his tea. "Most problems are just a ward that got itself confused. You let it forget, then you remind it."

Mike, who had once spent a full day debugging a server problem that was eventually solved by unplugging it and plugging it back in, said nothing, out of professional respect.

He looked along the wall. Most of the rune lines branched and looped and connected to each other in a great glowing net. But one line was different. It was older than the rest, a dull reddish gold, and it ran by itself along the base of the wall, up a stone pillar, across the ceiling, and away toward the front of the building, where Mike was fairly sure the main doors stood. At the doorframe it seemed to turn downward, into the floor, and down out of sight.

"What's that one?" he asked.

Durgan put his tea down. It was the first time Mike had seen him put anything down quickly.

"Don't touch that one," Durgan said. "Nobody touches that one."

"What does it do?"

"Footnote nine," said Pip, in a low, eager voice. "The original wardings of the guild hall were laid down over a pre-existing structure whose purpose is not recorded in any surviving—"

"Pip."

"—document, but according to the oldest building surveys, the foundation directly beneath the front doors contains—"

"*Pip.*"

Pip stopped. He looked at Durgan. Then, reluctantly, he looked at his slate and drew a line under something.

"After lunch," said Durgan.

Mike looked at the old red-gold line where it disappeared beneath the doors, thought about the faded runes he'd traced with his fingers the night before, and decided he would very much like to hear the rest of footnote nine. Possibly in a different building.

---

It was Durgan who explained the rest, later, after lunch, which Pip spent catching up on every footnote he had been saving since breakfast.

"The job's simple," Durgan said. "When the wards work, nobody upstairs knows we're here. When they fail, it's our fault."

"Do they fail often?"

"Only when someone upstairs has an idea." He pulled open a desk drawer. It did not so much open as erupt. Scrolls spilled out onto the floor: dozens of them, tied with official red ribbons, all bearing the guild seal.

"Reprimands," said Durgan. "Last year, Brannoc wanted an enchanted chandelier for the main hall. Lights itself, dims at night, plays a little tune when a hero comes in. *Quick job*, he said. Hook it into the fire ward, he said, it's just a bit of light." He nudged a scroll with his boot. "Fire ward wasn't built for chandeliers. Wards flickered for a month. Chandelier set fire to a bard."

"Was the bard all right?"

"Bard was fine. Lute wasn't." Durgan shrugged. "So Brannoc sent me a reprimand. *Fragile wards*, it said. *Lack of resilience in the Basement's work.*" He kicked the scrolls back toward the drawer. "Drawer doesn't shut anymore. Been meaning to ask for a bigger one. Figured that'd get me a reprimand too."

Mike looked at the scrolls, then at the steady, glowing wall that had kept a building full of loud, reckless people from burning, flooding, or collapsing for longer than he'd been alive. He thought of the incident report that had blamed his last deployment for his own death.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it.

Durgan grunted. It was, Mike would later learn, the dwarven grunt reserved for things that mattered.

The bell rang a third time, slower now, and the crier's voice dropped into something solemn.

"HEAR YE. The Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor MOURNS the passing of its BELOVED rune-tender, DURGAN FLINTMANTLE, after many years of faithful service. HEAR YE."

Durgan turned a page of his pamphlet. "I'm not dead."

"Footnote," said Pip. "He isn't."

"Where would they even get that?" Mike asked.

Durgan considered. "Last time anyone upstairs saw me, I expect."

---

In the middle of the afternoon, Mike took a folded sheet of paper out of his pocket and laid it on Pip's desk.

*The Registrar: A Guide. Page 1.*

Under the title, Edwin's note. Under that, in Mike's neat scribe's hand, every rule he'd learned so far: the black ink, the three knocks, the left side, the number seven, the humming, the four knocks and "on behalf of."

Pip read it. Then he read it again. He took off his spectacles, cleaned them on his shirt, put them back on, and read it a third time.

When he finally looked up, his voice was very small.

"You... write things *down*?"

"I try to."

"Up *there*?"

"Nobody else does. So somebody has to."

Pip made a sound like a kettle that had been asked a deeply personal question. He turned away and became very interested in a rune.

Durgan pushed a spare stool toward Mike with his boot. It was a battered three-legged thing with *VISITOR (THEORETICAL)* carved into the seat.

"Sit," he said. "You've earned the stool."

Mike sat. Then he had an idea.

"The stables," he said. "The crier said the guild will ward them by Friday. Has anybody actually *asked* you to do it?"

"Never do," said Durgan.

"Then I'll ask."

He climbed the ladder, went to the back room, knocked three times on the lectern, and addressed the Esteemed Registrar. He filed a work order, in black ink, for *Wards to be extended to the new guild stables*. In the space for *Assigned to*, he wrote *Ward Maintenance Department (Durgan Flintmantle, Pipwick Fizzlethorn)*. In the space for *Requested by*, he wrote *Guildmaster Brannoc, per town crier*.

The great brass stamp came down. **BOOM.**

"FILED," said the Registrar.

Mike brought the stamped copy back down the ladder and handed it to Pip.

Pip held it in both hands as if it might break. "It has our names on it," he whispered.

"It's a work order. So there's a record that you were asked. And when."

"Nobody has ever..." Pip's eyes, already huge behind the spectacles, were swimming. "Footnote: excuse me. Footnote: I have something in my eye. Footnote: it is gratitude."

Durgan cleared his throat roughly and looked at the ceiling. "How long's it take, then, d'you reckon? Stables."

The skill answered before Mike could even think about it.

**Estimated time to extend wards to the new stables: 9 days, 2 hours.**

"Just over nine days," Mike said. It seemed safer to round.

Pip took out his slate and chalk and began to calculate furiously: wall lengths, rune densities, junction loads, a long column of figures that spilled over onto the back of the slate. It took him ten minutes. When he finished, he stared at the result for a long time.

"Nine days," he said slowly. "And two hours."

He looked up at Mike. It was not quite suspicion. It was the look of a man who had just watched a stranger solve his favorite puzzle by glancing at it.

"Lucky guess," Mike said.

"Footnote," said Pip quietly. "It was not."

---

It was nearly closing time when the voice came down the grate again.

Not the crier this time. A deeper voice, louder, closer, a voice like a barn collapsing in slow motion. It seemed to be coming from the main hall directly overhead, booming through floorboards that had clearly never been designed to keep anything out.

"Has anyone seen the new clerk?" roared Guildmaster Brannoc. "Or... whoever it is that does the lights?"

The basement went quiet.

"Whoever it is," Pip repeated faintly, "that does the lights."

Durgan did not look up from his pamphlet. "Eleven years," he said.

From above, louder: "CLERK! I want an estimate on the Sunken Crypt! Tomorrow morning, first thing!"

Footsteps thudded away overhead, followed by the slam of an office door.

And in front of Mike's eyes, for the first time in two days, a familiar line of glowing text drifted into view. It had been counting down quietly since a beige waiting room a lifetime ago.

**Estimated time until you regret this: 0 years, 0 months, 1 day.**

Mike closed his eyes.

Beside him, Pip patted his arm gently, the way you might comfort someone at a funeral, or at the start of a very long project.

"See footnote one," he said.
