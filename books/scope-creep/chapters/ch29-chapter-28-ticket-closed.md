# Chapter 28: Ticket Closed

Three weeks and two days after the harvest festival, a girl walked into the Adventurers' Guild of Brindlemoor for her first day of work.

She was twelve years old. She had scrubbed her face until it shone, and put on the one clean dress she owned, and walked across the city to the center of Brindlemoor with a reference from a weaver folded in her pocket. She stood in the doorway, under the carved stone frame that said *LOAD-BEARING*, and looked into the main hall with the expression of someone who has just stepped off a cliff and is waiting to find out how far down it goes.

Mike watched her from his desk behind Osric's stack. He knew that expression. He had worn it himself, eight years ago, in a different clean shirt, in the same doorway.

On his first day, nobody had met him at the door. On his first day, the clerk before him had quit and left a single scrawled page, and the only person who knew anything had been hiding inside a stack of paper saying *Edwin knew*. On his first day, he had spent the whole morning guessing.

Tam met her at the door.

Tam was still only thirteen, but he stood up very straight, and he had been practicing what he was going to say for two days. He shook her hand very formally. He told her his name and asked hers. Her name was Bess. He told her that it was all right to be nervous, and that he had been nervous too, and that the first thing to know was that nobody here was going to shout at her for not knowing things.

Then he gave her the Registrar guide.

All three hundred and twelve pages of it, and the fourteen-page index, and a single sheet on top that Tam had written himself, titled *The First Day: What Actually Matters.* Bess looked at the stack in her arms with an expression of slowly dawning horror.

"You don't have to read it all today," said Tam. "Nobody reads it all today. You just have to know where it is." He pointed. "And if you forget anything, there's a song."

At the end of the row of desks by the window, Bramble had already picked up his lute.

> *Black ink only, knock three times,*
>
> *never on its left side...*

Bess listened with her mouth slightly open. By the second verse, she was humming along. By the third, so was half the hall.

And when she came past Osric's stack on her way to her new desk, a small folded note slid out of the side of it, at about knee height. She picked it up and opened it. It said, in thin, shaky handwriting:

> *Welcome.*

Mike watched her read it. He watched her look around the hall, at the desks, and the clerks, and the song, and the stack of paper that had just said hello to her. He watched the cliff-edge expression slowly go out of her face.

Nobody had quit. Nobody had left one scrawled page. Somebody had met her at the door.

He went back to his work.

---

It was, otherwise, a completely ordinary Tuesday.

That was the remarkable thing about it, if you knew what to look for. Nothing happened. Nothing caught fire. Nobody stood on a table. The stand-up took fifteen minutes, and everybody sat down for it, and when it was over, everybody got up and went and did what they'd said they would do, and nobody rang a gong.

Upstairs, in the war room, the board had one list on it. It was ranked, top to bottom, no ties. Each item had one name beside it, and a *DONE MEANS*, and an estimate. On the right-hand side, there was still a column headed *NOT DOING*, and it was still quite long, and nobody had added *(YET!)* to it, though Mike suspected Priscilla had thought about it.

At ten o'clock, a farmer came in with a contract, and Tolliver took it to the round room and filed it with the Registrar, humming. He knocked three times. He used black ink. He stood on the right side. The old golem's blue eyes brightened, and the contract was filed.

Nobody clapped.

Tolliver didn't seem to expect anyone to. He just went back to his desk and picked up the next one. It was, Mike thought, the most encouraging thing he had seen in weeks. When you stopped clapping for something, it meant it had become normal.

At noon, Durgan came upstairs for lunch.

He did it every day now. He came up the ladder at exactly noon, with the medal on his chest, and sat at the end of the long table in the main hall, and ate a sandwich, and said nothing to anyone, and went back down. The first few times, people had stared. Now they just moved their papers over to make room.

And at a quarter past one, something happened that the guild had not seen in nineteen years.

Brannoc went down to the Basement.

Mike saw him go. He saw the Guildmaster stop at the top of the ladder with his mug in his hand, and hesitate, and look down into the dark, like a man looking into a lake to see how cold it is. Then he climbed down. Carefully. One rung at a time.

Mike, out of pure curiosity, drifted over to the hatch to listen.

He heard Brannoc's feet reach the bottom. He heard a pause. He heard Brannoc clear his throat.

"Durgan," said Brannoc. "I've got a question. About the east wall. I was thinking of putting up a new notice board, and I wanted to check first whether it's..." He paused. "Whether it's holding anything up."

There was a very long silence from the Basement.

Then Durgan's voice, deep and slow.

"Hm."

Mike went back to his desk. Item four, on the one-page list on the war room door. *Ask the Basement first.* He had not expected to see it happen so soon, or so awkwardly, or by Brannoc. But there it was.

Out at the stableyard horse pond, Velka was testing Float 1.1. Mike knew because he had the test log on his desk, in her handwriting, which was spiky and furious and covered in crossings-out. Three tests so far. All logged. All successful. The fourth was scheduled for this evening. He was planning to be there.

Her brown hat was sitting on the corner of his desk, where she'd left it that morning. Nobody had remarked on it. Mike was fairly sure everyone had noticed.

Gareth the Bold was at the front door, where he was every day now, standing guard in his purple cape. It had become his official post. Nobody had decided that. It had just happened, the way the best things at the guild seemed to happen now: somebody had been good at something, and nobody had stopped them.

At two o'clock, the Duke of Varrenhold dropped by.

He didn't need anything. He came in through the front door with his dragon tooth and the yellow flower, now dried and pressed, tucked into his hatband, and walked the length of the hall, and looked at everything with great satisfaction.

Priscilla, at the counter, asked whether His Grace would like a cup of tea.

"NO," said the Duke cheerfully.

A junior clerk asked whether His Grace would care to sit down.

"NO," said the Duke, with even greater cheer.

He stopped by Mike's desk on his way out.

"I'M SLEEPING THROUGH THE NIGHT NOW," he told Mike, in what he clearly felt was a confidential tone. "EVERY NIGHT. EIGHT HOURS. NOBODY OUTSIDE MY WINDOW." He beamed. "ADEQUATE."

"I'm glad, Your Grace," said Mike.

The Duke nodded and swept out, saying "NO" to a pigeon on the steps, apparently just for the pleasure of it.

At three o'clock, a bell rang.

It was loud and sudden and it came from somewhere at the back of the hall, and every single person in the guild, every clerk, every adventurer, Priscilla at the counter and Gareth at the door and Tolliver with his pen and Bess at her new desk, froze where they stood, perfectly still, with their heads up, like deer at the edge of a wood.

Then the kitchen door swung open, and the cook called, "Afternoon bread's out!"

It was the lunch bell. It was just the kitchen's lunch bell.

The whole hall breathed out at the same time. It sounded like a wave going back down a beach. Somebody laughed, a little shakily. Gareth sheathed his sword, which he had apparently drawn without noticing.

Mike sat back in his chair and listened to his heart slowly settling. The night bell had been silent for weeks. It would, according to the skill, be silent forever. And still, every one of them had heard a bell and stopped breathing. Some things, he thought, took longer to fix than others. Some things you just had to wait out.

At half past three, Dex came and stood in the middle of the main hall and looked thoughtfully at the front door.

He looked at it for quite a long time. He tilted his head. He looked at the wall to the left of it, and then the wall to the right of it, with the expression of a man who has just had an idea.

"Has anyone," said Dex, "ever thought about moving the d—"

Gareth moved faster than Mike had ever seen him move. He crossed the hall in three strides, cape flying, and slapped one large hand flat over the words carved into the door frame, *LOAD-BEARING. HOLDS THE SEAL. DO NOT MOVE*, as if covering a child's eyes.

Dex closed his mouth.

The hall was very quiet.

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time until someone suggests moving the door again: 4 years, 1 month, 3 weeks.**

He looked at it for a moment, and then he smiled. Three weeks ago, it had been four years and two months. The clock was still running. It hadn't been reset. Dex hadn't finished the sentence.

It didn't count.

---

The letters from Quint & Daughters came on Wednesday, together, in the same post.

The first one was dated three weeks ago. It was on heavy bronze-colored paper, with a little embossed golem in the corner, and it had clearly been held up somewhere, possibly under a stack of other letters on someone's desk, for a long time.

> *Dear Valued Partner,*
>
> *I'm thrilled to reach out in my new capacity as Chief Ink Officer of Quint & Daughters Automata! As part of our exciting new legacy-informed, convergence-driven partnership model, we'd love to explore REFRAMING your OMNIFILE service agreement to better align with our shared vision for the future of filing.*
>
> *Let's synergize!*
>
> *— R. Spindlewick, C.I.O.*

The second one was dated that morning. It was on plain white paper. It was very short.

> *Dear Valued Client,*
>
> *Quint & Daughters Automata regrets to inform you that Mr. R. Spindlewick has moved on to new chapters, effective today.*
>
> *Your OMNIFILE service agreement remains in force. Please see clause fourteen.*
>
> *— M. Quint (eldest daughter)*

Mike looked at the date on the second letter. He counted back to the date Rupert had started. He didn't need the skill. He knew the number.

Three weeks, two days.

He pinned the two letters together, the bronze one in front, the white one behind, so that anyone who ever opened the file would read them in order, and filed them under *Q*, in the drawer with the pamphlet for OMNIFILE 2.0.

That evening, walking home through the old part of town, he passed the Rusty Flagon.

It was warm and loud and full of light, and the windows were open, and as Mike went by, he glanced in, the way you do. And there, behind the bar, in a long apron, with a towel over one shoulder and a jug of ale in his hand, was Rupert Spindlewick.

He was refilling the mug of a large, red-faced man in a heavy gold chain of office, the kind that guildmasters wore. The man was complaining. Mike could hear it from the street. Something about his clerks, and how slow they were, and how nobody had any *initiative*, and how a rival guild across town was suddenly doing everything on time, and nobody could explain how.

Rupert was nodding. He was nodding with deep, warm, patient sympathy. And as Mike watched, he set down the jug, and leaned across the bar, and put one hand flat on the counter, the way a man does when he's about to tell you something that will change your life.

"You know what your guild needs, sir?" said Rupert. "What your guild *really* needs?"

The guildmaster looked up from his ale.

"A *Chief Quill Officer*," said Rupert. "Somebody over all the clerks. Somebody with vision. Somebody with a *quill-first strategy*."

The guildmaster stared at him for a long moment.

Then, slowly, he began to nod.

Mike stood in the street for a moment longer. Then he walked on, and did not look back. Somewhere in the city, he thought, another guild was about to have a very interesting year.

He made a note to send them a copy of the guide.

---

At the stand-up on Thursday, Brannoc asked for an estimate.

He did it in front of everyone. The old bridge over the millstream had a cracked pillar, and the miller wanted it mended before the autumn rains, and Brannoc turned to the end of the circle and said, "Mike. How long?"

Mike asked the skill. "Twelve days. Four people. Starting Monday, so it's not a Friday."

Brannoc nodded. He didn't say *can we do it in eight*. He didn't say *what if we add more people*. He took out a small notebook he'd started carrying, and wrote it down, *twelve days, four people*, in ordinary letters that anyone could read.

Then he looked up, and turned to the circle, and spread his arms.

"So!" he said, in his barn-collapsing voice. "The bridge! Twelve days, four people, starting Monday. That's the plan I've been working on."

The circle nodded. A few people glanced at Mike. Velka rolled her eyes so hard that Mike worried for her.

Mike didn't mind. He found, a little to his own surprise, that he really didn't. It was the right number. It was written down. It would be done in twelve days, by four people, starting Monday, and the miller would have his bridge before the rain. And if Brannoc wanted to stand in the middle of the circle and call that his plan, well. Mike had the estimate on file. He had all of them on file.

Brannoc was listening. He was also still Brannoc. Both of those things, it turned out, could be true at the same time.

After the stand-up, Mike went up to Brannoc's office to drop off the bridge paperwork. He put it on the desk, under the full mug, and glanced, as he always did now, at the wall.

Two hundred and thirteen gilt frames. Floor to ceiling. Red letters. *MEDIOCRE, MEDIOCRE, MEDIOCRE*, all the way up.

And in the bottom corner, by the window: *ADEQUATE.*

And beside it, now, the frame next door to it, which had been *MEDIOCRE* when he came in on Monday, and was not anymore.

*ADEQUATE.*

Two. Two frames, out of two hundred and thirteen. Nobody had touched it. Nobody had stamped anything. It had just changed, quietly, one night, sometime that week, the way the Duke's cursed seal told the truth about whatever it was stamped on, whenever the truth happened to change.

Mike stood and looked at it for a while. Two hundred and eleven to go. At this rate, he estimated, without asking the skill, the guild would be *ADEQUATE* all the way to the ceiling in about four hundred years.

He thought that was probably fine.

---

On Friday, Wren Halloway came to visit.

She came in through the front door at nine o'clock, past Gareth and his cape, with her sleeves rolled up and her mug of coffee in her hand, and walked straight across the hall to Mike's desk.

"Mr. Dobbins," she said.

"Guildmistress."

"I'm not here to steal you," said Wren. "In case you were wondering. The window will keep." She took a sip of her coffee and looked around the hall, at the clerks working and the song being hummed and the Basement hatch, open, with a ladder sticking out of it. "I'm here because half the merchants in Brindlemoor have started coming to Tanner's Row and asking for *estimates*. Real ones. Apparently your guild has been giving them out. And they've started asking what we do on *Fridays*."

Mike looked at her.

"I hear you have a board," said Wren. "In a war room. With a column called *NOT DOING*." She set her mug down on the corner of his desk, next to Velka's hat. "I'd like to borrow it. The idea, I mean. If you'd be willing to show my clerks how it works."

Mike thought about it. He thought about the best guild in the city, the one with the fifteen-minute stand-up and the guildmistress who cleared every obstacle by noon, the one Brannoc had spent years trying to copy by carrying an empty mug. Coming here. To learn.

"It's Friday," said Mike. "We don't take on new work on Fridays."

Wren smiled. It was a slow, approving smile, the smile of someone who has just watched a test come back clean.

"Monday, then," she said.

"Monday," said Mike.

---

On Sunday, Mike had dinner with his parents.

He did it most Sundays now. He had not done it nearly enough, in the years before, because there had always been a night bell, or a crypt, or a Duke, or a deadline. Now there was just Sunday. He walked across town to the little house by the cobbler's shop, and his mother Greta fed him far too much, and his father Hob sat in his chair by the fire and told him about the shoes he'd mended that week, one by one, in great detail, and Mike listened to every word.

After dinner, Hob walked him to the door. On the way, they passed through the little front porch, where one of the boards had been loose for as long as Mike could remember. It wobbled when you stepped on it. Mike stepped over it without thinking, the way he had since he was four.

Hob stopped and looked down at it.

"I'll fix that," said Hob. "This weekend."

Mike asked the skill. He didn't even mean to. It was the oldest habit he had in this life, older than the guild, older than Brunhild or the Goose or the mill. It was one of the first things he had ever asked the skill, as a small boy, standing on this same porch, about a writing desk.

**Estimated time until the porch is fixed: 2 years.**

Mike looked at the board. He looked at his father, who was standing in the doorway with his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and his hands in his pockets, looking at the porch with complete, sincere, total confidence that he was going to fix it this weekend.

Mike thought about the writing desk. It had taken six years and three months. When it finally arrived, it had been the most beautiful desk Mike had ever seen. He still had it. He used it every night.

"That's fine, Dad," said Mike.

He meant it.

---

He was at his desk late that night, behind Osric's stack, which was now noticeably shorter. Osric had been taking pieces of it down, a few sheets at a time, and teaching Tam and Bess what was in them, and filing them properly. You could see over the top of it now, if you stood up.

Mike was writing the estimate for the millstream bridge into the master ledger. He had the lamp turned low. The hall was quiet. Velka's hat was still on the corner of his desk, because she had come back for it at six and then left it there again, which he had decided not to think about too hard.

He dipped his pen. He looked up.

And the guild was gone.

He was sitting in a plastic chair. The chair was orange, and molded, and slightly too low. The walls were beige. The carpet was beige. The ceiling was beige, with a long fluorescent light in it that flickered, very slightly, every few seconds, the way they all did. Somewhere to his left, a water cooler made a deep, contented *glug*. A sign on the wall said *PLEASE TAKE A NUMBER*. There was a small potted plant in the corner that was clearly made of plastic and was clearly dying anyway.

Mike knew this room.

He had sat in it for four hours, a very long time ago, in another life, after a coffee maker.

"Dobbins, Michael?"

He turned around.

She was sitting at a small beige desk with a stack of folders on it. She had luminous wings, folded untidily behind her, and her lanyard said *GODDESS OF TRANSITIONS — TIER 2 SUPPORT*, and she had three pens stuck in her hair. Possibly the same three pens. She looked exactly as harried as she had the last time, and slightly more tired, and she was holding a clipboard.

"Hi," she said. "Sorry to pull you in like this. Won't take long." She checked the clipboard. "Ticket number four-four-seven-one. I closed it a bit early last time. Corporate flagged it in the quarterly review." She sighed. "So I'm just following up before we close it out properly."

Mike sat down across from her. He found that he wasn't frightened. He felt, if anything, the way he felt at the dentist: resigned, and slightly curious about what they'd find.

"Before we start," said the Goddess, flipping through his file, "I've got some updates on your old stuff. People like to know." She ran a finger down a page. "Hartwell Logistics. The routing feature. The one you were working on when you... you know." She made a small, sympathetic gesture in the direction of a coffee maker that wasn't there. "It shipped."

Mike sat up. "It *did*?"

"Three years and two months late," said the Goddess.

Mike sat back.

"What did they say?" he said.

The Goddess checked the file.

"They blamed the code," she said.

Mike laughed. He laughed so hard and so suddenly that the water cooler glugged in alarm. He hadn't expected to. He'd thought, if he ever heard about Synergex again, that it would hurt. Instead it was just funny, the way a story is funny when it's happening to someone else, a long way away, in a world you don't live in anymore.

"Also," said the Goddess, turning the page, "a Mr. Doug, of Hartwell, has been sending us messages. Quite a lot of them." She held one up. It was entirely in capital letters. "He'd like to know when you'll be back. He says it's urgent. He says it's been urgent for three years."

"Tell him not this weekend," said Mike.

The Goddess made a note.

Then she put the file down, and folded her hands on top of it, and looked at him properly, for the first time.

"So," she said. "Was your issue resolved?"

Mike thought about it. He thought about it for a long time. He thought about the guild, and the door, and the board, and the wall with two frames out of two hundred and thirteen. He thought about Brannoc on the fountain, and Brannoc behind his desk. About a twelve-year-old girl being met at the door. About a fish that came in twenty minutes.

"Mostly," he said.

The Goddess nodded, as though that were the best answer anyone had ever given her. She slid a sheet of paper across the desk, and a pen.

"Just a quick satisfaction survey," she said apologetically. "Corporate likes them."

It was a single printed page, on cheap beige paper. At the top, it said *POST-TRANSITION EXPERIENCE SURVEY*. Underneath, there was one question.

> *On a scale of 1 to 5, how likely are you to recommend reincarnation to a friend?*
>
> *1   2   3   4   5*

Mike looked at it.

Nothing was circled. None of the numbers. Not the five, not any of them. There was no pre-populated field. No helpful mark already made, to save him time. Just five numbers in a row, all of them waiting, and a question that somebody actually wanted the answer to.

He looked at it for longer than he meant to. He felt something tighten in his throat, and was embarrassed by it, and then decided he didn't care.

He picked up the pen and circled 4.

The Goddess leaned over and read it. "Huh," she said. "Most people say five." She shrugged. "To be polite."

"It was a four," said Mike.

She looked at him for a moment. Then she smiled, and wrote something down.

"There's a second page," she said, a little awkwardly.

He turned the sheet over.

> *Please rate your Tier 2 Transitions Agent.*
>
> *(1 = poor, 5 = excellent)*

The Goddess cleared her throat. She looked very hard at the potted plant.

"My quarterly review," she said, "is based on that one."

Mike looked at the page. Then, because he couldn't help it, because he was who he was, he asked the skill.

**Estimated time until this metric is gamed: already.**

He looked at the Goddess. She was still looking at the plant. Her wings had drooped slightly.

He thought about goblin ears. About adventurers paid by the ear, and goblins farming themselves, and a metric that had stopped measuring anything the moment anyone cared about it. About every survey he'd ever filled in, in two lives, with someone standing over his shoulder.

And then he thought about her. About a tired woman in a beige room with three pens in her hair, who had been the only person in his whole first death to ask him what he actually wanted. Who had stopped, when nobody in several thousand years had asked her opinion, and given an honest one. Who had told him, before she pushed the button, that the skill wouldn't make anyone listen to him, because it was true.

He circled 5.

Then, in the margin beside it, in small, plain, neat, ordinary letters, he wrote one word.

*Honestly.*

He slid the paper back across the desk.

The Goddess picked it up and read it. She read the 5. She read the word in the margin. She read it again.

She was quiet for a while.

"Nobody's ever written that," she said.

---

She put the survey in his file. Then she picked the file up and flipped through the rest of it, slowly, page by page, the way Malphus read a ledger.

"I've been keeping an eye on you, you know," she said. "Bit of a pet project." She turned a page. "The board. The door. The forty-one boxes. The demon's changelog, which, by the way, our records department wants a copy of. They're still migrating from the merger. They've been migrating for three hundred years." She turned another page. "The waiter. The twenty minutes."

She stopped. She looked up.

"The question you didn't ask," she said. "At dinner."

Mike didn't say anything.

"That one's rare," said the Goddess. "Really rare. People who get the skill, they almost never stop using it. Once you know you *can* know, it's very hard to choose not to." She tilted her head. "Why didn't you?"

Mike thought about candlelight, and a brown hat on a table, and Velka saying *don't*.

"I'd rather find out," he said.

The Goddess nodded slowly. She looked at him for a long moment. Then she put the file down.

"I can take it back, if you want," she said. "The skill. It's allowed. It was a utility passive in the wrong field anyway." She shrugged. "And you don't really need it as much now. Other people's estimates have started coming true. Your guild gives them out. Tanner's Row gives them out. Waiters give them out." She smiled slightly. "It's catching."

Mike thought about it. He really thought about it.

He thought about a life without knowing. Without the little line of text, glowing in the corner of his mind, waiting for every question. Like everyone else. Like Velka. Like Brunhild at her forge, who knew exactly how long a sword took, because she had made a thousand of them.

And then he thought about all the times it had told him something nobody wanted to hear, and been right. About *nineteen days*, and *seven days*, and *unknown*.

"I'd like to keep it," he said. "If that's all right."

The Goddess raised her eyebrows.

"I just don't need to ask it everything," said Mike.

She looked at him for a moment longer. Then she smiled, a real smile, tired and warm and a little surprised, and picked up a large rubber stamp from her desk, and pressed it down hard on the front of his file.

*RESOLVED.*

"You know what I told you," she said, "last time? That the skill would be accurate, but it wouldn't make anyone listen to you?"

"I remember."

"It didn't," said the Goddess. "Make anyone listen." She tapped the file. "You did that part yourself."

She reached for the large red button on the corner of her desk.

"Ticket closed," she said. "For real this time. Have a blessed rest-of-your-life."

---

The beige room went.

The plastic chair went, and the water cooler, and the dying plastic plant, and the flickering light. And Mike was sitting at his desk in the guild hall, behind Osric's shorter stack, with his pen in his hand and the ink still wet on the bridge estimate, and Velka's hat on the corner of the desk, and the lamp turned low.

And in the air in front of him, the way it had once glowed in front of him a very long time ago, falling out of a beige room into a new life, and then again on the morning of his twelfth birthday, in the doorway of this hall, there was a line of glowing text.

He remembered the first one. He remembered it exactly. *Estimated time until you regret this: 12 years, 0 months, 3 days.* He remembered watching it count down, all through his childhood, through the mill and the scribe's and the Goose and the forge. He remembered standing in the doorway in his one clean shirt as it reached the end.

*0 years, 0 months, 3 days.*

He had regretted it. Three days later, exactly on time, he had regretted it very much.

The new line said:

**Estimated time until you regret this: never.**

Mike sat and looked at it until it faded.

Then he finished writing the bridge estimate, and blotted it, and filed it, and blew out the lamp, and picked up Velka's hat, and went home.

---

On Monday morning, Priscilla was at the front counter.

She was always at the front counter. She had been at the front counter on Mike's very first morning, eight years ago, beaming at a nervous farmer and promising him a wyvern by Friday. She would, Mike suspected, be at the front counter long after he was gone. It was where she belonged. She was very good at it.

A merchant had come in. He was large and prosperous and red-faced, in a fur-trimmed coat with a gold chain across his stomach, and he had already been talking for some time.

"...a caravan," he was saying, "to the capital. Four hundred miles. Through the Gray Peaks. *Dragon-proof*, naturally. There are dragons in the Peaks, you understand. And I'll want a priority bell, for emergencies. And one of those little ovens, for pies, in the back. My wife likes a pie." He spread his hands. "Nothing fancy. And I'll need it by Friday."

Priscilla's face lit up.

It lit up the way it always had, the way it had on Mike's first morning, and every morning since: like a festival lantern, like the sun coming out, with pure, delighted, boundless possibility. She leaned across the counter. Her eyes were shining.

"Oh, *absolutely*," said Priscilla. "We can have that ready by—"

She stopped.

She stopped with her mouth still open. And then, very slowly, she lifted one hand, and clapped it over her mouth.

Behind Osric's stack, at his desk, Mike looked up.

Priscilla stood very still behind the counter with her hand over her mouth. The merchant looked at her, puzzled. Mike watched her close her eyes. He watched her take a breath, a long one, the way she had at the counter three weeks ago, the day Malphus made Fridays, with Mike's hand on her shoulder.

She lowered her hand.

"...Let me check the estimate," said Priscilla.

The merchant stared at her. Then he sighed, an enormous, heavy, put-upon sigh, the sigh of a man who has been having a very trying week.

"That's what *Tanner's Row* said," he complained. "I've just come from Tanner's Row. They said they'd have to *check the estimate*. And then they came back and said *eleven weeks*." He threw up his hands. "Eleven weeks! For a caravan! Is *everybody* in this city doing estimates now?"

"I think they might be," said Priscilla, sounding faintly surprised.

She turned around and looked across the hall, over the desks and the clerks and the humming of the Registrar song, to the desk behind Osric's stack.

Mike asked the skill.

**Estimated time to do this properly: 11 weeks.**

He sighed. He couldn't help smiling. A dragon-proof caravan, four hundred miles, through mountains, with a pie oven. It was going to be a very long eleven weeks. It was going to need a list, and a ranking, and an owner for every item, and a column called *NOT DOING* that the merchant was not going to like at all.

He picked up a pen.

He went up the stairs to the war room, where the board was clean, and waiting, and where there was always, if you looked closely, a faint gray ghost of everything that had ever been written on it before.

He picked up the chalk. And at the top of the board, in clear, plain, ordinary letters, he wrote:

> *WHAT ACTUALLY MATTERS*

Then he went back downstairs to tell the merchant it wasn't going to be Friday.

*THE END*
