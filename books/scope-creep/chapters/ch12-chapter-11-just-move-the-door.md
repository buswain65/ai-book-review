# Chapter 11: Just Move the Door

Brannoc announced it at the stand-up the next morning, between the three questions and Gareth's dragons, with the energy of a man unveiling a statue.

"The door!" he boomed, swinging the dragon tooth toward the guild's front entrance. "Look at it! Stuck on the north wall like a mousehole! Facing an *alley*! The biggest guild in Brindlemoor, and our front door faces a fishmonger's back step!"

Sixty people turned to look at the front door. It was a perfectly ordinary pair of heavy oak doors, iron-banded and very old, set in a deep stone frame. Mike had walked through it every day for eight years. He had locked it every night. And on his very first night, when he was twelve, he had run his fingers over the faint, worn line of runes carved around its frame and wondered what they were for.

"East wall," Brannoc declared. "Facing the square. Facing the *fountain*. Where people can *see* us!"

Priscilla stepped into the circle, glowing. "And I've already told the whole town!" she said. "Grand Reopening! Three days from now! With a band, and bunting, and His Grace will cut the ribbon!"

By the window, holding the dragon tooth he still had not given back, the Duke of Varrenhold inclined his head a quarter inch. "ADEQUATE," he said.

The circle applauded. Duchess the sheep, who had wandered in again, bleated. The dog wagged.

Mike, behind his pillar, asked the skill.

**Estimated time to move the door: 1 hour.**

He let out a breath. Then a second line appeared beneath the first, slowly, as though the skill had been thinking it over.

**Estimated time to move the door safely: 3 months, 2 weeks.**

Mike looked at the two numbers for a long moment. One hour. Three and a half months. It was, he thought, the purest form of the problem he had ever seen. It was the same door, in the same wall, going to the same place. The only difference between the two numbers was everything you couldn't see.

He looked across the circle. At the edge, by the coat rack, Durgan and Pip had been made to attend the stand-up again. Pip had gone completely white. Durgan was not looking at Brannoc. He was looking at the front door.

---

"After lunch," said Pip.

They were in the Basement. It was a quarter past one, and Pip had eaten nothing. He had been pacing in front of the rune board since the stand-up, clutching his slate, and now he stopped and turned around and faced Mike and Durgan with an expression of terrible, solemn purpose.

"Eight years ago," he said, "I began a footnote. Footnote nine. And Durgan told me: *after lunch*." He took a deep breath. "It is after lunch."

Durgan, at his desk, opened his mouth. Then closed it. Then made a small gesture: *go on*.

Pip went to the shelf beside the board. He had brought down a ledger from the Registrar's oldest stack the night before, with Mike's help and the Registrar's grudging permission: a heavy, crumbling, leather-bound book, so old its pages had gone brown at the edges like toast. He opened it to a page he had marked with a strip of paper.

"The deed," said Pip. "The original deed of sale for the land under the guild hall. Two hundred years old."

He held it out. Mike read it in the lantern light. It was not written like any deed he'd ever seen. It was written like an advertisement.

> *FOR SALE. Lovely plot near the square! Convenient for market. Good drainage. Quiet neighbors.*
>
> *(One demon, sealed.)*
>
> *Priced to sell!*

Mike read the fourth line three times.

"*One demon*," he said.

"*Sealed*," said Pip. "In parentheses." He turned the page. "The founders of the guild needed land near the square, and they had almost no money. This was the cheapest plot in Brindlemoor by a factor of thirty. Footnote: nobody else would buy it. Footnote: on account of the demon. So the founders bought it, and they built the guild hall right on top of the demon's prison. And to make sure it stayed shut, they made the guild's front door the keystone of the seal."

He pointed at the board. At the old red-gold line, the one that ran alone along the base of the wall, up the pillar, across the ceiling, and away to the front of the building.

"The runes around the doorframe hold the seal shut," Pip said. "And the doorframe holds up the front of the building. It's load-bearing. *Magically and physically*. The whole guild, every quest, every clerk, every bell, every stand-up, all of it is sitting on top of a bargain-price demon."

There was a long silence in the Basement.

"They built the entire organization," said Mike slowly, "on a cheap shortcut that nobody remembers."

"Footnote," said Pip. "Yes."

Mike had worked in four companies in his last life. All four of them, he reflected, had been built on top of something like this. A database somebody had set up over a weekend, in a hurry, with a password of *password1*, that everything else had been bolted to ever since. Nobody remembered why it had been done that way. Everybody was afraid to touch it. And it held everything up.

He had just never had one with a demon in it before.

"There's more," said Pip quietly.

They went upstairs, the three of them. It was the quiet hour after lunch, and the hall was nearly empty. Pip walked to the old front doors and knelt down in front of the doorframe, on the side away from the alley, with a lantern and a magnifying glass. Mike and Durgan crouched beside him.

The runes ran up the frame in a long, worn, unbroken line. Mike had seen them every day for eight years. But he had never looked at them through a magnifying glass, and so he had never noticed that, low down, near the floor, tucked between two runes in the same old hand but much smaller, almost invisible, there were words.

Pip held the glass steady. Mike leaned in and read them out loud.

"*Temporary*," he said. "*Replace before winter*."

Nobody spoke.

Mike read it again. He traced the tiny letters with one finger. They were cut into the stone with care. Somebody, two hundred years ago, had built a seal to hold back a demon, and they had known it wasn't good enough. They had meant to come back and do it properly. They had left themselves a note.

And then winter had come. And another. And another. Two hundred of them.

"It was always a patch," said Mike.

"Footnote," said Pip, very softly. "Every load-bearing thing is."

Durgan put out one broad, square hand and laid it flat on the doorframe, beside the little note, and held it there for a long moment. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Mike understood. Somewhere, two hundred years ago, there had been a Basement. And somebody in it had been told there wasn't time to do it right.

---

They went to Brannoc's office that afternoon, all three of them.

It was the first time Durgan Flintmantle had ever set foot in the Guildmaster's office. He stood just inside the door, in his old leather apron, with his singed braid and his hands at his sides, and looked around at the weapons on the walls and the one-eyed dragon head and the two hundred and thirteen framed ADEQUATEs with an expression of total, mineral calm. Pip stood beside him, holding the ledger. Mike stood beside Pip, holding his guide.

Brannoc looked up from his lunch, a roast pheasant, with mild surprise.

"Who's this?" he said, looking at Durgan.

"Ward Maintenance," said Mike.

Brannoc's brow furrowed. "The lights?"

"Nineteen years," said Durgan.

Then he explained the seal. He did it simply and slowly and without any footnotes, in a voice like rocks settling. He explained the demon, and the land, and the door, and the runes, and the line on the board, and the little carved note. He explained that the front door of the guild hall was the only thing between Brindlemoor and something that had been sealed under it for two hundred years. And that if the door was moved, the seal would break.

Brannoc listened to all of it, chewing pheasant. When Durgan had finished, he swallowed and wiped his mouth.

"Right," he said. "And how many times has this demon gotten out?"

Durgan looked at him. "None."

"*None*. In two hundred years." Brannoc spread his greasy hands, as if this settled it. "Then it's an edge case."

"It's never gotten out," said Durgan slowly, "*because of the door*."

Brannoc waved the pheasant leg. "Details. What's the best case?"

Mike, who had been waiting for this, said: "Three months. And two weeks. To do it safely."

"*Three months?* To move a *door*? The Grand Reopening is in three days!" Brannoc leaned forward, frowning, and lowered his voice to the tone of a reasonable man making a reasonable compromise. "All right. What if we only move it a *little*?"

"Still the seal," said Durgan.

"Half a door's width? Just so it's a *bit* more to the east?"

"Still the seal."

Brannoc drummed his fingers. Then his face lit up with inspiration. "What if the door stays exactly where it is," he said slowly, "and we just move the *wall*?"

There was a pause.

"Still," said Durgan, "the seal."

Brannoc sat back, frustrated. He chewed for a while. He looked at Durgan, and at Pip, and at Mike. And then something crossed his broad face that Mike had seen on the faces of many managers in two worlds: the pleased, crafty look of a man who has remembered a phrase he once heard an expert use and is about to use it back at them.

"Well," said Brannoc. "Can't you just *dispel it and cast it again*?"

Pip made a small sound, like a kettle dropped from a height.

Mike didn't move.

Durgan stood very still in the middle of the Guildmaster's office for a long time. His own line, nineteen years of it, the thing he said to everything: to clerks, to wards, to flickering runes, to Pip's panics, to a twelve-year-old boy who'd climbed down a ladder to say hello. It had come back to him in another man's mouth, across a desk, covered in pheasant.

When he finally spoke, his voice was quieter than Mike had ever heard it.

"No," said Durgan.

He let that sit.

"Some things," he said, "you can't recast once they're broken."

The office was silent. Even Brannoc stopped chewing. For a moment, just a moment, Mike thought he saw something get through: a flicker of the faraway look Brannoc had worn when he talked about the Wyrm of Kessel Ridge, the look of a man who had once, long ago, seen something that could not be undone.

Then it was gone.

"Let's take that offline," said Brannoc, and picked up the pheasant again.

They filed out. Sir Roderick was waiting in the corridor outside, armored, at attention. He had clearly been listening at the door, which a paladin was not supposed to do, and he looked as if he knew it.

"How old is it?" he asked quietly. "The door?"

"Two hundred years," said Mike.

The paladin's long face was grave. He looked past them, down the hall, at the old oak doors in their worn stone frame.

"Two hundred years old," said Sir Roderick. "Undocumented. Holding something up." He shook his head slowly. "Don't touch it."

It was the most anyone in the guild hall, outside the Basement, had ever said on the Basement's side. Mike found that he wanted to shake the man's gauntleted hand. Durgan, it turned out, did. Briefly. Firmly. Without a word.

---

The wizard arrived the next morning.

It was the same one: the thin, fast-talking man in the silver coat, from the capital, who had wired three bells and a wagon into the fire ward and never once asked what it was for. He came in through the old front door with a bag of chisels, a pot of rune-ink, a stack of new oak boards, and four workmen, and he whistled as he looked at the doorframe.

"Oh, *lovely*," he said. "Lovely old work. Very old-fashioned." He tapped the runes with a chisel. "Load-bearing, you say?"

"Magically and physically," said Pip, who had come up the ladder and was standing beside the door like a small, furious sentry.

The wizard laughed. "Load-bearing *door*? Never heard of one. Doors don't bear loads, my friend. Walls bear loads. Doors are holes." He clapped Pip on the shoulder, and Pip staggered. "Quick job. We'll have it done by tomorrow night."

"But the *seal*—"

"Oh, the runes? No trouble." The wizard was already unrolling a long strip of tracing paper against the frame. "We'll just copy them across. Same runes, new frame, east wall. Nice and tidy."

Pip went from pale to a shade Mike had only ever seen on Roderick at the stand-up. "You can't *copy* a seal," he said, his voice rising. "It's not a picture! It's *bound to its stone*! The runes and the stone were carved together, they were *set* together, the seal knows its own frame the way—footnote—the way a—"

"Mm-hm," said the wizard, rubbing charcoal over the tracing paper.

Mike watched him work, and felt the cold, familiar feeling of a man watching a disaster he had seen many times before, arrive on schedule.

He knew this. He knew it so well it made his teeth ache. Somewhere, in some other life, a contractor had found a block of code on the internet that did almost exactly what he needed. He hadn't understood it. He hadn't needed to. He had copied it, letter for letter, into the system, where it sat looking right and doing nearly the right thing for months, until the day it didn't.

The wizard copied the runes all afternoon. He did it very carefully, Mike had to admit. Every rune, every curve, every line, traced and transferred and inked onto the new oak frame that his workmen were building against the east wall, facing the fountain. He did not understand a single one of them. He didn't need to. They looked exactly right.

And near the bottom of the frame, between two runes, tiny and neat, carefully copied in the same hand and the same ink as everything else, he added a short line of very small letters.

Mike saw it as the wizard was cleaning his chisel. He crouched beside the new frame and read it.

*TEMPORARY. REPLACE BEFORE WINTER.*

He looked up at the wizard. "You copied the note."

"I copied *everything*," said the wizard proudly. "Every mark. You can't be too careful with old work like this. Never know which bits matter."

Mike looked at the little line of text for a long time. Two hundred years ago, somebody had written themselves a reminder to come back and fix this properly. And now it had been faithfully copied onto the new door, as if it were part of the spell. Somewhere in the new seal, between the runes that were supposed to hold back a demon, there was now a line that said, very clearly, that none of it was meant to last.

He had seen this before too. In code, they called them comments. Nobody ever deleted them. Nobody ever did what they said.

---

That evening, Mike went to the Registrar.

He knocked three times on the lectern, addressed the Esteemed Registrar, and in black ink, in his neatest hand, he wrote out a formal objection.

> *Change: Front door of the guild hall moved from north wall to east wall.*
>
> *Risk: Breaking of the seal on the demon beneath the hall.*
>
> *Ward Maintenance consulted: Yes.*
>
> *Ward Maintenance recommendation: Do not move the door.*
>
> *Overruled by:*

He left that last line blank, and took the form to Brannoc's office.

The Guildmaster read it through, slowly, with a frown, and Mike braced himself. But when he got to the bottom, to the blank line, Brannoc's face cleared, and he laughed.

"*Overruled by!*" he said. "Ha! Yes!" He seized his quill, dipped it, and signed the line himself. He didn't just sign it, he *signed* it: an enormous, sweeping signature that ran clean across the bottom of the page and curled back on itself in a great looping flourish, the signature of a man who had once killed a dragon and wanted everyone to know.

*BRANNOC.*

"There," he said, blowing on the ink. "For posterity, lad. Let them see who made the call." He handed it back, beaming. "Decisiveness! That's what leadership is!"

Mike took the form. He looked at the signature. It was the biggest thing on the page.

"Yes, sir," he said. "They'll see."

On his way back to the Registrar, he passed Hesper Vane in the corridor. She was coming from the notary's room with her satchel and her parasol, and she glanced at the paper in his hand as he passed, and stopped.

"May I?"

Mike gave it to her. She read it from top to bottom, including the signature, twice. Then she looked up at him over the top of the page with her pale, dry eyes.

"Would you like this witnessed?"

"Yes," said Mike. "Very much."

She took out her quill, signed below Brannoc's flourish in a small, neat, precise hand, *Witnessed: H. Vane, Notary*, and handed it back without another word.

The Registrar stamped it. **BOOM.** **FILED.**

And then, standing alone in the round room with the golem humming quietly beside him, Mike asked the skill the question he had been avoiding all day.

*If the door is moved, what happens to the demon?*

The answer came slowly, line by line.

**Estimated time until the demon stirs, once the door is moved: 1 day.**

**Estimated time until it wakes: 47 days.**

Mike stood very still. Forty-seven days. He had a number. For the first time since the stand-up, he had an actual number, and it was worse than any of the others.

Beside him, the Registrar's blue eyes dimmed and brightened, slowly, once.

---

The Grand Reopening was on the third day, at noon, in brilliant sunshine.

The square had been hung with bunting in the guild's colors, blue and gold. A band played on a little wooden stage by the fountain. There were pie sellers, and a juggler, and a man selling small wooden replicas of the new door. Half the town had come. They crowded the square in front of the east wall of the guild hall, where the new door stood. It was tall and handsome and freshly oiled oak, set into its new stone frame, with the copied runes glinting in the sunlight and a broad blue ribbon tied across it in a bow.

The old door had already been lifted out of its frame on the north wall that morning. The workmen had carried it out to a cart. The empty frame was being bricked up while the band played.

Priscilla climbed onto the stage. She was wearing a hat with a feather nearly as tall as she was. She made a speech. It was about vision, and boldness, and doors, and how a guild's door was its *smile*. Then, glowing, she raised one hand toward the crowd.

"And I'd like to give special thanks," said Priscilla, "to our wonderful Ward Maintenance team, who've worked so hard to make this possible! Dorgan! And Pippa! Stand up, you two! Take a bow!"

The crowd turned, smiling, looking for them, ready to applaud.

There was an empty space at the back of the crowd, by the guild wall. Nobody was standing in it.

The applause started anyway, warm and generous, and rolled across the square toward the empty space, and broke against nothing. After a moment, it trailed away.

"Shy!" said Priscilla brightly, and moved on.

Mike knew exactly where they were. They were downstairs, at the board, watching the red-gold line. He had wanted to be with them. But someone had to file the Grand Reopening paperwork, and someone, he had decided, had to see it happen.

The Duke of Varrenhold mounted the stage in his burgundy dressing gown. A page handed him an enormous pair of golden scissors. He looked at them, then at the ribbon, then at the crowd, with his usual expression of mild, aristocratic disappointment.

Then he cut the ribbon.

The blue ribbon fell away. The crowd cheered. The band struck up. And the Duke, holding the golden scissors aloft, delivered his verdict.

"ADEQUATE."

The crowd roared. Priscilla seized the padded mallet, which she had brought out from the hall for the occasion, and struck the great brass sales gong, which had also been brought out for the occasion.

*BWONNNNNNNNNG.*

Mike stood at the edge of the square, very still, and waited.

Nothing happened.

The sun shone. The band played. The crowd surged forward to touch the new door, to run their hands over the runes. A small boy put his face against the oak and said, "It smells like a tree," and his mother laughed. Nothing groaned, nothing cracked, nothing shook. The new door stood in its new frame and looked exactly like a door.

Around the corner, on the north wall, where the old doorway had already been bricked up, a man from a tour company was screwing a small brass plaque to the new bricks. Priscilla had sold him the old doorway that morning, for a very good price, as a *heritage feature*. Mike walked around the corner to look.

*SITE OF THE ORIGINAL DOOR,* the plaque said. *c. 200 years. PLEASE DO NOT LEAN.*

As he watched, a tourist in a straw hat wandered up, read the plaque, nodded thoughtfully, and leaned on it.

Mike looked at the tourist. He looked at the plaque. He looked at the bricks.

Then he went inside, and down the ladder.

---

The Basement was dark except for the board.

Durgan and Pip were standing in front of it, side by side, and neither of them turned when Mike came down. They had been standing like that, Mike realized, for some time. Since before noon. Since before the ribbon.

He came and stood beside them, and looked at what they were looking at.

The red-gold line was dark.

It was dark along the base of the wall. Dark up the pillar. Dark across the ceiling, all the way to the front of the building, where it had always turned and gone down into the floor. For the first time in two hundred years, it was not glowing at all.

The three of them stood there in silence for what felt like a very long time.

Then the line came back on.

It flickered once, twice, and then filled in, red-gold, from end to end, glowing again along the wall and up the pillar and across the ceiling. But it didn't run to the north anymore. It ran east now, toward the new door, the copied door, facing the fountain.

And it wasn't steady.

For eight years, Mike had seen that line glow with a dull, constant, unchanging light, like a coal that never went out. Now it was pulsing. Slowly. Brightening, then dimming, then brightening again, in a long, deep, unhurried rhythm, over and over.

Like something breathing.

Nobody said anything. Pip had his slate pressed to his chest with both arms.

Durgan walked to the far end of the Basement, to the corner directly under the old north doorway, the one being bricked up upstairs, with a plaque and a tourist leaning on it. He knelt, slowly, on the stone floor. He took off one glove. And he laid his bare hand flat on the flagstones.

He held it there for a long time.

Then he looked up at Mike. In eight years, Mike had seen Durgan bored, irritated, amused, angry, and, once or twice, very quietly kind. He had never seen him afraid.

"It's warm," said Durgan.
