## Chapter 27: The Audit

<br/>

"Counsel," said Mark, "will now review the proposal."

<br/>

He laid the thick cream-colored envelope on the workbench in the back room of the Fish & Hammer with the gravity of a man presenting evidence to a jury.

<br/>

Torren looked at it. Then he reached into the front pocket of his good apron and took out a pair of small, round, wire-rimmed reading spectacles, unfolded them with great care, and settled them on the end of his nose.

<br/>

There was a short silence.

<br/>

"You have *glasses*?" said Lyra.

<br/>

"For reading," said Torren, with dignity. "And close work. And legal matters." He opened the envelope. "I don't wear them in public. They make me look clever, and then people expect things."

<br/>

Mark looked at the spectacles on the end of the smith's nose, and felt a small, unreasonable stab of envy so pure that he had to look away.

<br/>

*"I had one perk,"* he thought.

---

Torren read the proposal the way he did everything important: slowly, out loud, one finger moving under each word, his lips shaping the long ones twice.

<br/>

It took forty minutes.

<br/>

Nobody interrupted him. Vespera sat in the good chair with her tea. Lyra perched on her stool with her arms folded. Garek stood in the doorway, because Garek had now been standing in the doorway for so long that nobody, including Garek, could quite remember him being anywhere else. Legacy watched from the frame above his head. Corwin lay on the long bench under the window with his eyes closed and might have been asleep, though he occasionally said "Mm" at the end of a clause, in a tone that suggested he'd followed every word.

<br/>

At last, Torren turned over the final page, read the signature line, set the document down, and took off his spectacles.

<br/>

"Well," he said. "I have notes."

<br/>

"Go ahead," said Mark.

<br/>

Torren cleared his throat.

<br/>

"First," he said. "It doesn't say anything about goats."

<br/>

"Torren—"

<br/>

"It's a contract for *Oakhaven*," Torren said firmly. "Half the business in Oakhaven is goats. You sign a contract in this town that doesn't mention goats, you're asking for trouble. It's like building a forge and not mentioning fire."

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth to object. Then he thought about the per-container goat tariff, and the charged goats, and the entire Ironhead Syndicate supply chain, and closed it again.

<br/>

"Noted," he said. "Goat clause."

<br/>

"Second." Torren tapped the page. "It says the Fish & Hammer gets a 'full Concord charter.' It says I get to be a 'Master Artificer.' It says nothing *at all* about Bessie."

<br/>

"Bessie is a blower."

<br/>

"Bessie," said Torren, "has worked in that forge for fourteen years. She was here before any of you. She's earned a pension." He folded his arms. "If they're going to take over the shop, I want it in writing that Bessie gets to retire somewhere warm."

<br/>

"She's a *blower*," said Lyra.

<br/>

"She has *feelings*," said Torren, and the room decided, collectively, not to pursue it.

<br/>

"Third." Torren picked up the pencil he kept behind his ear, leaned over the page, and struck a line through two words. Above them, in large, careful capitals, he wrote something else. He turned the page around so they could see.

<br/>

*Master ~~Artificer~~ NAMING Artificer.*

<br/>

"If I'm going to be a Master of something," Torren said, "I want it to be the thing I'm actually good at."

<br/>

Nobody could argue with that.

<br/>

"And my formal legal opinion," said Torren, putting the spectacles back in his apron pocket and folding his hands on his stomach like a judge delivering a verdict, "having read the whole thing, every clause, twice, is this."

<br/>

He paused for effect.

<br/>

"It's a Gerald," said Torren, "in a nice coat."

<br/>

There was a short, respectful silence.

<br/>

"Counsel's opinion," said Mark gravely, "is entered into the record."

---

But after the laughter had faded, nobody said anything for a while.

<br/>

Mark watched them. He'd sat in a great many rooms after a great many offers, and he knew the silence that follows a joke about a deal, when everyone has laughed and everyone is now privately reading the part of the deal that was written for them.

<br/>

Torren was reading the line about the Master's rank, and not laughing.

<br/>

Lyra was reading the clause that listed *the instruments designated L-1 and V-1*, and her jaw had gone very tight.

<br/>

And Vespera wasn't reading at all. She was holding the second page in her lap and looking at a single line on it, and had been looking at it for some time.

<br/>

*Mage Vespera shall be restored to the rank and honors she held in the service of the regional Chapterhouse, in full, prior to her departure.*

<br/>

Mark didn't know what that rank had been. He didn't know why she'd left the capital, or what she'd lost when she did. She had never told him, and he had never asked. But he could see from her face, from the absolute stillness of it, that whatever it was, she had wanted it back for a very long time, and had made her peace with never having it, and was now being offered it on cream-colored paper by a man with a gold ring.

<br/>

"Vespera," he said quietly.

<br/>

She looked up. Her face rearranged itself at once into polite composure, but not quite fast enough.

<br/>

"It's a very generous offer," she said evenly.

<br/>

"It is."

<br/>

"It would protect the Infirmary. Forever. Every patient in that line." She set the page down. "I'm not saying we should take it. I'm saying I understand why someone would."

<br/>

"So do I," said Mark.

<br/>

"I *don't*," said Lyra. Her voice was sharp. "He wants L-1. He wants V-1. He wants every clean thing we've made, locked in a box in his cellar, and he wants you to go *hunting* for people like Tessa Fenn." She jabbed a finger at the third clause. "'The location and correction of drift.' Corwin's drift. Vespera's drift. *Mine*."

<br/>

"Mm," said Corwin, from the bench.

<br/>

"I know," said Mark.

<br/>

"So we say no."

<br/>

"We say," Mark said, "that we'll decide together. And not today." He picked up the proposal, squared the pages, and slid them back into their envelope. "We have sixteen days. Aldric gave us until the new moon. I've never made a good decision in my life with a deadline sixteen days away and the other side's pen still warm. We take the time we were given."

<br/>

Torren nodded slowly. "That's sensible."

<br/>

"Sixteen days," Vespera agreed.

<br/>

It was, as it turned out, an extremely optimistic estimate.

---

The streetlamps went out at dawn.

<br/>

Mark saw it from the window of the workshop, where he'd fallen asleep at the bench sometime after three with his cheek on the ledger. He woke to the gray half-light and blinked blearily out through the high window toward the main road, where the long chain of blue Aether-lamps ran north toward the gate.

<br/>

The farthest lamp flickered and went dark.

<br/>

Then the next one.

<br/>

Then the next.

<br/>

He watched, not quite awake yet, as the lights went out one after another, coming toward him down the road, block by block, with the even, unhurried rhythm of a man walking. Not all at once, the way a fuse blows. *In order.* One lamp, a pause, the next lamp, a pause.

<br/>

He sat up very slowly.

<br/>

*"That's not a power failure,"* he thought. *"That's a traversal."*

<br/>

Outside, very faintly, a child's voice shouted a single word. Then another child's voice, further off, shouted the same word. Then three more, from three different directions.

<br/>

"*Gerald!*"

<br/>

"*GERALD!*"

<br/>

"*Gerald, Gerald, GERALD—*"

---

By the time Mark reached the end of the lane, the message bus had collapsed.

<br/>

He found Pip at the corner where the bus usually reported, standing in the center of a seething crowd of errand runners, every one of them shouting at once. There must have been thirty of them. They'd come running from every corner of Oakhaven, from the North Gate and the tanneries and the timber yards and the river docks, and every one of them had the same message, and every one of them was trying to deliver it first.

<br/>

"Gerald at the North Gate!"

<br/>

"Gerald on the high street!"

<br/>

"Gerald at the chandler's!"

<br/>

"*It went into the chandler's*—"

<br/>

"*Gerald gerald gerald*—"

<br/>

Pip, Bus Master of the Oakhaven Message Bus, was standing on an upturned crate with both hands raised, his face scarlet, trying to restore order by shouting "*ONE AT A TIME!*" at a volume that was mostly making things worse.

<br/>

He saw Mark and nearly fell off the crate.

<br/>

"It's *broken*!" he wailed. "The bus is *broken*! They're all saying it at once! I can't hear *anything*! Everyone's saying *Gerald* and nobody's saying *where*!"

<br/>

Mark looked at the screaming, jostling mass of small, filthy, overexcited children, every single one of whom had faithfully executed their protocol at precisely the same moment.

<br/>

"We've DDoSed ourselves," he said.

<br/>

Pip stared at him. "We've *what*?"

<br/>

"Distributed denial of service. Too many messages at once. The bus can't carry them all, so it can't carry any of them." Mark sighed. "It's a very old problem. Usually it's caused by enemies. Occasionally it's caused by a very large number of people being extremely helpful at the same time."

<br/>

Pip looked at him blankly. Somewhere behind him, a seven-year-old was shouting *Gerald* into the face of a nine-year-old who was shouting it back.

<br/>

"I'm putting that on the list," Pip said darkly. "The list of things you say."

<br/>

"Everyone does," said Mark. "Where's Wren?"

<br/>

He found her at the back of the crowd, sitting very calmly on a rain barrel, not shouting at all. She'd clearly worked out within the first thirty seconds that shouting wasn't going to get through, and had decided to wait until someone with sense arrived.

<br/>

"Report," said Mark.

<br/>

Wren hopped down off the barrel.

<br/>

"It came in the North Gate before dawn," she said. "Walking. On its own. Nobody saw it come up the road. It was just *there*." She took a breath, getting her details in order. "Tall. Taller than Torren, but thin. Long grey robe, right down to the ground, so you can't see its feet. No face."

<br/>

"No face?"

<br/>

"There's a face *shape*," Wren clarified. "Smooth. Grey. Like a mask, except there aren't any eyeholes. It carries a big book in one hand and a thin silver stick in the other." She frowned, concentrating. "It walks *exactly* the same speed. All the time. Not fast. Not slow. Like it's counting its steps. It went into the first house past the gate, the cooper's, and came out after a bit. Then it went into the *next* house. Then the next. It doesn't skip any." She looked up at Mark. "When it comes out of each one, the lamps outside go dark."

<br/>

"Did it hurt anyone?"

<br/>

"No." She hesitated. "But nobody tried to stop it, either. Everyone just got out of the way."

<br/>

Mark stood very still in the gray dawn light, with thirty children shouting *Gerald* behind him.

<br/>

*Grey robe,* he thought. *Grey, like the wax on Aldric's letter.*

<br/>

*Someone whom I would very much prefer you never met.*

<br/>

"Message bus," Wren said, a little uncertainly, as if she wasn't sure it still counted. "Out."

---

Mark saw it for himself at the Harrow timber mill, a little after nine.

<br/>

He hadn't meant to get close. He'd meant to watch from the far end of the street, the way you'd watch a storm cell from the porch. But the Harrow mill had been the Fish & Hammer's third customer, back when the blower business was just Mark and a needle-file and a scrap bin, and their big furnace blower was one of his. He'd grounded it himself. He knew exactly where the copper wire ran down into the floor.

<br/>

So he crouched in the doorway of a cooper's shop across the street, behind a stack of barrel staves, and watched the Auditor walk into his work.

<br/>

Wren had described it perfectly. It was very tall, a head taller than the tallest man in the street, and thin in a way that didn't look starved so much as *specified*, as if someone had worked out the minimum width a thing needed to be and then built it to exactly that. The grey robe hung straight to the cobbles without a wrinkle. Where its face should have been was a smooth, featureless oval of pale grey, like an egg carved from slate.

<br/>

In its left hand it held a book: thick, heavy, bound in grey. In its right, a slender silver rod, about the length of a forearm.

<br/>

It walked at exactly the speed Wren had described. Mark counted its steps against his own pulse and found them perfectly even. One. Two. Three. Four. It did not adjust its pace for the slope of the street, or for the puddles, or for a dog that ran barking up to it and then, abruptly, stopped barking, sat down, and lay flat.

<br/>

It went into the Harrow mill.

<br/>

Mark moved without quite deciding to, along the side of the building, to the small dusty window that looked into the furnace room. He'd fixed that window's latch himself, three months ago, while waiting for a crystal to charge.

<br/>

Inside, the mill hands had pressed themselves against the far wall. The furnace blower sat humming quietly on its iron stand: bronze housing, Guild stamp, the thin copper wire running down from the regulator disc into a crack in the flagstones. It was running dirty, as all the blowers did: Guild-standard trace, Guild-standard Restriction, forty percent pinch on the return. Mark's patch didn't change the spell. It only gave the heat somewhere to go.

<br/>

It was not clean. It had never been clean. It shouldn't have set off a single gold ring in the province.

<br/>

The Auditor stopped in front of it.

<br/>

It didn't listen to the blower's hum. It didn't seem interested in the heat, or the noise, or the faint blue flicker of the crystal. It simply raised its silver rod and laid the tip of it, very precisely, on the regulator disc.

<br/>

Then it opened its grey book with its other hand, and looked down at a page.

<br/>

Then back at the disc.

<br/>

Then at the page.

<br/>

Mark had seen that motion ten thousand times in his old life. He had *done* that motion. Checklist. Item. Checklist. Item. Document, then hardware, then document again.

<br/>

The rod moved slowly down the copper wire to the flagstones. It stopped at the place where the wire disappeared into the floor.

<br/>

The Auditor turned a page.

<br/>

A faint line of grey light ran from the tip of the rod down the wire, and the wire *parted*. Not cut. Not burned. It simply came apart at the point where it met the disc, as cleanly as if it had never been attached. The rod moved to the regulator, and Mark watched, his stomach dropping, as the patched traces on the silver disc *changed*: the parchment insulator crumbled to dust, the filed channels narrowed, the metal flowing back into exactly the shape it had been when it left the Guild workshop.

<br/>

Forty percent pinch. Perfect template.

<br/>

The Auditor made a mark in its book. Mark couldn't read it from the window. But the shape of the glyph glowed briefly on the page as it was written, and his translation layer caught it anyway, the way it caught everything, and laid the words neatly into his head:

<br/>

`[NON-COMPLIANT: UNSANCTIONED MODIFICATION. RESTORED TO SPECIFICATION.]`

<br/>

The Auditor closed the book. It turned. It walked out of the mill at exactly the speed it had walked in, and as it passed through the doorway, the Aether-lamp hanging outside flickered and went dark.

<br/>

Behind it, in the furnace room, the restored blower hummed for a few seconds. Then it began, very faintly, to tick, the way a stove ticks as it heats.

<br/>

Mark knew that sound too. It was the sound of heat with nowhere to go.

<br/>

He crouched below the window for a long moment, very cold despite the sun.

<br/>

*"It doesn't care if the spell is clean,"* he thought. *"It isn't listening for noise. It doesn't care about the rings."*

<br/>

*"It's checking the bill of materials against the spec."*

<br/>

He thought about Lyra's tournament bracelet, cut to perfect Guild template, which had walked through four hundred witnesses and four gold rings without a flicker. Camouflage. Beautiful, terrible camouflage. It had only ever worked because everything that had been hunting them, every construct and every ring, had been listening for *how magic sounded*.

<br/>

This thing didn't listen. It read.

<br/>

And every single thing the Fish & Hammer had ever built, every blower and every crystal and every wristband, every grounding wire and recharged shard, had been built *off-spec*. On purpose. Because the spec was the problem.

<br/>

*"Our entire camouflage strategy,"* Mark thought, *"just failed compliance."*

---

"Rule one," Mark said that evening, "answer only the question asked."

<br/>

They were in the back room of the Fish & Hammer with the shutters closed and a single lamp lit, and he was standing in front of them with a clipboard, and he had never felt more like himself in his life.

<br/>

He had survived a great many audits. He'd survived SOX audits and SOC 2 audits and ISO audits, two separate PCI audits in one memorable year, and one internal audit led by a man named Dennis who had once spent four hours asking about a single expense report for a sandwich. He'd been audited by people who wanted to find something, people who didn't care either way, and once, in 2014, by a woman who'd been so thorough that he still occasionally dreamed about her.

<br/>

He knew how this went.

<br/>

"An auditor doesn't want a conversation," he said. "An auditor wants evidence. Every extra word you say is evidence. So if it asks what something is, you say what it is. You don't say what it does, or why you made it, or how proud you are of it. You stop talking."

<br/>

"It doesn't *talk*," said Lyra. "Wren says it doesn't even have a mouth."

<br/>

"Then it'll be the best audit any of you ever sit through," Mark said. "Rule two. Never volunteer anything. If it doesn't open a drawer, you do not mention the drawer. You do not look at the drawer. You do not *think* about the drawer."

<br/>

Torren, on his stool, was taking notes on his own clipboard with tremendous concentration. He had put his reading spectacles back on for the occasion, and he now appeared to have stopped minding who saw them.

<br/>

"Rule three. If you don't know the answer, you say, *'I'll have to check.'* Never guess. Guessing is how a two-hour audit turns into a two-week audit."

<br/>

"I'll have to check," Torren repeated, writing it down.

<br/>

"Rule four." Mark held up the brass clipboard. "Always have a clipboard. In two worlds and forty years, I have never once seen anybody question a brass clipboard."

<br/>

Torren nodded fervently.

<br/>

"Rule five," said a voice from the bench under the window.

<br/>

They all looked at Corwin, who hadn't opened his eyes.

<br/>

"Do as little as possible," Corwin said. "At all times. In every respect." He folded his arms more comfortably. "I've been following rule two for three years. And rule five. They work very well together."

<br/>

"That's not technically an audit rule," said Mark.

<br/>

"It's a life rule," said Corwin. "It covers audits."

<br/>

Mark considered this, then wrote *Rule 5: (Corwin)* on the clipboard and underlined it.

<br/>

"And Prototype 2.0?" Vespera asked quietly. "If it touches anything we've made, it ruins it. You saw that at the mill."

<br/>

"I know," said Mark. "So it can't find it."

<br/>

"Where are you going to hide it? It reads *everything*."

<br/>

Mark thought back, a long way, to the morning in Torren's yard when High Inspector Valerius had swept in with his detection rods and his Enforcers, and a fourteen-year-old with a shed full of recharged crystals had needed to make them disappear very fast.

<br/>

He looked across the room, at the shelf where Lyra kept the power budget, and smiled.

<br/>

"Lyra," he said. "How big is Cheese the Fourth?"

---

The Auditor did not sleep, did not eat, did not stop, and did not hurry.

<br/>

By the end of the second day, it had worked its way through the whole of the North Gate quarter, one building at a time, in an order that Corwin, lying on the coal pile with his eyes closed, worked out within an hour from the bus reports.

<br/>

"It's a grid," he said. "It does every building on one side of a street, then every building on the other side, then turns left at the end and starts the next street. It never goes back. It never skips one." He yawned. "It's very efficient. I admire it. I'd never do it myself."

<br/>

The bus had recovered by then, after Pip had instituted a strict new rule that only one runner per district was allowed to say *Gerald*, and only after first saying where. The reports came in steadily, every hour, and every hour they were worse.

<br/>

The Harrow mill's blower had shorted out by noon the first day, exactly as Mark had known it would. By the second evening, so had the blowers at both the other timber mills, the tannery, the cooperage, and the big municipal bakery on Ash Street. Every one of them was a Fish & Hammer patch. Every one of them had been restored, perfectly, to a specification designed to destroy itself.

<br/>

Torren took it badly.

<br/>

He didn't shout. He didn't swear. He simply went very quiet each time a runner came in, and listened, and nodded, and then went out to the forge yard and stood for a while in front of Bessie, Mabel, Dot, and Gertrude, still humming in their row, as if checking that they were all right.

<br/>

"That's Bessie's *sisters*," he said, when Mark came to stand beside him. "The mill blowers. All of them. We fixed them together, Bessie and me. I used to tell her about them." He sniffed and wiped his nose on his sleeve. "Now they're all going to burn out in a month. And they won't even know why."

<br/>

Mark didn't say anything. There wasn't anything useful to say. He stood beside Torren for a while, in front of the four humming blowers, until the smith was ready to go back inside.

---

On the third morning, the Auditor reached the east bank.

<br/>

Hollis Grey came to the workshop in the afternoon. He didn't send a runner. He walked the whole way himself, still in his Academy robes, with flour dust on his sleeves, and stood in the yard for a long moment before he knocked, as if he wasn't sure he was allowed.

<br/>

"It came to the mill," he said. "This morning."

<br/>

Mark had known what he was going to say since he saw him in the yard. He let him say it anyway.

<br/>

"It went straight to the grinding wheel. Didn't look at anything else. Didn't even look at my father." Hollis's big hands were opening and closing at his sides. "It touched each of the crystals with that silver stick. One by one. And they went grey." He swallowed. "Dead glass. All of them. Every crystal you sold us. In about a minute."

<br/>

"I'm sorry," said Mark.

<br/>

"It wrote something in its book. And then it walked out, and the wheel stopped." Hollis looked at the floor. "My father stood there in the mill for an hour after. Just stood there. Looking at it. He's got Chapterhouse crystals coming in a week, if he can pay for them." He paused. "He can't, really. Not after the last ones. That's why we switched."

<br/>

There was a long silence.

<br/>

"It's not your fault," Hollis said at last, looking up. "I want you to know I know that. Dad knows it too. He said to tell you." His jaw tightened. "He said the last month was the best the mill's had in twenty years."

<br/>

Then he turned around and walked back out into the yard before Mark could answer, and Mark stood in the doorway of the workshop for a long time, watching the big, steady figure walk away down the lane, flour dust on his sleeves.

<br/>

He'd been proud of the crystal pipeline. He'd been proud of the mill. He'd written in his ledger that night: *Customer. Family runs on our crystals. Best thing that's happened to the mill in twenty years.*

<br/>

*"I built a dependency,"* he thought. *"And I didn't build any resilience into it. And then I left it running in production, in the open, where anybody could audit it."*

<br/>

*"I know better than that. I've always known better than that."*

<br/>

He went back inside, picked up his clipboard, and added Hollis's mill to the list.

---

Nan Brask, characteristically, offered it tea.

<br/>

The report came from Brann, the enforcer with the folded ear, who had been on the door at the Syndicate warehouse that afternoon and who arrived at the Fish & Hammer still visibly shaken, which was not a state anyone had previously seen him in.

<br/>

"It just walked in," he said, lowering himself onto a crate. "I was on the door. I stood in front of it. I said, 'Members only.' It stopped. And it waited." He shuddered. "It doesn't have a face, Mark. I've been in a lot of fights. I've hit a lot of people with a lot of chairs. I've never had nothing look at me before."

<br/>

"What did Nan do?"

<br/>

"Nan said, 'Let it in, Brann, don't be rude.'" Brann shook his head slowly. "So I let it in. And it walked right up to her chair. And she didn't even stop knitting. She just looked up and said, 'Would you care for some tea?'"

<br/>

"And?"

<br/>

"It didn't answer. It doesn't talk. So she poured it a cup anyway, and put it on the little table next to her, and went back to her knitting." Brann rubbed his folded ear. "And then it audited the *chart*."

<br/>

"The knitted chart?"

<br/>

"The org chart. The big one, on the wall behind her chair. With the goat." Brann's eyes were wide. "It stood in front of it with that silver stick, and it went over the whole thing. Stitch by stitch. Every box. Every line. The *goat*. For *eleven minutes*. I counted. Nan kept knitting the whole time. Never said a word." He swallowed. "Then it wrote something in its book. And it turned around, and it walked out, and it didn't touch the crystals in the back at all."

<br/>

"The *crystals*?" Lyra sat up. "Nan's whole back room is recharged crystals. Our crystals. It must have—"

<br/>

"It didn't go in the back room," said Brann. "It never opened the door." He paused. "Nan says that's because the back room's officially goat storage. On the paperwork. Has been for eleven years. And goat storage doesn't need auditing."

<br/>

Mark stared at him.

<br/>

Then he started, very quietly, to laugh, for the first time in three days.

<br/>

"She passed," he said. "She passed on *documentation*. She had the right paperwork filed, so it scoped her back room out of the audit." He wiped his eyes. "Oh, she's *good*. She's so good."

<br/>

"She was annoyed, though," Brann added.

<br/>

"About what?"

<br/>

"It didn't drink the tea," said Brann. "She said that was very rude. And then she said she wanted to know what it had written about her chart, because she's been working on that chart for eleven years and she's very proud of the goat." He hesitated. "She sent me to ask if you could read it. What it wrote. In the book. She says you read funny things."

<br/>

Mark thought about the glyph glowing on the page in the Harrow mill. *Restored to specification.*

<br/>

"I didn't see," he said. "But I'll find out."

<br/>

He did, eventually. Nan, it turned out, had kept the teacup, because the Auditor had touched the saucer with its rod on the way past, and a single line of faint grey glyphs had been left behind on the porcelain, like a watermark. Mark read it through his translation layer two days later, sitting in Nan's parlor, while she knitted and waited and pretended not to care.

<br/>

`[ORGANIZATIONAL STRUCTURE: COMPLIANT. GOAT: NOTED.]`

<br/>

Nan looked at it for a long time when he told her.

<br/>

"*Noted*," she repeated at last, with deep satisfaction.

<br/>

She had the saucer framed.

---

The Infirmary closed on the fourth day.

<br/>

It had to. The Auditor was only six streets from the armory yard by then, and Mark had made the call himself: no clean casting, no heat therapy, nothing that might draw it even one street out of its order. Lyra's grip trainers were fine; they were just leather and ash, and any auditor in the world could inspect them to its heart's content. But the heat was what made the line worth standing in, and the heat was Vespera's bracelet, and Vespera's bracelet was a clean focus off-spec in every possible way.

<br/>

So they put up a sign. Lyra painted it. *CLOSED FOR AUDIT. GRIP TRAINERS STILL AVAILABLE. DO YOUR LEG LIFTS.*

<br/>

The patients came anyway.

<br/>

They didn't come for treatment. They came and stood in the yard, in a loose, quiet crowd, the enforcers and dockhands and goat handlers and the tannery worker with the bad elbow, and they looked at the sign, and they didn't leave. Some of them sat on crates. Brann stood by the gate with his arms folded. The door guard from the Syndicate warehouse, the one who could turn his head to the left again, stood next to him, and turned his head to the left every few minutes, slowly, as if making sure it still worked.

<br/>

Vespera watched them from the window of the workshop, very still.

<br/>

"I could help them," she said quietly. "Just the worst ones. Just for a minute each. Low draw. It wouldn't—"

<br/>

"It would," said Mark. "You know it would."

<br/>

"I know." She didn't look away from the window. "That man by the water barrel. His shoulder locks up in the cold. I've been warming it every morning for two weeks. He can raise his arm above his head now. He couldn't before." Her hands tightened on the windowsill. "It's going to be cold tonight."

<br/>

"I know."

<br/>

"Five hundred years," Vespera said, very softly, "of the Guild telling everyone magic must hurt. And the first time I ever used it to make the hurting stop, a thing in a grey robe comes and tells me it was against specification."

<br/>

Mark had nothing to say to that, so he didn't try. He stood beside her at the window, and together they watched the patients in the yard, waiting for a door that wasn't going to open.

<br/>

It started to rain a little after dusk.

<br/>

They stayed anyway.

---

Mark didn't go to lectures on the fifth day.

<br/>

It was the first class he had ever skipped in either world, and he did it on principle. Rule six, which he hadn't told anyone because it seemed too obvious to say out loud: *during an active audit, do not voluntarily walk into any room the auditor might enter.*

<br/>

The Auditor entered the Grand Amphitheater at ten o'clock in the morning.

<br/>

Mark heard about it at noon, from Tobin Pell, Academy node number two of the Oakhaven Message Bus, who arrived at the Fish & Hammer in a state of such breathless, white-faced excitement that it took him three tries to get the first sentence out.

<br/>

"It came in," Tobin gasped, collapsing onto a crate. "In the middle of the lecture. The doors just *opened*. By themselves. And it walked in, and everybody went silent, and Ignis—" He paused to breathe. "Ignis was *thrilled*."

<br/>

"Thrilled?"

<br/>

"He was so *happy*, Mark. I've never seen him happy. He threw his arms out, like this—" Tobin flung his arms wide, nearly toppling off the crate, "—and he said, '*At last!* A *true* authority of the Concord! *At last*, someone who will see what I have seen!' And then he turned around and pointed straight up at the top row. At your seat." Tobin's eyes were huge. "And he shouted, 'There! The boy who sits *there*! *Audit him!*'"

<br/>

"My seat was empty," said Mark.

<br/>

"Your seat was empty," Tobin agreed. "Everyone turned and looked at it. Ignis kept pointing at it. The Auditor sort of… turned its face toward it. For a while. And then it turned back around." He leaned forward. "And it went to the *lectern*. Because the lectern was first. Because it does everything in *order*."

<br/>

From the coal pile, without opening his eyes, Corwin said, "Mm. Front to back."

<br/>

"So it audits the lectern," Tobin went on, unstoppable now. "The acoustic rune. Fine. Compliant. Then the testing anvil. Fine. Then it turns to Ignis. And it holds out its stick toward his *staff*." He took a deep breath. "And Ignis *gives it to it*. He holds it right out. Proud. Like he's showing off. 'Inspect it! The finest ironwood in the province! A sapphire of the first water! Twenty years of sacred attunement!'"

<br/>

"And?"

<br/>

"And the Auditor touches the silver tip. And looks at its book. And looks at the staff. And looks at its book." Tobin's voice dropped to a reverent whisper. "And then it slides the stick down the shaft, all the way to the bottom of the staff. To the iron heel. And it *stops*."

<br/>

Lyra leaned forward on her stool. "What was at the heel?"

<br/>

"*A wire*," said Tobin.

<br/>

There was a short silence.

<br/>

"A little copper wire," Tobin said. "Wound around the inside of the heel, under the iron cap, where nobody would ever see it. Running from the silver core down to the tip of the heel. So that every time he plants his staff on the floor—"

<br/>

"—it grounds," said Mark slowly.

<br/>

"The Auditor writes in its book," Tobin said. "And the glyph glows, and nobody can read it, of course. But then it does something else. It pulls the iron cap off the heel. Just lifts it off with the stick. And it draws the wire out, all of it, slow, like pulling a thread out of a hem. And it *holds it up*." Tobin swallowed. "In front of everyone. A hundred and forty students. A little coil of copper wire, hanging off the end of the stick, catching the light."

<br/>

Lyra put both hands over her mouth.

<br/>

"And then the wire just came *apart*," said Tobin. "And the staff sort of *ticked*. And Ignis stood there, holding it, in front of a hundred and forty students, with his mouth open. And he said, 'That is *impossible*. That is a *slander*. I have never—I would *never*—'"

<br/>

Torren had gone very quiet in the corner of the workshop.

<br/>

Mark turned slowly to look at him.

<br/>

"Torren," he said.

<br/>

"Hm?"

<br/>

"A big ironwood staff. Silver core. Sapphire the size of a fist." Mark paused. "Did that come through here?"

<br/>

Torren's face went through a complicated series of expressions, landing eventually on a sort of guilty delight.

<br/>

"*Oh*," he said. "*Reginald!*"

<br/>

"*Reginald*?"

<br/>

"Six weeks ago," Torren said. "Fellow came in at closing time. Servant, by the look of him. Hood up, wouldn't give a name. Said his master had a staff that 'ran a bit warm' and wanted one of the 'static safety cables,' but very discreetly, and could we fit it out of sight." He beamed. "Biggest sapphire I've ever seen. Pompous-looking thing. Gold braid on the grip. I called it Reginald. I did the heel myself. Lovely job, if I say so. Paid in gold, too." He thought about it. "Is that bad?"

<br/>

"Archmage Ignis," said Mark slowly, "has been standing in front of the Academy every morning for three months telling a hundred and forty students that Aether demands suffering. That the burns are sacred. That pain is proof of the spirit." He took a breath. "And for six weeks, his own staff has been running cool because he secretly bought a grounding wire from *us*."

<br/>

The workshop was absolutely silent.

<br/>

Then Lyra started to laugh.

<br/>

It started as a small, helpless snort behind her hands, and then it grew, until she was bent double on her stool, wheezing, tears running down her face. Torren caught it next, his great booming laugh shaking the tools on the wall. Then Tobin, nervously, then not nervously at all. Even Vespera's composure broke, and she pressed a hand to her mouth, her shoulders shaking. On the coal pile, Corwin's mouth twitched.

<br/>

Mark laughed too. He couldn't help it. It was the first good news in five days.

<br/>

"Oh, that's not even the best part," said Tobin, wiping his eyes.

<br/>

"There's *more*?"

<br/>

"The Auditor restored his staff. Back to spec. Proper Guild standard. And it left." Tobin grinned. "And Ignis was so angry that he finished the lecture anyway. To prove a point. He did a full kinetic demonstration on the anvil. Forty-line incantation. 'Behold the sacred strain!'" He shook his head slowly. "And his staff *heated up*. Properly. The way it's supposed to. The way it hadn't for six weeks. And he wasn't ready for it. And halfway through the chant, he dropped it and started hopping around the stage holding his hand and yelling."

<br/>

"Yelling what?"

<br/>

Tobin cleared his throat and did his best impression of an Archmage in agony.

<br/>

"'*Suffering is SACRED! Suffering is SACRED! OW! OW! IT IS SACRED!*'"

<br/>

It was a long time before anyone in the Fish & Hammer could speak.

---

The Auditor reached the armory yard on the sixth day, a little after ten in the morning.

<br/>

They were ready for it. As ready as they could be.

<br/>

Prototype 2.0 was inside Cheese the Fourth: a great pale wheel of hard mountain cheese the size of a cartwheel hub, which Lyra had hollowed out from underneath with a graver, wrapped the wristband in waxed linen, slid it into the center, and sealed up again with a warm knife so precisely that nobody could see the join. The cheese sat on the shelf beside the iron box marked *DO NOT CHARGE*, where it had sat every day for a month, looking exactly like a cheese.

<br/>

"If it audits dairy," Lyra had said grimly, "we deserve to lose."

<br/>

The recharged crystal stock was another matter. There were nearly two hundred of them, in crates, glowing quietly in the back room. There was nowhere to hide two hundred glowing crystals, and nowhere to move them that the Auditor wouldn't eventually reach. Mark had made the decision the night before: leave them. Let it take them. Protect what mattered.

<br/>

Lyra had argued about L-1.

<br/>

"Put it in the cheese with Prototype 2.0," Mark had said. "There's room."

<br/>

"No."

<br/>

"Lyra—"

<br/>

"*No*." She'd held up her wrist. The bracelet sat on it the way it always did: slim and silver, with the long, sweeping, First Era strokes she had taught herself to cut over three weeks of evenings, tracing the old plates, line by line, until her hand knew them. "This is the best thing I've ever made. I made it *right*. I'm not hiding it in a cheese like it's something to be ashamed of." Her jaw had set. "It can look at it. Let it look. I want it to see what the lines are *supposed* to look like."

<br/>

Mark had looked at her for a long moment. Then he'd stopped arguing.

<br/>

Vespera wore V-1 on her left wrist. There was no question of hiding it. If it was going to find her, it would find her through the bracelet or without it.

<br/>

And Garek stood in the doorway.

---

The Auditor walked through the yard gate at exactly the speed it had walked through every other gate in Oakhaven. It crossed the yard. It passed the forge, cold and dark for the day. It passed the four blowers in their row, Bessie and Mabel and Dot and Gertrude, still humming, still running clean and cool, their copper wires running down into the floor.

<br/>

It did not stop at them.

<br/>

Not yet. Mark understood instantly, his stomach sinking. *It does the building first. Then the yard. Order.*

<br/>

It reached the workshop door, and stopped.

<br/>

Garek filled the doorway. He had his feet planted wide and his arms folded across his chest and his shoulders touching both sides of the frame, the way he had stood for six days, and he looked up at the smooth, grey, featureless oval of the Auditor's face, a full head above his own, and didn't move.

<br/>

"Morning," said Garek.

<br/>

The Auditor waited.

<br/>

It didn't push. It didn't speak. It didn't raise its silver rod or open its grey book. It simply stood, two feet from Garek, perfectly still, with the absolute, bottomless, infinite patience of a thing that had never once in its existence been in a hurry.

<br/>

Garek waited too.

<br/>

After a few minutes, he began his leg lifts.

---

At the one-hour mark, a small crowd had begun to gather in the lane outside the yard gate.

<br/>

At the two-hour mark, it was not a small crowd anymore. The Infirmary patients had come, as they did every day, and stayed. The bus runners had come. Half the timber yard had come to see what was happening at the Fish & Hammer. Brann was there, with his arms folded, and the door guard who could turn his head, turning it.

<br/>

At the two-and-a-half-hour mark, an enterprising noodle seller from the river stall set up a cart at the yard gate and began doing a brisk trade.

<br/>

"*Noodles!*" she called. "*Hot noodles! Watch the standoff! Noodles!*"

<br/>

"I'll take one," said Corwin, from the coal pile, holding up a copper without opening his eyes.

<br/>

At the three-hour mark, Garek switched legs.

<br/>

At the four-hour mark, somebody in the crowd started taking bets. The odds on Garek were holding steady at two to one, mostly on the strength of the leg lifts, which he was performing with a slow, grim, metronomic precision that drew a small round of applause every time he completed a set.

<br/>

The Auditor did not move. It had not moved at all. It had not shifted its weight, or adjusted its grip on the rod, or turned its head. Mark had been watching it the whole time from the window, and as far as he could tell, it would quite happily stand there until the sun burned out.

<br/>

At the five-hour mark, he went out to the doorway.

<br/>

"Garek."

<br/>

Garek didn't take his eyes off the Auditor. His forehead was shining with sweat. His left knee, Mark could see, had begun to tremble very slightly at the top of each lift.

<br/>

"I can do another hour," Garek said. "Easy."

<br/>

"I know you can."

<br/>

"Two, if I switch legs again."

<br/>

"I know." Mark put his hand on the big man's shoulder. "But it'll wait forever. That's the thing about auditors, Garek. They don't get tired. They don't get bored. They don't have anywhere else to be. They bill by the hour." He squeezed the shoulder. "Step aside."

<br/>

Garek looked at him for a long moment.

<br/>

Then, slowly, with enormous reluctance, he lowered his knee, unfolded his arms, and stepped out of the doorway.

<br/>

In the lane, the crowd let out a long, collective groan. Several people paid out on bets. The noodle seller sold three more bowls.

<br/>

"Five hours, eleven minutes," said Corwin, from the coal pile. "That's a record."

<br/>

"Do the knee tonight," said Mark quietly, as Garek passed him. "Ice. Then heat. Then the lifts."

<br/>

"Twice?"

<br/>

"Three times," said Mark. "You've earned it."

<br/>

The Auditor stepped through the door.

---

Inside the workshop, it was very quiet.

<br/>

They had arranged themselves the way Mark had told them to: standing, not sitting, hands visible, nothing in their pockets they'd have to explain. Lyra by the workbench, L-1 on her wrist. Vespera by the window, V-1 on hers. Torren in the center of the room, in his good apron, his reading spectacles on, holding the brass clipboard with the visitor log in front of him like a shield. Garek came back in and took up a position beside the door, breathing hard. Corwin had stayed on the coal pile outside, on the grounds that the coal pile was a separate building for audit purposes, which Mark had decided not to argue with.

<br/>

And Mark stood by the shelves, next to the iron box and the cheese, with his hands loosely at his sides and his face perfectly neutral, the way he had stood through every audit of his life.

<br/>

The Auditor stopped just inside the door.

<br/>

Up close, it was worse. It didn't smell of anything. It didn't breathe. There was no sound from under the grey robe at all, not even the creak of a joint. The smooth oval of its face was the color of wet slate, and it was not quite featureless after all: very faintly, if you looked at it for long enough, you could see a fine grid of lines etched across its surface, as precise as the traces on a regulator disc.

<br/>

Torren stepped forward, as they had rehearsed.

<br/>

"Good morning," he said, with only a very small tremor in his voice. "Welcome to the Fish & Hammer. You'll want to sign in." He held out the clipboard, with the pen on its string. "Name, House, purpose of visit, time in. Very important. Safety."

<br/>

The Auditor turned its face toward the clipboard.

<br/>

For a moment, nothing happened.

<br/>

Then it raised its silver rod and laid the tip of it, very precisely, on the brass.

<br/>

Mark's breath stopped in his chest.

<br/>

The Auditor drew the rod slowly down the length of the clipboard. It turned the rod and traced the edges. It tapped the clip. It tapped the pen on its string. It opened its grey book, looked at a page, and looked back at the clipboard.

<br/>

Then it took the clipboard out of Torren's hands.

<br/>

It turned it over. It examined the back. It turned it over again. It ran the rod across the visitor log, line by line, across the two signatures from the Thorne men and the words *Inspection* and *Pending* in Torren's careful capitals.

<br/>

Then it wrote something in its book, and handed the clipboard back.

<br/>

The glyph glowed on the page for a moment as it was written. Mark read it without wanting to.

<br/>

`[CLIPBOARD: INSPECTED. COMPLIANT.]`

<br/>

Torren stood holding the clipboard in both hands, staring down at it.

<br/>

Mark stood by the shelves, and felt something cold and fundamental shift deep in the foundations of his understanding of the universe.

<br/>

In two worlds. In sixty years. In every office, every warehouse, every server room and loading dock and Chapterhouse and cellar he had ever walked into.

<br/>

*Nobody* had ever questioned a brass clipboard.

<br/>

He looked across the room at Lyra. She was staring at the clipboard with her mouth slightly open, the way she might have stared at the sun rising in the west. By the window, Vespera had gone pale. Even Garek, by the door, looked shaken.

<br/>

"It *inspected* it," Torren whispered.

<br/>

"I know," said Mark, very quietly.

<br/>

"It inspected the *clipboard*, Mark."

<br/>

"I know."

<br/>

"That's never happened."

<br/>

"I know, Torren."

<br/>

Torren looked at the clipboard for another long moment. Then, with great dignity and a slight wobble in his chin, he tucked it under his arm and stepped back into his place.

<br/>

"I'll have to check," he said faintly, to nobody in particular.

---

Then the audit began, and it was exactly as bad as Mark had known it would be.

<br/>

It did the room in order, left to right, top to bottom, the way he would have done it himself.

<br/>

Edna first, humming in her corner. Rod on the disc. Book. Disc. Book. The copper wire came apart. The traces flowed back into template. Edna's hum faltered and changed pitch, and then, very faintly, began to tick.

<br/>

Then the crates in the back room. It stood over them for a long time, nearly two hundred crystals glowing softly blue in their straw, and touched each one with the rod, one at a time, with exactly the same unhurried precision. Each one went grey as it was touched. It took eleven minutes. Mark counted them, the way Brann had counted Nan's chart, because counting was something to do. When it was finished, the back room was dark.

<br/>

Then the workbench. Lyra's gravers and files. Torren's tongs. The magnifying lens. All fine. All compliant. It didn't care about tools. Only about what the tools had made.

<br/>

Then the shelves.

<br/>

It stopped in front of the iron box. It raised the lid with the tip of its rod. It looked down for a long moment at the dormant grey core inside, nestled in its padding, with *DO NOT CHARGE* painted on the inside of the lid in Lyra's neatest capitals. It opened its book.

<br/>

`[CONCORD ASSET. DORMANT. COMPLIANT.]`

<br/>

It closed the lid.

<br/>

Then it turned to the cheese.

<br/>

Mark did not look at the cheese. He did not look at the floor near the cheese, or the shelf under the cheese, or anyone who was looking at the cheese. He looked, very steadily, at the Auditor's slate-grey face, and thought about nothing at all, the way he'd once thought about nothing at all while a very thorough woman in 2014 had opened a drawer that contained something he would have preferred she didn't open.

<br/>

The Auditor looked at the cheese for a long time.

<br/>

It did not raise its rod.

<br/>

It turned away.

<br/>

Behind him, Mark heard Lyra let out a very long, very quiet breath.

<br/>

*"It doesn't audit dairy,"* he thought. *"Out of scope. Thank God for scoping."*

---

Then it turned to the people.

<br/>

It did Garek first, because he was nearest the door. It held the rod an inch from his chest and moved it slowly from head to foot, and Garek stood absolutely still and stared at the ceiling and did not do a leg lift.

<br/>

`[COMPLIANT.]`

<br/>

Then Torren, who closed his eyes and held his clipboard very tightly.

<br/>

`[COMPLIANT.]`

<br/>

Then Vespera.

<br/>

It moved the rod down from her head to her shoulders, and stopped at her left wrist. It stayed there for a long moment, a hand's width from the silver bracelet. Then it opened its book.

<br/>

The rod touched V-1, very lightly.

<br/>

Vespera flinched, just once, as if something cold had brushed against her skin. A faint grey light flickered over the bracelet, and then *through* it, and when it faded, there was something on the inside of her wrist, just below the silver: a thin line of glyphs, pale grey, like a mark left by a pencil.

<br/>

She turned her wrist over and looked at it.

<br/>

Mark read it from across the room.

<br/>

`[CORRECTION SCHEDULED.]`

<br/>

The bracelet itself was untouched. Still slim and silver, still clean. The Auditor hadn't restored it. It had marked *her*.

<br/>

Vespera looked at the glyphs on her wrist for a long, silent moment. Then she lowered her arm to her side, raised her chin, and looked back at the smooth grey oval of the Auditor's face with an expression Mark had seen her wear only once before: the day she'd faced down High Inspector Valerius in Torren's yard, with her staff held high.

<br/>

The Auditor turned away from her.

<br/>

It turned to Lyra.

---

Lyra didn't move. She stood by the workbench with her arms at her sides and L-1 on her right wrist, and looked straight up at the faceless grey oval looming over her, and her chin was very slightly raised.

<br/>

The rod came down from her head. Shoulders. Arm.

<br/>

It stopped at the bracelet.

<br/>

The Auditor opened its book. It looked at a page. It looked at L-1.

<br/>

It looked at the page again, for a longer time.

<br/>

Mark thought, later, that it was the longest the Auditor had looked at anything since it had arrived in Oakhaven. Longer than the knitted chart. Longer than the crates. As if, somewhere inside it, some process was comparing the long, sweeping, First Era strokes on Lyra's bracelet against its specification, and finding the gap between them so large that it had to measure it twice.

<br/>

Lyra looked up at it.

<br/>

"Your template is wrong," she said.

<br/>

Her voice was perfectly clear and perfectly cold. It didn't tremble at all.

<br/>

"Those are the right lines," she said. "The real ones. I copied them from the plates you took. Every channel open. Every return grounded. No pinch." She held her wrist up, deliberately, right under the rod. "This is what it's *supposed* to look like. Yours is broken. Every bracelet you've ever passed is broken. You're checking everything against a mistake."

<br/>

The Auditor looked at her for a long moment.

<br/>

Then it wrote something in its book. The glyph glowed briefly on the page, and then faded, and Mark, for once, could not read it from where he stood.

<br/>

The rod touched L-1.

<br/>

It took about two seconds.

<br/>

Mark watched it happen. He would remember it for a very long time. The silver of Lyra's bracelet dulled, as if a breath had fogged it. And then the lines *moved*. The long, sweeping First Era strokes that she had taught herself to cut, evening after evening, tracing the ancient plates by lamplight until her hand knew every curve, drew in on themselves. The open return channel pinched shut, forty percent, as neatly as a purse-string. The clean grounding path folded and closed. The whole delicate, beautiful architecture of it crumpled and reformed, smoothly and silently, into exactly the same shape as every Guild-standard bracelet ever cut in the Empire.

<br/>

The same shape as the bracelet she had made for the tournament, under protest, muttering *this is wrong* under her breath.

<br/>

Two seconds. Three weeks of evenings.

<br/>

`[RESTORED TO SPECIFICATION.]`

<br/>

The Auditor lowered its rod.

<br/>

Lyra didn't look at her wrist. She didn't look away from the Auditor's face. She stood absolutely still by the workbench, with her chin raised and her ruined bracelet on her arm, and didn't let anything at all show on her face, and Mark, standing by the shelves, thought he had never seen anyone braver in his life.

<br/>

The Auditor turned away from her.

<br/>

It turned to Mark.

---

He stood very still beside the shelves, beside the box and the cheese, with his hands loose at his sides.

<br/>

The Auditor raised its rod.

<br/>

It held it an inch from his chest. It moved it slowly upward, to his face, and held it there, between his eyes. Up close, he could see the fine, precise grid of lines across its slate-grey face, and he could hear, very faintly, a high, thin, crystalline hum, like the hum of the carriage, like the hum of something old and very far away.

<br/>

It opened its book.

<br/>

It looked at the page.

<br/>

It looked at Mark.

<br/>

It looked at the page.

<br/>

And then it did nothing at all.

<br/>

It didn't write. The page stayed blank. No glyph glowed. The rod stayed where it was, an inch from his face, perfectly steady, for one long second, then two, then five, while the Auditor stood in front of him with its book open and its blank grey face turned toward him and did *nothing*, as if it were waiting for a line on a list to load, and the line had not loaded, and there was nothing else it had been told to do.

<br/>

Then it lowered the rod.

<br/>

It turned its face away from him, toward the door, and walked out of the workshop at exactly the speed it had walked in, as if there had been nobody standing by the shelves at all.

---

In the yard, it did the blowers.

<br/>

Torren went out after it. Mark tried to stop him and then didn't, because there was no stopping him, and because he understood. Torren stood in the yard in his good apron, his reading spectacles still on his nose, and watched the Auditor walk down the row.

<br/>

Gertrude first. Rod on the disc. The copper wire came apart.

<br/>

Then Dot.

<br/>

Then Mabel.

<br/>

Torren said nothing. He stood very straight with his hands at his sides.

<br/>

Then the Auditor stopped in front of Bessie.

<br/>

"Not Bessie," said Torren.

<br/>

He didn't say it loudly. He said it quietly, and politely, the way you'd ask a stranger not to step on a flower bed.

<br/>

"Please," said Torren. "She's fourteen years old. She's the oldest. She was here before any of them. She was here before *me*, near enough." His voice wavered. "She's running clean. She's not hurting anybody. She's just a blower."

<br/>

The Auditor raised its rod.

<br/>

"I'll have to check," Torren whispered. It was the only rule he could remember. "I'll have to check. I'll have to—"

<br/>

The rod touched the disc.

<br/>

The copper wire that Mark had soldered to Bessie's housing three months ago, on the very first morning of the Fish & Hammer, back when it had been nothing but a scrap boy and a needle-file and a smith who named things, came apart as cleanly as a thread.

<br/>

Bessie's hum faltered. Changed pitch.

<br/>

And then began, very faintly, to tick.

<br/>

The Auditor wrote in its book. It turned. It walked out of the armory yard gate, past the noodle cart and the silent crowd and the bus runners and the patients, who all stepped back out of its way without a word. As it passed under the arch of the gate, the Aether-lamp above it flickered and went dark.

<br/>

It turned left at the end of the lane, toward the next street, and was gone.

<br/>

Torren stood in front of Bessie for a long time. He put one big hand flat on her bronze housing, as if feeling for a fever.

<br/>

"She'll be all right for a month," Mark said quietly, beside him. "Maybe more. I'll fix her again. When this is over. I'll fix all of them."

<br/>

"I know you will," said Torren.

<br/>

He didn't take his hand away.

---

Lyra held it together until the lamp outside the yard gate went dark.

<br/>

Then she walked into the back room, the dark one, where the crates of dead grey crystals sat in their straw, and closed the door behind her.

<br/>

Mark gave her a few minutes. Then he went in.

<br/>

She was sitting on the floor between two crates with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up, holding her right wrist in her left hand, looking down at the bracelet. She wasn't making any noise. The tears were just running down her face, steadily, and dripping off her chin onto the dull silver.

<br/>

Mark sat down on the floor beside her.

<br/>

He didn't say anything about fixing it. There was nothing to fix. He'd been the senior engineer in the room for forty years, and he'd learned, very slowly and too late, that sometimes the most useful thing the senior engineer in the room could do was nothing at all.

<br/>

So he sat beside her in the dark, and waited.

<br/>

"Three weeks," Lyra said at last. Her voice was thick. "Every night. I traced every line off the old plates. Before they were taken. I copied them so many times I could cut them with my eyes shut." She turned her wrist over. "And it just… folded them up. Like they were nothing. Like they were *wrong*."

<br/>

"They weren't wrong."

<br/>

"I know they weren't wrong!" Her voice cracked. "That's the *worst* part! They were *right*! They were the first right thing anyone's cut in five hundred years! And it looked at them, and it measured them, and it *knew* they were different, I *saw* it know, and it fixed them anyway, because they didn't match the book!" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "It's not even *evil*, Mark. It's just *following the spec*."

<br/>

"I know," said Mark quietly. "That's what auditors do. They're not checking whether you're right. They're checking whether you match."

<br/>

Lyra cried for a while. Mark sat beside her.

<br/>

After a time, the door opened, very softly, and Torren came in. He had taken off his reading spectacles. He lowered himself down onto the floor on Lyra's other side, slowly, with a grunt, the way a big man does when his knees aren't what they were, and sat there with his back against the crates.

<br/>

He looked at the bracelet on her wrist for a long moment.

<br/>

"What was she called?" he asked.

<br/>

Lyra sniffed. "What?"

<br/>

"The bracelet." Torren nodded at it. "What was her name?"

<br/>

"*L-1*," said Lyra. "She didn't have a *name*, Torren. She was a prototype."

<br/>

Torren considered this with grave disapproval.

<br/>

"Well, she should have," he said. "Everything that works hard should have a name. And everything that's *lost* should have one twice over. So people remember." He looked at the bracelet again, thinking. "*Tilly*," he said at last. "She looks like a Tilly. Brave. Small. Did her best." He nodded firmly. "Poor Tilly."

<br/>

Lyra stared at him.

<br/>

And then she laughed: a wet, cracked, helpless laugh, with her face still streaming, and she leaned sideways against Torren's big shoulder and kept laughing and crying at the same time, and Torren put one huge arm around her and said nothing at all.

<br/>

"Poor Tilly," Lyra said finally, into his apron.

<br/>

"Poor Tilly," Torren agreed.

<br/>

Mark sat with them in the dark until she'd stopped.

<br/>

When she finally sat up, wiping her face on her sleeve, she looked down at the bracelet on her wrist again. But this time, Mark noticed, she wasn't looking at it the way she had before. She was turning her wrist slowly back and forth in the dim light from the door, the way she turned a disc under her lens, and her eyes had narrowed.

<br/>

"Four places," she said.

<br/>

"What?"

<br/>

"It only touched four places." She traced them with a fingertip, one by one. "When it was measuring. Before it fixed her. It held the rod at the clasp. Then the return channel, here, where the pinch goes. Then the crystal seat. Then the far end of the main trace." She looked up at Mark, and there was something in her red-rimmed eyes that hadn't been there an hour ago. "It didn't check anything else. It checked four points against its book. *Four*. The book says what four places to look at, and it looks at those four, and nothing in between."

<br/>

Mark said nothing. He waited.

<br/>

"It's not reading the whole bracelet," Lyra said slowly. "It's reading the *checkpoints*."

<br/>

She was quiet for a long moment.

<br/>

Then she got up off the floor, walked out of the back room to the workbench, and picked up her graver.

---

They held the damage review that night, because Mark believed in damage reviews, and because nobody could sleep.

<br/>

He wrote it on the clipboard, the one the Auditor had inspected. He found that he didn't like holding it anymore. He held it anyway.

<br/>

*Losses:*

<br/>

*- Every Fish & Hammer blower in Oakhaven restored to Guild specification. Estimated time to failure: 4–6 weeks. Including Bessie.*

<br/>

*- ~200 recharged crystals (workshop stock): dead.*

<br/>

*- Grey Mill (Hollis's family): all crystals dead. Wheel stopped.*

<br/>

<br/>

<br/>

<br/>

He looked at the last line for a while. Then he added nothing else to the page, and turned it over.

<br/>

"It skipped you," Lyra said, from the workbench, without looking up.

<br/>

Mark had been waiting for someone to say it. He'd hoped it wouldn't be her.

<br/>

"It didn't skip me. It checked me. I wasn't carrying anything off-spec."

<br/>

"It checked Garek. It checked Torren. They weren't carrying anything off-spec either. It wrote *compliant* for them." She set the graver down and looked at him. "It didn't write anything for you, Mark. It just stood there. Like it didn't know what you were."

<br/>

"Probably," Mark said, "a scoping issue."

<br/>

Lyra held his gaze for a long moment. Then she picked the graver back up.

<br/>

Vespera had said nothing since the audit. She sat in the good chair, by the cold fire, turning her left wrist over and over in her lap, looking at the pale grey glyphs below her bracelet.

<br/>

"It isn't just me," she said at last.

<br/>

Everyone looked at her.

<br/>

"Valerius came to The Silver Ladle last night," she said. "He didn't eat. He didn't read his book. He sat down across from me, at my table, without asking, which he has never done." She paused. "He rolled up his sleeve."

<br/>

Mark went very still.

<br/>

"The Auditor visited the Chapterhouse two days ago. It read the inspection reports. All of them. It read his report from the night of the archive. The one where he wrote that he found nothing." She turned her wrist so the glyphs caught the lamp. "He has the same mark. In the same place. *Correction scheduled.*" Her voice was very level. "He said, 'It appears I am now on your side, Mage Vespera. I would have preferred to choose it.'"

<br/>

There was a short silence.

<br/>

"Did he say anything else?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

Vespera turned faintly pink, which on her was a full blush.

<br/>

"He asked whether I'd be at the Ladle again on Thursday," she said, with great dignity. "I said I would have to check."

<br/>

From the coal pile outside, very faintly, through the window, came Corwin's voice: "Rule three."

---

Corwin came inside a little after midnight, which was rare enough that everyone stopped talking. He lay down on the long bench under the window, folded his arms over his chest, and spoke to the ceiling.

<br/>

"I worked out the pace," he said. "From the bus reports. It's very consistent. That helps."

<br/>

"How long?" said Mark.

<br/>

"It's done about a third of the city in six days. Not counting the Academy's lower levels. It did the top floors of the Academy and walked straight past the stairs down. Didn't even look at them." Corwin yawned. "It's saving them for last. Like dessert."

<br/>

"How long until it finishes?"

<br/>

"Nine days," said Corwin. "Then it goes down the stairs. Past the archive. Past the third landing." He paused. "To whatever's at the bottom."

<br/>

The workshop was very quiet.

<br/>

"Aldric gave us sixteen days," said Torren slowly. "Until the new moon."

<br/>

"Aldric's deadline doesn't matter anymore," said Mark. "His offer was to make the problem go away. The problem just walked past his offer and audited a goat chart." He set down the clipboard. "We've got nine days. Then that thing reaches the Anchor, and I don't think anything we've built is going to be in specification after that. Including Vespera. Including Valerius."

<br/>

Nobody said anything.

<br/>

There was a knock at the yard gate.

<br/>

They all looked at each other. Garek moved, without a word, to the doorway.

<br/>

Pip came running across the yard in the rain. He wasn't saying *Gerald*. He wasn't saying anything. He was holding a folded square of cream-colored paper, sealed with red wax, and he handed it to Mark at the door and backed away from it as if it might bite.

<br/>

"Man in a dark coat," Pip said. "At the gate. Gave it to me. Didn't wait."

<br/>

Mark broke the seal. The handwriting was elegant, and familiar, and very slightly less precise than it had been the last time.

<br/>

*Mr. Vance,*

<br/>

*I did say I would prefer you never met.*

<br/>

*My offer stands. Sooner would now be wiser.*

<br/>

*A.T.*

<br/>

Mark read it twice. Then he folded it and put it in the ledger, between the pages of Log 18, and didn't say anything at all.

---

It was nearly two in the morning when the second knock came.

<br/>

It wasn't at the yard gate. It was at the workshop door itself: three soft, uneven raps, as if whoever was knocking couldn't quite close their hand.

<br/>

Garek was asleep sitting up against the frame. He woke at once and was on his feet before the third knock, filling the doorway, one hand on the latch.

<br/>

He looked back at Mark.

<br/>

Mark nodded.

<br/>

Garek opened the door.

<br/>

Julian Thorne stood on the step in the pouring rain.

<br/>

He was soaked to the skin. His fine dark coat was black with water and splashed with mud to the knees. His boots were ruined. His hair was plastered flat to his skull. Both his hands were still wrapped in linen to the wrists, and the linen was grey and filthy now, and Mark could see darker stains seeping through across the palms.

<br/>

There was no ring on his finger. There was no carriage behind him in the lane. There was no horse.

<br/>

"I walked," Julian said. His voice was hoarse and very quiet. "From the capital. I didn't think anyone would look for me on foot." He swayed slightly on the step. "Forty miles. My father's men watch the roads. They don't watch the fields."

<br/>

Nobody in the workshop moved.

<br/>

Julian looked past Garek, into the lamplit room: at Lyra, at the workbench, with her graver in her hand and her ruined bracelet on her wrist. At Vespera, in the good chair, with the grey glyphs on the inside of her arm. At Torren, half-risen from his stool. At the dark back room and the grey crystals.

<br/>

And at Mark.

<br/>

"It's here, isn't it," Julian said. "The Auditor. I saw the lamps going out from the hill."

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark.

<br/>

Julian closed his eyes for a moment. When he opened them, they were red-rimmed and exhausted, and absolutely clear.

<br/>

"Then you need to know what the words mean," he said. "All three of them. What's on the other side of the dam." He swallowed. "And you need to know before it gets to the vault."

<br/>

Rain dripped from the doorframe. Above it, in her web, Legacy shifted one leg.

<br/>

Mark looked at Julian Thorne, standing on the step in the dark with his blistered hands, having walked forty miles through the fields to stand there.

<br/>

"Garek," he said quietly. "Let him in."

<br/>

Garek stepped aside.
