## Chapter 30: The Stroke Fallacy

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 21: Console Access (Written Fast)*
> *Location: base of the Anchor. Below Deep Vault 3. Below the rack room. Below the battery bank ('Grandmother'). Approx. 01:40.*
> *Status: Two-factor authentication passed (Keeper key + clean signature). Session open. Session lock engaged at foot of stair. ~40 constructs awake and waiting on the far side of it. They are very patient.*
> *Casualties: Vespera, both palms, second-degree, from the old staff. She chose it. Torren is holding her up and telling her about Bessie.*
> *Auditor: Left its grid for the first time. Heading here. ETA unknown.*
> *Aldric: Unknown. Assume coming.*
> *Objective: Read the source. Find the list. Find out who wrote the rules.*
> *Time remaining: Less than I would like.*
> *Gerald.*

<br/>

Mark shoved the ledger back into his satchel and turned to face the column.

<br/>

The surface waited.

<br/>

It was flat and dark and faintly luminous, like a still pool at night with a lamp somewhere far beneath it. It filled the whole face of the obsidian in front of the alcove, from the floor to well above his head and out to either side further than he could reach. And across its center, in clean silver-white glyphs, the single line still hung, patient and expectant.

<br/>

`[SESSION OPEN]`

<br/>

Nothing else happened.

<br/>

Mark waited. Lyra waited. Julian waited.

<br/>

The surface went on waiting too.

<br/>

"Well," said Lyra at last, in a whisper. "Do something."

<br/>

"I'm thinking."

<br/>

"Think *faster*."

<br/>

Mark reached out, a little cautiously, and touched the line of glyphs with one fingertip.

<br/>

The line vanished. The surface rippled, and smoothed, and a new block of glyphs wrote itself across the dark, neatly, in rows. Mark's translation layer caught them and laid them into his head:

<br/>

`[MEASURE]`
`[TRIBUTE]`
`[REGISTRY]`
`[RECONCILIATION]`
`[ARRIVALS]`
`[CONCORD RECORD]`

<br/>

"It's a menu," Mark said.

<br/>

"A *what*?"

<br/>

"A list of places you can go. Things you can look at." He stared at it. "It's a *menu*. A top-level navigation menu. Six items. No icons. No search bar." He reached out and touched the empty space below the last item, then the space above the first, then the corners of the surface, one after another, with growing urgency.

<br/>

Nothing happened.

<br/>

"What are you doing?" said Julian.

<br/>

"Looking for search."

<br/>

"For *what*?"

<br/>

"A way to type in what you want and have it *find* it," said Mark. "Instead of clicking through six layers of menus like a person trying to find the tax form on a government website." He stepped back and looked at the six glyphs with something approaching despair. "Five hundred years. *Five hundred years*, and nobody added *search*."

<br/>

From the far side of the cavern, at the foot of the stair, a voice boomed across the crystal forest.

<br/>

"*IS IT WORKING?*"

<br/>

It was Torren.

<br/>

"*IT'S LOADING!*" Mark shouted back.

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"*It's loading,*" Mark heard Torren report, at a slightly lower volume, to someone beside him.

<br/>

"*What does that mean?*" came Vespera's voice, faint and strained but still unmistakably Vespera.

<br/>

"*No idea,*" said Torren. "*Sounds serious, though.*"

---

Mark touched `[MEASURE]`.

<br/>

The menu vanished. Before anything else could appear, a single block of text wrote itself slowly across the center of the surface, very large, in a heavier stroke than anything else they'd seen.

<br/>

`[BY PROCEEDING, THE OPERATOR ACKNOWLEDGES THE MEASURE, ITS NECESSITY, AND ITS COST. THE OPERATOR ACCEPTS THAT WHAT IS READ HERE MAY NOT BE UNREAD.]`

<br/>

Beneath it, a single small glyph, glowing faintly.

<br/>

`[ACCEPT]`

<br/>

Mark's hand moved toward it automatically, the way a hand moves toward a light switch in a familiar room. He'd done it ten thousand times. Every software install, every website, every vendor portal, every app update on every phone he'd ever owned: a wall of text, a single button, and his finger on the button before his eyes had reached the second line.

<br/>

His fingertip was an inch from the glyph when he stopped.

<br/>

He stood there with his hand in the air.

<br/>

"Mark?" said Lyra.

<br/>

"Terms of service," Mark said slowly.

<br/>

He took his hand back. He put it in his pocket. Then he leaned forward, very close to the surface, and read the block of text again from the beginning. Every word. Slowly. Twice.

<br/>

*Acknowledges the Measure, its necessity, and its cost.*

<br/>

*What is read here may not be unread.*

<br/>

"Sixty years," Mark said quietly, to nobody in particular. "Sixty years. Thousands of agreements. Every one of them, I clicked *accept* without reading a single line." He straightened up. "This is the first time in my entire life I've ever actually read the terms of service."

<br/>

"Is it a good one?" asked Julian.

<br/>

Mark considered.

<br/>

"It's very short," he said. "And very honest. Which is how I know it's going to be bad."

<br/>

He touched `[ACCEPT]`.

---

The Measure, it turned out, had a version number.

<br/>

It was the first thing that wrote itself across the surface after the terms of service faded: a small block in the upper corner, the way a system's build information always sits in the upper corner, where nobody ever looks.

<br/>

`[THE MEASURE]`
`[ESTABLISHED: YEAR 1]`
`[LAST MODIFIED: YEAR 1]`

<br/>

Mark read it three times.

<br/>

"Last modified," he said, "Year *one*."

<br/>

"Is that bad?" said Lyra.

<br/>

"It means nobody's touched it. Not once. Not a single change, in five hundred years." He pressed his fingers to his forehead. "No updates. No fixes. No tuning. They wrote it, they turned it on, they locked the door, and they left. And everyone just kept *running it*." He laughed, slightly wildly. "Five hundred years of unapplied patches. Do you know what my old security team would have *done* to me if I'd shipped something and left it alone for five hundred *weeks*?"

<br/>

"What?" said Julian, genuinely curious.

<br/>

"I'd have had to do a *presentation*," said Mark darkly. "With *slides*."

<br/>

Julian looked at Lyra.

<br/>

"Forty-five," said Lyra, writing on her sleeve with a stub of charcoal, since her parchment was in her other apron. "Slides."

---

They split the work the way they'd split it during the triangulation, and it worked just as well down here as it had in the workshop.

<br/>

Mark drove. He touched the glyphs, navigated the menus, read the text as his translation layer laid it into his head, and called it out. Lyra stood on his right with her lens screwed into her eye and copied the shapes of anything that seemed important onto her sleeve, glyph by glyph, without interpreting. Julian stood on his left and read the parts that were written not in First Era script at all, but in a cramped, abbreviated notation that had been squeezed into the margins of almost every page.

<br/>

"It's Keeper shorthand," Julian said, frowning at it. "Father taught me. On the second landing. It's what the Keepers use in the inspection reports. It's not secret, exactly. It's just old. Nobody outside the Houses ever bothers to learn it."

<br/>

"Someone wrote Keeper shorthand in the margins of a five-hundred-year-old system console?" said Mark.

<br/>

Julian looked at the notes for a long moment.

<br/>

"Several someones," he said. "Over a long time. Different hands." He pointed. "That one's very old. That one's newer. That one…" He frowned. "That one just says *why?* Three times. And then nothing."

<br/>

Mark looked at the three small glyphs in the margin, cramped and faded. *Why? Why? Why?*

<br/>

He knew that note. He'd left it himself, in the comments of other people's code, more times than he could count.

<br/>

"Someone else came down here," he said slowly. "A long time ago. And read this. And didn't understand it either."

---

The first thing they read properly was the Concord's own record.

<br/>

Mark had expected it to be villainous. He'd been bracing for it since Aldric's study: some cold, grandiose declaration of power and control, the kind of thing a fantasy novel would have carved into black stone at the bottom of a dungeon. He had his skepticism ready, like a shield.

<br/>

It wasn't villainous.

<br/>

It was a design document.

<br/>

`[CONCORD RECORD — YEAR 1 OF THE MEASURE]`

<br/>

`[PROBLEM STATEMENT]`

<br/>

`[Clean casting draws its energy from the world: from the heat of the air, the earth, the living things nearby. Each clean spell, cast without loss, rings at a frequency that carries beyond the wall. In sufficient numbers, the ringing thins the wall between this world and the far side. Where the wall is thin, the cold comes through. Where the cold comes through, the heat of whole cities is drawn out in a single night.]`

<br/>

`[See: Varrow. Tessaly. The Nine Towns of the Lower March. The city of Aun.]`

<br/>

`[We were the ones who rang the bells. We did not know.]`

<br/>

Mark stopped reading aloud.

<br/>

He stood in front of the surface and read the last line again, silently.

<br/>

*We were the ones who rang the bells. We did not know.*

<br/>

"Mark?" said Lyra quietly.

<br/>

"It wasn't an invasion," Mark said. "The frozen cities. Julian's paintings." He shook his head slowly. "Nothing came through and froze them. *They* froze them. The First Era. With their own clean casting. Too much, too loud, all at once." He swallowed. "The shed. Lyra. The night in the shed. The iron block, and the frost, and the room dropping twenty degrees in a heartbeat. That's what they did. To a *city*. Without meaning to."

<br/>

Lyra had gone pale.

<br/>

He read on.

<br/>

`[SOLUTION]`

<br/>

`[All casting shall pay its cost in the body of the caster, rather than in the world. All instruments shall be made to lose. The lost heat shall be gathered through the earth and held, and the holding shall keep the wall. The world shall be quiet. The cost shall be pain. We accept the cost on behalf of all who come after, who will not be asked.]`

<br/>

`[This is a terrible thing we do. We have found no better thing.]`

<br/>

`[Let whoever reads this find one.]`

---

Mark stood very still in front of the surface for a long time.

<br/>

He had read a great many design documents in his life. Thousands. Most of them had been bad. A few had been very good. He'd only ever read a handful that had been honest: that had said, in plain words, *this is the tradeoff we made, and this is what it cost, and we are not sure we were right*.

<br/>

He'd never read one that apologized.

<br/>

*Let whoever reads this find one.*

<br/>

"Aldric was telling the truth," Julian said quietly beside him. "Wasn't he. About all of it."

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark. "About the dam. About the cost. About the bells." He looked at the last line. "He just stopped reading before the end."

<br/>

He touched the surface, and the Concord record faded, and the menu came back.

<br/>

There was a small note in the margin beside `[CONCORD RECORD]`, in Keeper shorthand. Julian leaned in to read it, and then went very quiet.

<br/>

"What does it say?" Mark asked.

<br/>

Julian didn't answer at first. Then he read it out, in a flat voice.

<br/>

"*Keepers shall maintain the seals, and shall not operate the console. One factor only. Let the Keepers keep. Let no single House hold the whole.*" He paused. "It's signed with the Concord seal."

<br/>

"So they *meant* it," said Lyra slowly. "The two-factor door. Giving you the key and banning clean casting. It wasn't a mistake. They did it on purpose."

<br/>

"Separation of duties," said Mark. "It's actually good practice. The person who guards the door shouldn't also be able to walk through it. You don't give the security guard the root password." He looked at Julian. "They didn't trust any one family with the whole thing. Not even yours. *Especially* not yours."

<br/>

Julian was staring at the note.

<br/>

"Twenty generations," he said. "Every one of them came down here. Every year. Wrote *nothing to report* on the wall." His voice was strange. "We thought we were the *Keepers of the Measure*. We thought we were its *guardians*. My father thinks he's the most important man in the province."

<br/>

"You were its guardians," said Mark gently.

<br/>

"We were its *janitors*," said Julian. "We checked the locks and swept the stairs. For five hundred years. And they never even told us what was behind the door." He laughed: a short, cracked, bitter sound, very unlike him. "My *father*—"

<br/>

He stopped. He didn't finish the sentence.

<br/>

Mark didn't make him.

---

`[TRIBUTE]` was the power budget.

<br/>

Mark knew it the moment the surface filled. He'd stared at a thousand dashboards in his life, in a thousand darkened operations rooms at three in the morning, and this one had the same unmistakable shape: a long column of names down the left side, a column of numbers beside each name, and across the top, a single line that mattered more than all the rest of them put together.

<br/>

`[WALL INTEGRITY: SUSTAINED]`
`[TRIBUTE RECEIVED (CURRENT CYCLE): 1.04 OF REQUIRED]`
`[MARGIN: 0.04]`

<br/>

"Four percent," Mark said quietly.

<br/>

"Is that good?" said Lyra.

<br/>

"It means the dam needs a hundred units of heat to keep the wall up, and it's getting a hundred and four." He looked at the number. "It's *running*. It's holding. But four percent isn't a margin. Four percent is a rounding error. In my old job, if a system was running at a four-percent margin, someone would have been woken up at two in the morning and told to fix it or find a new job."

<br/>

He touched the line, and it opened out into a long, slow curve: a graph, drawn in fine silver lines across the dark surface, running from left to right across what Mark realized, after a moment, was five hundred years.

<br/>

At the left edge, in Year 1, the line started high. Comfortably high. A thick, healthy margin, well above the threshold.

<br/>

Then, slowly, steadily, century after century, it came down.

<br/>

Not sharply. There were no cliffs, no sudden drops. Just a long, gentle, patient slide, the way a roof sags over a hundred years, the way a riverbank erodes. And over the last hundred years or so, it had flattened out just above the threshold line and stayed there, skimming along at a few percent, like a plane flying so low over the sea that its wheels were nearly touching the waves.

<br/>

"It's been falling," said Julian. "For five hundred years."

<br/>

"Why?" said Lyra. "If everyone's paying the tax—"

<br/>

"Because the tax isn't arriving." Mark touched the graph, and a small block of explanation wrote itself beneath it, with a cramped Keeper note in the margin beside it that just said *known*. "The pinch in the return channel is supposed to send the lost heat into the ground. Down the staff, through the heel, into the earth, into the trunks. Down here." He traced the curve. "But the heat doesn't all go into the ground, does it? Most of it goes into the caster's *hands*. Into the air. Into the sparks and the light and the noise and the screaming. Into the silk handkerchiefs Julian's mother sends him every month to wipe the sweat off his forehead." He glanced at Julian. "Sorry."

<br/>

"No," said Julian quietly. "You're right."

<br/>

"The Concord designed the pinch to lose heat into the earth. But over five hundred years, the Guild kept changing the staffs. Thicker insulation. Fancier grips. Silk wrappings. Ironwood to stop the heat burning people's hands." Mark shook his head slowly. "Every improvement that made it hurt a little less also stopped a little more of the heat getting to the ground. They were fixing the symptom and starving the dam. And nobody down here ever told anyone up there why it mattered."

<br/>

"*Why?*" read Julian, softly, from the margin. "*Why? Why?*"

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark.

<br/>

He was about to touch the surface and move on when Lyra said, "Wait."

<br/>

She was leaning very close to the column of names on the left side of the surface, with her lens. "These are places," she said. "Aren't they? Sectors. Like on the trunk labels." She pointed. "Sector 02. Sector 03. Sector 04—that's Oakhaven, that's us—"

<br/>

She stopped.

<br/>

Mark looked where she was pointing.

<br/>

Every number in the column was small and steady and grey, a little below where it had been a century ago, sliding gently down with the rest of the graph.

<br/>

Except one.

<br/>

`[SECTOR 04 — TRIBUTE: 1.31 OF EXPECTED]`
`[ANOMALY: SUSTAINED INCREASE, CURRENT CYCLE]`

<br/>

"A thirty-one percent increase," said Mark. "In Oakhaven. Over the last few months."

<br/>

"What does that mean?" said Lyra.

<br/>

Mark stared at the number for a long moment. Something tugged at the edge of his mind, the same faint, elusive something that had tugged at him on the ledge above the crystal forest. Heat. The ground. Where it went.

<br/>

He reached for it, and it slipped away again.

<br/>

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Something in Oakhaven started paying a lot more tax a few months ago. And I don't know what." He took out his ledger and wrote it down, quickly, at the bottom of Log 21: *Sector 04 tribute anomaly: +31%. Cause unknown. Investigate later.*

<br/>

He underlined *investigate later* twice, and put the ledger away.

---

`[RECONCILIATION]` was the list.

<br/>

It filled the surface in a long, neat column, line after line after line, in the same clean silver glyphs as everything else. Each line was a name, and beside each name was a sector, and a short reason, and a single word.

<br/>

`[SCHEDULED]`

<br/>

Mark read the first line, and felt Lyra go still beside him.

<br/>

`[VESPERA — SECTOR 04 — UNSANCTIONED CLEAN INSTRUMENT; UNSANCTIONED CLEAN CASTING — RESTORATION SCHEDULED]`

<br/>

The second line:

<br/>

`[VALERIUS — SECTOR 04 — FALSIFIED INSPECTION RECORD; CONCEALMENT OF DRIFT — RESTORATION SCHEDULED]`

<br/>

And the third, and the fourth, and on down the surface. Forty-eight names in all. Most of them Mark didn't recognize. A tanner on Ash Street. A cooper. A midwife in the river quarter who, it seemed, had been warming her hands over a recharged crystal before deliveries for the last two months, and had quietly started warming the mothers' backs as well. A carter. Two of the dockhands from the Infirmary line.

<br/>

And, two-thirds of the way down:

<br/>

`[TESSA FENN — SECTOR 04 — EFFICIENCY ANOMALY (NATIVE): LOSS BELOW TOLERANCE — RESTORATION SCHEDULED]`

<br/>

"*Tessa*," Lyra breathed.

<br/>

"She never cast clean," said Mark. His voice was very flat. "Not once. She cast *dirty*. With a chipped wooden rod she couldn't afford to replace. She just taught herself to waste less." He stared at the line. "*Efficiency anomaly.* They're going to restore her for being *too good at the bad way*."

<br/>

Julian said nothing. He was looking at Tessa's name with an expression Mark couldn't read. *Firewood*, Mark remembered. *You called her rod firewood. In front of the whole class.*

<br/>

He scrolled down. The list went on. Forty-eight names. Forty-eight people who would wake up on some morning in the next few days and find that the clean, quiet, painless way of doing something they'd learned had been closed inside them, pinched back to specification, or closed so far that they couldn't do it at all.

<br/>

At the bottom of the list, as they watched, the surface flickered.

<br/>

A new line wrote itself, all at once, not at the bottom of the list but at the very *top*, above Vespera, above all of them, and it glowed brighter than any of the others, a hard, cold, brilliant white.

<br/>

`[SESSION HOLDER — SECTOR 04 — PRIORITY: CRITICAL]`

<br/>

Beside it, where the reason should have been, there was only a blank space.

<br/>

And beside that, where the single word should have been, there wasn't *scheduled*.

<br/>

There was a number. And it was getting smaller.

<br/>

"What's that?" whispered Lyra.

<br/>

"A timer," said Mark.

<br/>

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. Everyone at the alcove understood what was coming down the stair toward the session holder, and roughly how fast.

---

Then Mark touched `[ARRIVALS]`, and the cavern seemed to go very quiet.

<br/>

It didn't start with a list. It started with another block of Concord text, older and more cramped than the design document, and it began with the three words that had been chasing Mark through archives and gate inscriptions and old men's mumbling for two months.

<br/>

He couldn't read them.

<br/>

He stood in front of the surface and looked at the first line, and there was a gap in it. Not a blank. Not a smudge. A *gap*, a clean, smooth hole in the middle of the sentence, three glyphs wide, as though somebody had cut a neat rectangle out of the page and the words on either side had drawn politely back from the edge of it.

<br/>

`[CONCERNING THE                 :]`

<br/>

"Julian," he said. His voice sounded strange to him. "Read me the first line."

<br/>

Julian leaned in.

<br/>

"*Concerning the Advenai Ulteri Exori*," he read. "Those who arrive from the far side, arisen from outside."

<br/>

Mark nodded slowly. "Go on. Read it all. Out loud. I'll… I'll follow along where I can."

<br/>

Julian looked at him oddly. Then he read.

---

The Concord had known exactly what the *Advenai* were.

<br/>

They had written it down, in Year 1, in the same plain, honest, apologetic voice as the design document. Julian read it slowly, line by line, and Mark followed along on the surface, reading the parts his translation layer would let him read, which was most of it, with a three-glyph hole every few lines like a missing tooth.

<br/>

*They are not monsters,* the Concord had written. *They are people.*

<br/>

*They come from beyond the wall, from other worlds than ours, as many and as various as the stars. When our world rings loud, and the wall grows thin, they fall through, as a coin falls through a worn pocket. They do not choose to come. Most come at a moment of great crisis in their own world, when something in them is already loosening its hold on the place where they were. They arrive whole and hale, and the wall remakes them as it lets them through: young, unscarred, able. They arrive speaking our tongue, because the Measure gives it to them, so that they will not be afraid.*

<br/>

Julian's voice had begun to shake. He kept reading.

<br/>

*And they cast clean.*

<br/>

*Almost at once. Within days, or weeks. Because no one has ever taught them that the Art must hurt. They come from worlds where it does not, or where there is no Art at all, and they look at our staffs and our pain and our forty-line incantations, and they see only waste. And they are right. And so they make it better.*

<br/>

*And every clean spell they cast rings. And every ring thins the wall. And every thinning lets another fall through. Who casts clean. Who rings.*

<br/>

Julian stopped reading.

<br/>

Nobody in the alcove said anything.

<br/>

"It's a feedback loop," said Mark at last, very quietly. "A positive feedback loop. Arrivals cast clean. Clean casting rings. Ringing thins the wall. A thinner wall lets in more arrivals. Who cast clean." He stared at the surface. "Each one makes the next one more likely. And it accelerates. Until a whole city goes cold in one night." He closed his eyes for a moment. "That's what happened in the First Era. Isn't it. They didn't *mean* to freeze those cities. It just… ran away from them."

<br/>

"Keep reading," said Lyra, in a very small voice.

<br/>

Julian kept reading.

<br/>

*We cannot stop them coming. The wall is the wall, and it has always been thin. We can only stop them from ringing. And so we have done a terrible thing. Each one who comes through shall be found, and held, before they learn to ring. Gently, if it can be done. Held, not harmed. Kept quiet, below, until a better way is found.*

<br/>

*We call this correction. It is not correction. It is only a door, closed.*

<br/>

*Let whoever reads this find a better way, and open it.*

<br/>

Julian's voice broke on the last line. He stood very still in the red-gold light, with his bandaged hands pressed flat against his chest, and didn't read anything else for a long time.

<br/>

"Held," he said finally. "Not harmed. *Held*."

<br/>

"Below," said Mark.

<br/>

"She's alive." Julian turned to look at him. His eyes were wet. "The girl. From the coast. They didn't—she's not—" He couldn't finish.

<br/>

Mark reached out and touched the surface, below the block of Concord text, and the list appeared.

---

It was a short list. Shorter than the reconciliation list by far. A few dozen lines, covering five hundred years.

<br/>

`[ARRIVAL — SECTOR 07 — YEAR 14 — HELD]`
`[ARRIVAL — SECTOR 02 — YEAR 38 — HELD]`
`[ARRIVAL — SECTOR 11 — YEAR 61 — HELD]`

<br/>

The years ran down the list in an uneven, irregular sequence, sometimes decades apart, sometimes only a year or two. Line after line ended with the same word.

<br/>

`[HELD]`

<br/>

Thirty-one people, over five hundred years, from thirty-one other worlds. Every one of them had arrived somewhere in the Empire, young and whole and speaking the language, and had looked at a staff and a chant and a burned hand, and seen waste, and tried to make it better.

<br/>

And every one of them was below. Somewhere. Still.

<br/>

*Kept quiet, below, until a better way is found.*

<br/>

Nobody had ever found one.

<br/>

Julian had moved up beside Mark and was reading the list with his lips moving. Near the bottom, he stopped, and pointed with one shaking bandaged finger.

<br/>

`[ARRIVAL — SECTOR 11 (COAST) — YEAR 499 — HELD]`

<br/>

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

<br/>

Mark looked at the line for a long time. Year 499. Last winter. A fishing village on the coast. A girl in a too-big smock, standing very small in a torchlit courtyard, with four Keepers and a construct around her, and Julian watching from across the yard. Whoever she was, and wherever she'd come from, and whatever she'd been trying to make better.

<br/>

`[HELD]`

<br/>

"She's alive," Julian whispered. "She's alive. Somewhere."

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark.

<br/>

And then, because he was an engineer, and because he'd been trained his whole life to read every line of a log right to the end, and because there was one more line on the list below hers, he looked down.

---

The last line on the list didn't say *held*.

<br/>

It didn't say anything Mark could read at first. It was brighter than the others, the same hard, cold white as the line that had appeared at the top of the reconciliation list, and it was shorter, and the beginning of it was a gap.

<br/>

A clean, smooth, three-glyph hole in the light.

<br/>

`[                 — SECTOR 04 — YEAR 500]`

<br/>

Mark looked at it.

<br/>

He thought, very calmly, *That's a rendering error.*

<br/>

He thought, *The filter's catching the term in the record header. It catches it everywhere. That's all it is. It's a display bug. The line could belong to anyone. Year 500. Sector 04. There are thousands of people in Sector 04.*

<br/>

He reached out, and his hand was perfectly steady, and he touched the line.

<br/>

It opened.

<br/>

The surface rippled, and the list slid away, and the line expanded to fill the dark, one row at a time, the way a record expands when you click on it in any system in any world: a header, and then the fields beneath it, one after another, each one writing itself out in clean silver-white glyphs, and each one landing in Mark's head with the soft, neat, terrible click of his translation layer catching it and turning it and setting it down.

<br/>

`[                 ]`

<br/>

`[UNREGISTERED ENTITY — ORIGIN: EXTERNAL]`

<br/>

`[ARRIVAL: SECTOR 04 — RIVER, NORTH OF OAKHAVEN]`

<br/>

There was a date beside it. Mark didn't need to work it out. He knew the date. He'd written it at the top of the first page of his ledger, in neat English cursive, sitting on a bench outside a scribe's shop with three coppers' worth of charcoal: *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance. Log 01.* He'd worked it out afterward, counting back the days. The morning he woke up by the river.

<br/>

`[ORIGIN EVENT: TRANSIT TRAUMA]`
`[PRESENTATION: CONSISTENT WITH CARDIOVASCULAR EVENT]`
`[CONSTRICTION OF CHEST. RADIATING PAIN, LEFT NECK AND ARM. METALLIC TASTE. NARROWING OF VISION. LOSS OF CONSCIOUSNESS.]`

<br/>

Mark stopped breathing.

<br/>

*A strange tightness gripped his chest. It felt like an invisible vise clamping down on his sternum, followed by a sharp, electric burning sensation creeping up the left side of his neck. The metallic taste of copper blossomed across the back of his tongue. His vision tubed down to a pinprick.*

<br/>

*His left arm felt like it had been filled with liquid lead.*

<br/>

He had never told anyone those details. Not one. Not Vespera. Not Lyra. Not in the ledger. He'd never even written them down. He'd only ever thought them, once, in the dark, slumped forward over a mechanical keyboard in Raleigh with the backspace key held down under his forehead, as the darkness pulled him under.

<br/>

*Ah. Massive cardiac event. Or a massive stroke.*

<br/>

`[BODY: RECONSTITUTED — JUVENILE TEMPLATE]`

<br/>

`[LANGUAGE INTERFACE: INSTALLED]`

<br/>

`[CONTENT FILTER: ACTIVE — TERM SUPPRESSED:                  ]`

<br/>

He read the last line three times.

<br/>

*Term suppressed.*

<br/>

And beside it, the same clean, smooth, three-glyph hole.

<br/>

He knew what was in the hole. He'd known since the gate inscription. He'd written it on the whitewashed wall of the workshop in large capitals only a few days ago, and drawn a box around it, and coined an acronym for it, and put it on the list of *Strange Things Mark Says*.

<br/>

*It is not about me,* he had written, in Log 15. *It is about them.*

<br/>

*The filter in my head is likely the same filter that blinds every reader of First Era script.*

<br/>

It wasn't. It had never been. Vespera could read the roots. Julian could read the gate. Mott, in his grey wool, could say the words out loud and correct their stress. Every reader in Oakhaven could see those three glyphs. Every reader in the world.

<br/>

Except one.

<br/>

The filter wasn't in the script. It had never been in the script.

<br/>

It was in *him*. It had been installed in him, by the same system that had installed the language, on the morning he woke up by the river, for exactly one purpose. So that no arrival could ever read its own name.

<br/>

`[REGISTRATION: NONE]`

<br/>

`[STATUS: NOT HELD. LOCATION: UNRESOLVED.]`

<br/>

The last field finished writing itself, and the surface went still.

---

Nobody said anything.

<br/>

The crystals hummed. Far away, at the foot of the stair, Torren was murmuring something low and steady to Vespera. The silver curtain shimmered. Forty constructs stood on the stair beyond it, waiting.

<br/>

Mark realized that he was sitting down.

<br/>

He didn't remember deciding to. He was sitting on the smooth raised step at the mouth of the alcove, with his back against the cool black stone of the column, and his satchel in his lap, and his hands resting on the satchel, and he was looking at nothing.

<br/>

*Unregistered entity.* That was why the Auditor's page had stayed blank. That was why the rings had never once flared at him, in the arena, at dinner, on the stairs. That was why the construct in the archive had come for him, not because of the clean casting, or not *only* because of the clean casting, but because *TARGET CLASS* had three glyphs on it, and he was those three glyphs.

<br/>

That was why Mott had asked.

<br/>

*Are you one of the new ones?*

<br/>

*Yes,* he'd said. Every night. For months.

<br/>

*I know,* Mott had said.

<br/>

"Mark," said Lyra.

<br/>

She was crouching in front of him. He hadn't seen her move. Her lens was pushed up on her forehead and her face was very close and very pale, and she was looking at him with an expression he'd never seen on her before.

<br/>

"Mark. What did it say?"

<br/>

He looked at her. He opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

<br/>

"Julian," Lyra said, without taking her eyes off Mark, "read it to me."

<br/>

Julian read it.

<br/>

He read it slowly, the whole record, top to bottom, out loud, in a voice that got quieter and quieter as he went. He read the three words in the header that Mark couldn't see. He read *unregistered entity*. He read *origin: external*. He read *river, north of Oakhaven*, and *transit trauma*, and *constriction of chest*, and *juvenile template*, and *language interface*, and *content filter*. He read *term suppressed*, and then he read, quietly, into the silence, the three words that the filter had suppressed.

<br/>

Then he stopped.

<br/>

Lyra was still crouched in front of Mark. She hadn't moved through any of it. When Julian finished, she stayed exactly where she was, looking at Mark's face.

<br/>

"You're one of them," she said. "Aren't you."

<br/>

Mark looked back at her.

<br/>

"Yes," he said.

---

He told them.

<br/>

He hadn't meant to. He'd never told anyone, in this world, all of it. Vespera knew pieces. Torren knew less. Lyra knew that he talked strangely, and wrote in a script nobody could read, and had a list of strange things he said that had reached forty-five entries. None of them knew about the desk.

<br/>

So he told them about the desk.

<br/>

He told them about a city called Raleigh, in a place called North Carolina, in a world with no Aether and two moons fewer than this one, where it rained on the pine trees and he had lived for thirty years in a house with a home office and a second monitor. He told them he'd been sixty years old. He told them about his knees, and his lumbar spine, and his arthritic knuckles that clicked on the keys, and his reading glasses, which he'd worn every day for forty years and was still, every single morning, reaching up to push up his nose.

<br/>

He told them about the night. Eleven forty-two on a Thursday, and the rain on the roof, and a message on the screen from a man named Brad.

<br/>

"Brad?" said Julian.

<br/>

"Brad," said Mark. "He was my manager. He was twenty-nine years old. He wanted me to rebuild the entire authentication system overnight." He was quiet for a moment. "He's on the list. The strange things list. Number one, I think."

<br/>

"Number three," said Lyra softly. "Number one is 'Gerald.'"

<br/>

Mark almost smiled.

<br/>

He told them about the tightness in his chest, and the burning in his neck, and the taste of copper, and the dark coming in from the edges, and his forehead on the keyboard, and the backspace key. And he told them what he'd thought, there at the very end, sliding down into the dark.

<br/>

*Massive stroke. Still less painful than the eight a.m. stand-up.*

<br/>

"And then I woke up by the river," he said. "In this body. And I thought: *Well. That's it. I'm dying, and this is the last thing my brain is doing. A nice dream. Endorphins. Teenage retinas.*" He looked at his hands. "And then a pig-bear sneezed on me, and I cut my arm on a piece of slate, and I was hungry, and I saw two moons, and I thought, *All right. Not a dream.*"

<br/>

He looked up at the column.

<br/>

"But I never stopped thinking it was a stroke," he said. "Not really. Not underneath. Even after I knew it wasn't a dream. Even after I knew it was real. I just… moved the stroke. I decided I'd died, there, at my desk, and this was some kind of… afterward. A second chance. A reward. A *glitch*." He laughed, very slightly, and it didn't sound like a laugh. "I never once asked how I *got* here. Not seriously. I asked how everything *else* worked. Every lamp. Every blower. Every crystal. I took apart the whole world to see how it was built." He stopped. "And I never once took apart the thing that put me in it."

<br/>

Nobody said anything.

<br/>

"Because a stroke has a diagnosis," Mark said quietly. "You can *file* a stroke. It goes in the record. It has a code. People understand it. *He was sixty, he worked too hard, he had a stroke at his desk.* That's a story. It ends." He looked at the record on the surface, at the three-glyph hole at the top of it, the one only he couldn't see. "*This*, I couldn't file."

<br/>

His hand went up, without his deciding it, to the bridge of his nose. To push up glasses that weren't there.

<br/>

This time, he didn't lower it.

<br/>

He sat there on the step at the foot of the Anchor, at the bottom of the world, with his fingertips resting on bare skin, and closed his eyes.

<br/>

"It was never a stroke," he said.

---

Lyra sat down on the step beside him.

<br/>

She didn't say anything clever. She didn't say anything at all for a while. She just sat down next to him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and put her hands in her lap, and looked out at the crystal forest with him.

<br/>

Then she said, "You're one of the new ones."

<br/>

"Yes."

<br/>

Lyra thought about this for a long moment.

<br/>

"Okay," she said.

<br/>

She reached over and took his free hand, the one that wasn't pressed against the bridge of his nose, and held it, very firmly, in both of hers, the way she'd held his fist on the edge of the arena before the final, with her smudged, calloused, silver-polished engraver's fingers.

<br/>

"You're still *our* new one," she said.

<br/>

Mark didn't open his eyes. But his fingers closed around hers.

---

Julian stood alone in the mouth of the alcove.

<br/>

He'd read every line of the record aloud. He'd read the three words in the header. He was standing very still now, with his bandaged hands at his sides, looking down at the boy sitting on the step: the boy who'd beaten him in the arena without lifting a finger, who'd run across the scorched sand with a length of copper wire, who'd said *because you're not the one who built it*.

<br/>

*Advenai*, his father had said, in the torchlit courtyard. *Something from the far side, that came through. Wearing one. Like a coat.*

<br/>

*It's a mercy, Julian. For her, and for us.*

<br/>

Mark opened his eyes and looked up at him.

<br/>

Julian's face was very pale. For a long moment, Mark could see everything he'd been taught warring with everything he'd seen, right there in the red-gold light: a year of trying to believe, a ring on his finger, a construct on the stairs, a key in his mother's hand, forty miles of fields in the rain.

<br/>

Then something in his face gave way.

<br/>

"She was a girl," Julian said.

<br/>

His voice cracked on it.

<br/>

"The one on the coast. In the courtyard. In the big smock." He swallowed. "She wasn't wearing anything. She wasn't a *thing*. She was just a girl. Like you're just—" He stopped. "She was just a *girl*."

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark quietly. "I think she was."

<br/>

Julian stood there for a moment longer, with his hands shaking at his sides.

<br/>

Then he sat down on the step too, on Mark's other side, carefully, in Torren's enormous shirt, and put his bandaged face in his bandaged hands, and didn't say anything else for a long time.

<br/>

Nobody asked him to.

---

Mark went to tell the others himself.

<br/>

He didn't send Lyra. He didn't shout it across the cavern. He got up off the step, after a while, and walked across the floor of the crystal forest to the foot of the stair, where Vespera was sitting on the bottom step with her blistered hands wrapped in strips of Torren's apron and her old ironwood staff across her knees, and Torren was standing over her like a wall, watching the silver curtain and the forty patient silver figures beyond it.

<br/>

They both looked up as he came.

<br/>

He told them. Plainly, without decoration, the way he'd once told a CEO about a breach. *Unregistered entity. Origin: external. The river. The desk. The chest pain. It was never a stroke. I'm one of them. I'm what Gerald was built to hunt.*

<br/>

When he finished, Torren looked at him for a long, long moment.

<br/>

Then he shrugged his enormous shoulders.

<br/>

"Well," said Torren. "You're still Mark."

<br/>

And that, apparently, was the end of it, as far as Torren was concerned. He turned back to watching the curtain. After a moment, without looking around, he reached out one huge, soot-stained hand and patted Mark twice on the head, the way he patted Bessie.

<br/>

Mark stood there and found that he couldn't say anything at all.

<br/>

Vespera hadn't spoken. She was looking up at him from the bottom step, in the red-gold light, with her burned hands in her lap.

<br/>

"You knew," Mark said.

<br/>

"I knew you were sixty," said Vespera. "And that you came from a new world. You told me, the night I couldn't learn to cast silently. You said it as if it was nothing." She looked at him for a long moment. "I wondered if you'd ever say it as if it was something."

<br/>

"Did you know I was *this*?"

<br/>

"No," said Vespera honestly. "Not this. Not the words. I didn't let myself put it together." She looked down at her wrapped hands. "I suppose we all have things we don't let ourselves put together."

<br/>

She looked back up at him.

<br/>

"I wondered," said Vespera, "when you'd tell *yourself*."

---

There was one more question.

<br/>

Mark had known it was coming since the record opened. He'd been carrying it across the cavern and back, under his ribs, like a stone in his pocket. He went back to the alcove, and stood in front of the surface, and looked at his own record, with Lyra and Julian on the step behind him.

<br/>

Below *STATUS*, at the very bottom of the record, there was one more field he hadn't touched. It was small. It sat by itself.

<br/>

`[ORIGIN]`

<br/>

He touched it.

<br/>

He thought about the house in Raleigh. The office. The second monitor. The rain on the loblolly pines. The backspace key. He thought about whether anyone had come in the morning. Whether his phone had kept buzzing on the desk. Whether there was a stone somewhere in North Carolina with his name on it, or a bed in an ICU with a machine breathing beside it, or nothing at all, because the wall had simply opened and taken him, the whole of him, and left an empty chair.

<br/>

"Am I dead there?" he asked the column.

<br/>

The field opened.

<br/>

`[ORIGIN STATUS: UNKNOWN]`

<br/>

That was all.

<br/>

Mark stood and looked at it for a very long time.

<br/>

*Unknown.* Not dead. Not alive. Not a diagnosis. Not a code. Not a story that ended. Just a field in a record, in a system five hundred years old, that didn't know, and had never known, and had never been built to find out.

<br/>

He'd spent forty years asking systems questions. He'd learned a long time ago that *unknown* was the most honest answer a system could ever give you, and the hardest one to live with.

<br/>

"Mark?" said Lyra softly, behind him.

<br/>

"It doesn't know," said Mark.

<br/>

He found, to his surprise, that he was all right. Not happy. Not even close. But all right, in the way that a man is all right when a test result comes back and it isn't good news, but at least it isn't a lie.

<br/>

He reached up and pushed the empty air in front of his face, where his glasses would have been, one more time, out of forty years of habit.

<br/>

Then he lowered his hand, and turned back to the menu.

---

"Right," said Mark.

<br/>

He said it the way he'd said it a thousand times, at the end of a thousand bad meetings, in a thousand operations rooms, when the post-mortem was over and the slides were closed and somebody had to stand up and say *what do we actually do now*.

<br/>

Lyra looked up from the step. So did Julian.

<br/>

"Right," Mark said again. "We came down here to read the source. We've read it. We know what the Measure is. We know what it costs. We know the dam's running on a four-percent margin and falling. We know what the *Advenai* are." He didn't let his voice change on the word. "We know what correction means for them, and what it means for Vespera. And we know there's a list with forty-eight names on it, and a timer at the top, and something coming down the stairs to execute it." He touched the surface, and the menu came back. "So now we stop it."

<br/>

"*Can* we?" said Lyra.

<br/>

"I'm logged into the admin console of the system that runs the entire world," said Mark. "I'm going to find out."

<br/>

He opened `[RECONCILIATION]`.

<br/>

The list filled the surface again. Forty-eight names. Vespera at the top, under the hard white line with the shrinking number beside it. Valerius. The tanner. The midwife. Tessa Fenn. Mark touched Vespera's line, and it opened into a small record of its own, with her name and her sector and her offense, and beneath it, a short row of glyphs that he recognized at once, with a jolt, as *actions*. Things you could *do* to a record.

<br/>

`[VIEW]` `[DEFER]` `[RESCIND]`

<br/>

He reached out and touched `[RESCIND]`.

<br/>

The surface flickered. The glyph dimmed. And a single line wrote itself across the center of the dark, in a flat, neutral, perfectly polite grey:

<br/>

`[INSUFFICIENT PRIVILEGE.]`

<br/>

Mark stared at it.

<br/>

He touched `[DEFER]`.

<br/>

`[INSUFFICIENT PRIVILEGE.]`

<br/>

He went back to the menu. He opened `[MEASURE]`, and found a long list of settings he only half-understood, with numbers beside them that he could guess the meaning of, and he touched one, very carefully, just to see.

<br/>

`[INSUFFICIENT PRIVILEGE.]`

<br/>

He tried `[TRIBUTE]`. `[ARRIVALS]`. He tried touching his own record and opening it and touching `[STATUS]`.

<br/>

`[INSUFFICIENT PRIVILEGE.]`

<br/>

`[INSUFFICIENT PRIVILEGE.]`

<br/>

`[INSUFFICIENT PRIVILEGE.]`

<br/>

"*Mark*," said Lyra, standing up.

<br/>

"I know." His voice was very level. He'd been here before too. Every engineer had. You fight your way through the authentication, through the firewall, through two-factor and the session lock and the terms of service, and you finally get your hands on the console, and it lets you look at everything, and it won't let you *touch* a single thing.

<br/>

"Read-only," he said. "I've got read-only access."

<br/>

"*Why?*" said Julian.

<br/>

"Let's find out." Mark touched the polite grey line, and it opened.

<br/>

`[OPERATOR: UNREGISTERED ENTITY.]`
`[UNREGISTERED ENTITIES ARE GRANTED READ ACCESS ONLY.]`
`[TO RECEIVE OPERATOR PRIVILEGES, THE OPERATOR MUST BE REGISTERED.]`

<br/>

Below it, one more small glyph glowed patiently.

<br/>

`[REGISTER]`

<br/>

Mark looked at it.

<br/>

Then, very carefully, without touching it, he touched the line above it, the one about registration, to see what it would say.

<br/>

`[REGISTRATION ENTERS THE OPERATOR INTO THE REGISTRY OF THE MEASURE, WITH FULL RECORD, CLASS, AND STATUS.]`
`[A REGISTERED OPERATOR RECEIVES FULL PRIVILEGES TO MODIFY THE MEASURE.]`
`[A REGISTERED OPERATOR IS SUBJECT TO THE MEASURE.]`

<br/>

And then, beneath it, in the same flat, neutral, perfectly polite grey, as if it were mentioning the weather:

<br/>

`[NOTE: OPERATOR CLASS IS                 . UPON REGISTRATION, STANDARD PROTOCOL APPLIES. CORRECTION WILL BE SCHEDULED.]`

<br/>

The three-glyph hole sat in the middle of the line like a missing tooth.

<br/>

Mark didn't need Julian to read it to him this time.

---

He stood in front of the surface for a long time, and read the whole of it again, slowly, from the top. The way he'd read the terms of service. Every word.

<br/>

Then he turned around.

<br/>

"To get write access," he said, "I have to register."

<br/>

"So register," said Lyra at once.

<br/>

"And the moment I'm registered," said Mark, "the system knows exactly what I am. Class, record, status. Everything. It writes me into the Registry. And the Measure does to registered *Advenai* what it's done to every registered *Advenai* for five hundred years." He looked at the arrivals list, at the long column of the same word. "It schedules a correction. At the top of the list. *Held, below.*"

<br/>

Lyra's face went white.

<br/>

"So I get root access," Mark said quietly, "and the first thing root access does is put me on the list."

<br/>

Nobody said anything.

<br/>

The crystals hummed. In the forest, Grandmother glowed her patient red.

<br/>

"It's actually a very elegant design," Mark said, after a while, in a strange, flat, conversational voice. "I'd have admired it, in another life. You build a system to catch people from outside. And then you lock the console so that only people from *inside* can change it. Keepers have the key, but they can't cast clean. Natives can cast clean, but they can't get past the filter on the arrival records." He gestured at the surface. "And an arrival like me can get all the way down here, and pass both factors, and read *everything*… and the only way to change a single line is to hand myself over to the thing I'm trying to fix."

<br/>

"There must be another way," said Julian.

<br/>

"There's always another way," said Mark. "I just haven't found it." He looked up at the surface, at the patient glyph, at the hole. "*Let whoever reads this find a better way.* They wrote that five hundred years ago. And then they built a console that makes sure anyone who could find one has to put themselves in a cell to use it."

<br/>

"So we leave," said Lyra fiercely. "We go back up. We find another way. We have *six days*—"

<br/>

"We don't," said Mark.

<br/>

He pointed at the top of the reconciliation list. At the hard white line. At the number beside it, which had been getting smaller since they first opened the list, and was now much, much smaller.

<br/>

"*Session holder,*" he said. "Priority critical. The Auditor isn't on its grid anymore. It isn't waiting six days. It's coming here, now, for whoever's at this console." He paused. "And when it gets here, it doesn't just come for me. It reconciles. The whole list. All at once. Vespera. Valerius. Tessa. Forty-eight people." He looked at the number. "We don't have six days. We have *that*."

---

He turned away from the surface, because he couldn't look at the number anymore, and his eye fell on the small block of build information in the upper corner, where nobody ever looks.

<br/>

`[THE MEASURE]`
`[ESTABLISHED: YEAR 1]`
`[LAST MODIFIED: YEAR 1]`
`[WALL INTEGRITY: SUSTAINED]`

<br/>

And below those, one line he hadn't noticed before. A single small field, on its own, at the very bottom of the block.

<br/>

`[EXTERNAL ATTENTION: LOW]`

<br/>

Mark looked at it for a long moment.

<br/>

*External attention.*

<br/>

Attention from where? Attention from *what*?

<br/>

*Loud things are heard,* Aldric had said, turning his teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer. *Heard by what? That isn't mine to tell.*

<br/>

Nothing on the console explained it. Nothing in the Concord record mentioned it. It was just a field, in the corner, reading *low*, the way a gauge on a dashboard reads *normal* right up until the moment it doesn't.

<br/>

He filed it, in the back of his mind, in the drawer where he kept things he didn't have time to be frightened of yet.

<br/>

Then he turned back to Lyra and Julian, and opened his mouth to say *Right* a third time.

<br/>

Before he could, Torren's voice came booming across the cavern from the foot of the stair, and it wasn't asking whether it was working anymore.

<br/>

"*MARK!*"

---

They all turned.

<br/>

At the top of the stair, up on the ledge, in the mouth of the dark passage that led back to the rack room, the long silver line of constructs had begun to move.

<br/>

Not toward the curtain. *Aside*.

<br/>

One by one, smoothly, silently, the tall silver figures were stepping out of the center of the stair and pressing themselves back against the rock wall, the way a crowd in a narrow corridor presses back against the walls to let someone important through. Their blank oval faces turned, all together, up toward the passage.

<br/>

And down the middle of the stair, between them, came the Auditor.

<br/>

It walked at exactly the same speed it had always walked. It didn't hurry. It didn't slow. Its grey robe hung straight to the steps without a wrinkle. It held its grey book in one hand and its slender silver rod in the other, and on the front of its robe, over the place where a heart would have been, a single glyph glowed silver-white: the same silver-white as the light on the column, and the glyph at the top of the Anchor, and the line at the top of the list.

<br/>

It came down the stair, step by step, through the parted ranks of constructs, all the way to the bottom.

<br/>

It stopped in front of the shimmering silver curtain.

<br/>

On the other side of the curtain, two steps away, Vespera rose slowly to her feet, with her wrapped and burning hands gripping the old ironwood staff. Torren moved in front of her without a word, his feet set wide and his fists raised, a quarter-dwarf blacksmith in a good apron standing between a mage and a thing that had never once been stopped.

<br/>

The Auditor didn't look at either of them.

<br/>

It raised its silver rod, and touched the tip of it, very precisely, to the face of the curtain.

<br/>

The curtain flickered.

<br/>

Then, very slowly, starting from the point where the rod touched it, the shimmering silver light began to thin, the way frost thins on a window when you hold a warm hand against it. Not breaking. Not tearing. Just fading, a little at a time, from the center outward, steadily, patiently, inevitably.

<br/>

On the surface of the column behind Mark, a new line wrote itself, in hard, bright white, across the top of everything.

<br/>

`[RECONCILIATION: IMMINENT]`

---

Mark stood in the mouth of the alcove and looked across the cavern at the thinning curtain, and the grey figure behind it, and Vespera and Torren in front of it.

<br/>

Then he looked back at the surface. At the reconciliation list, and forty-eight names, and the hard white line at the top of it. At the little patient glyph that said `[REGISTER]`. At the three-glyph hole where his name should have been.

<br/>

Then he took out his ledger.

<br/>

He did it without thinking, the way he'd done it a hundred times, in a hundred bad moments: in a coal-pit shed, in a scrap yard, in a frozen archive, on a carriage seat, in the dark. He opened it to the page where Log 21 sat, half-finished, written fast at the top of the session. He turned back two logs, to Log 19, and then to Log 20, and found the line under *Personnel* that he'd left blank in both of them. The one that said *Me:*, and nothing else.

<br/>

He uncapped his charcoal stick.

<br/>

And on the third blank line, at the bottom of Log 21, in the same neat, small English cursive he'd used every day since the bench outside the scribe's shop, he finally wrote it.

<br/>

> *Personnel:*
> *- Me: Advenai Ulteri Exori. Arrived from outside.*
> *It was never a stroke.*

<br/>

He looked at it for a moment.

<br/>

He could read every word of it. Of course he could. It was in English. It was in his own hand. The filter had never been able to touch his own handwriting.

<br/>

Then he closed the ledger, put it back in his satchel, and turned to face the console.
