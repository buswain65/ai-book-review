## Chapter 10: Resource Allocation & Hardware Hacking

<br/>

The news of what happened on the south road traveled through Oakhaven's lower district with surprising speed—and, like all news, it got significantly more impressive with every retelling.

<br/>

By the time Mark arrived at the south armory forge the next morning, the usual ambient chatter of the laborers died down, replaced by wide-eyed glances and hushed whispers.

<br/>

"—took down *five* of them—"

<br/>

"—I heard he breathed fire on Garek's hand—"

<br/>

"—my cousin says the truncheon turned into a snake—"

<br/>

Mark shoveled his first load of coal in silence. Version 1.0 of the incident had involved three seconds, two men, and one friendly-fire rivet. By breakfast, the rumor mill had apparently shipped version 4.2, which featured an eight-man brawl, open flames, and a minor act of serpent transmutation. The release notes were wildly inaccurate, but Mark had to admit the marketing team was excellent.

<br/>

Across the yard, Dobbs had positioned his wheelbarrow as far from Mark as the yard's dimensions allowed. Every time Mark glanced his way, Dobbs made a small, discreet warding sign with two fingers.

<br/>

Tam, on the other hand, sidled up beside Mark with a hopeful expression. "Can you teach me the hair thing?"

<br/>

"No," Mark said.

<br/>

"Please?"

<br/>

"It's not a feature," Mark said. "It's a bug."

<br/>

Tam went away looking deeply unconvinced.

<br/>

According to the word on the street, Garek had checked into a local apothecary late last night to get his wrist wrapped, claiming he had "tripped over a stone." Mark respected the spin. It was technically true—he *had* fallen over, and a stone had certainly been nearby—and it left out every relevant detail. Mark had read quarterly reports from VPs that did exactly the same thing. But everyone in the timber yards knew the truth: the Ironhead Syndicate's meanest enforcer had been dropped in seconds by the new fourteen-year-old coal boy.

<br/>

Smith Master Torren was waiting by the storage pit, his thick, hairy arms crossed over his leather apron. He gave Mark a long, appraising look as the boy stepped up to the wheelbarrows.

<br/>

"Garek came by my house before dawn," Torren grunted, his deep voice rattling his chest. "Told me to keep you away from his turf, said you move like a demon and use un-sanctioned stone magic." He paused. "Also said you keep a foul-tempered chicken familiar."

<br/>

Mark picked up a shovel, his face completely neutral. "That's slander. The chickens act independently."

<br/>

</p>

<br/>

Torren's eyebrows rose.

<br/>

"And I don't use magic to haul coal, Torren," Mark added. "Garek's brother was harassing an errand boy."

<br/>

Torren stared at him for three long seconds, then let out a sudden bark of laughter that shook his braided beard.

<br/>

"Harassing? Garek's a brute who needed his teeth kicked in three years ago," Torren chuckled, wiping a speck of soot from his eye. "Put the shovel down, Mark. I'm taking you off coal."

<br/>

Mark paused. "Am I fired?"

<br/>

"Fired? Hell no. You're too useful," Torren said, gesturing toward a heavy wooden door at the rear of the main forge. "I need someone who pays attention. You're working the scrap sorting and tool repair yard now. Five coppers a day, plus access to the offcut bins."

<br/>

Mark's eyes lit up. "Five coppers?"

<br/>

It was a hundred-and-fifty-percent raise after a single week on the job. In Raleigh, after a record-breaking year in which he had personally saved a flagship product launch, Mark had received a two-percent raise, a laminated certificate, and a pizza party. The pizza had been cold. Someone had ordered pineapple on all of them.

<br/>

"And if you need a place to stay that isn't a loud tavern," Torren added, tossing an iron key to Mark, who caught it neatly, "there's a stone storage shed behind the coal pit. It's dry, it's got a heavy bolt on the door, and it's yours for two coppers a week."

<br/>

Mark looked at the iron key in his hand. "Any... current residents I should know about?"

<br/>

"Just the one spider," Torren said. "Big one. She's been in there longer than I've owned the forge. Don't bother her and she won't bother you."

<br/>

"A legacy tenant," Mark said. "Understood."

<br/>

A private, secure workspace in the heart of the metalworking district, paid for by less than half a day's wages.

<br/>

"Deal," Mark said.

---

By late afternoon, Mark had settled into his new "laboratory."

<br/>

The shed was small—roughly eight by ten feet—with thick granite walls, a heavy oak door, and a dirt floor set two feet below street level. A small high window let in the pale afternoon sunlight, illuminating a sturdy workbench made of solid oak timbers. In San Francisco, Mark reflected, this exact space would rent for four thousand dollars a month and be listed as a "cozy industrial micro-loft with exposed stone and natural earth flooring."

<br/>

In the upper corner of the doorframe, a spider roughly the size of a walnut regarded him with eight unblinking eyes.

<br/>

Mark regarded her back.

<br/>

"I'm going to call you Legacy," he told her. "You were here before me, nobody knows exactly how you work, and everyone's too scared to remove you."

<br/>

Legacy did not respond, which Mark took as acceptance of the terms.

<br/>

He sat at the bench, pulled his leather notebook from his tunic, and opened it to a fresh page, writing in swift, clean English cursive:

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 03: Workspace Secured & Hardware Acquisition*
> *Current Objective: Reverse-engineer local magical artifacts to identify the physical substrate layer of spell logic. Moving beyond biological channeling to static hardware execution.*
> *Facilities Note: Shared office space with one (1) spider. Do not disturb.*

<br/>

He set the charcoal stick down and looked at the item he had salvaged from Torren's scrap bin earlier that afternoon.

<br/>

It was a discarded, broken Aether regulator—a standard Guild model, the same one used in everything from the town's streetlamps to forge blowers. The casing was cast bronze, stamped with the seal of the Oakhaven Mages' Guild, but the top nozzle was charred black from a short-circuit explosion that had cracked the glass housing.

<br/>

Mark reached into his pocket, pulled out a small iron chisel, a magnifying lens he'd borrowed from Torren, and a small needle-file, and got to work.

<br/>

The first obstacle was immediate. There were no screws. Not one. The entire casing was sealed shut with hammered rivets, as if the designer had looked at the concept of repairability and personally rejected it.

<br/>

*"User-serviceable parts inside: none,"* Mark thought sourly, working the chisel under the first rivet head. *"Some things are truly universal."*

<br/>

Twenty minutes and one skinned knuckle later, he had the casing open. He disassembled it methodically, laying the components out in a neat, organized row on his oak bench like an engineer performing an autopsy on a server motherboard:

<br/>

1. **Power Supply:** A fingernail-sized shard of mana-crystal, still holding a faint, weak blue glow, resting in a padded lead socket.
2. **Conductors:** Two thin, flat copper ribbons routing from the crystal socket toward the burner head.
3. **Control Board:** A circular, silver-plated brass disc, about the size of a silver dollar, mounted directly between the copper conductors and the ignition nozzle.
4. **Small Copper Retaining Pin:** Currently rolling off the edge of the bench.

<br/>

Mark lunged. Missed. The pin hit the dirt floor, bounced once, and vanished into the shadows beneath the workbench with the precision of a guided missile.

<br/>

He spent the next six minutes on his hands and knees, patting the dirt floor in the dark, while Legacy watched from the doorframe with what he could only interpret as quiet contempt.

<br/>

*"The First Law of Hardware,"* Mark thought, finally fishing the pin out from behind a bench leg. *"There is always one part that escapes. It is never a part you have a spare for."*

<br/>

Back at the bench, Mark picked up the silver-plated disc and held it under the magnifying lens, angling it toward the sunlight from the window.

<br/>

The surface of the disc was covered in microscopic, razor-thin grooves—intricate, geometric patterns that the locals called "runes." Near the center of the disc, a thick black scorch mark bridged three parallel lines, where the metal had melted and fused together.

<br/>

Mark stared at the microscopic grooves, his software-architect brain instantly recognizing the layout.

<br/>

*"It's not writing,"* Mark whispered, a cold surge of electricity running down his spine. *"It's not a prayer, and it's not a spell."*

<br/>

He leaned closer, tracing the lines with his needle-file.

<br/>

The grooves weren't decorative. They were **printed circuit traces**.

<br/>

The depth of the cut dictated the resistance of the line. The angle of the intersections created physical switches and logic gates. Where two lines crossed separated by a thin layer of non-conductive varnish, it formed a basic capacitor. The entire "rune" was simply a three-dimensional passive circuit board carved out of metal to route Aether without needing a human brain to hold the instructions.

<br/>

"The mages aren't performing miracles," Mark murmured to the empty shed, a wide, triumphant grin breaking across his face. "They're just burning hardcoded ROM chips."

<br/>

He shot to his feet in sheer excitement, threw both fists in the air, and cracked his head squarely against the low oak crossbeam.

<br/>

*Thonk.*

<br/>

Mark sat back down very slowly, holding his skull with both hands.

<br/>

*"Note to self,"* he thought, eyes watering. *"The ceiling is also legacy infrastructure."*

<br/>

From the doorframe, Legacy shifted one leg. Mark chose to believe it was applause.

<br/>

When the regulator had shorted out, it wasn't because of a curse or a failure of faith—a surge of Aether had jumped the gap between two un-insulated traces, melting the silver plate and creating a bridge that locked the circuit in a closed-loop feedback failure.

<br/>

"Standard short-circuit," Mark said, rubbing his head. "Fixable in five minutes."

<br/>

He took his needle-file and carefully scraped away the melted, fused silver, clearing the scorch mark until the two parallel traces were physically separated once more.

<br/>

He needed an insulator. He looked around the shed. Stone. Dirt. Oak. Spider. Then his eyes fell on his brand-new notebook.

<br/>

Mark sighed. He tore a tiny strip of dry parchment from the back page, soaked it in a drop of resin from a pine wood scrap, and wedged it between the traces as a temporary insulator.

<br/>

*"Every engineer eventually cannibalizes the documentation to fix production,"* he thought. *"I just didn't expect to do it on day one."*

<br/>

Next, he looked at the grounding pathway. The original design relied entirely on the bronze casing to absorb the feedback, which had eventually overheated the unit.

<br/>

Mark took a six-inch piece of thin copper wire from Torren's offcut bin and wrapped one end securely around the brass disc's outer ring. He didn't have a hammer, so he drove an iron nail straight through the dirt floor into the damp earth below using a fist-sized rock. It took eleven strikes and one more skinned knuckle. Mark reflected that hammering critical infrastructure into place with whatever rock happened to be nearby was a fairly accurate summary of his entire career.

<br/>

He wrapped the other end of the wire around the nail.

<br/>

*"Grounding strap installed,"* Mark noted mentally.

<br/>

He re-seated the tiny mana-crystal shard into the lead socket and pressed the small copper actuator lever on the side of the housing.

<br/>

*Click.*

<br/>

Nothing happened.

<br/>

Mark frowned. He pressed it again.

<br/>

*Click.*

<br/>

Nothing.

<br/>

He checked the traces. Clean. He checked the grounding strap. Solid. He checked the copper ribbons. Intact. Then he looked at the crystal, sitting snugly in its socket, and realized he had put it in backward.

<br/>

For a long moment, Mark sat in silence.

<br/>

Forty years of software engineering. Three market crashes. Four acquisitions. Countless production outages solved at three in the morning. And he had just opened the single oldest ticket in the history of technical support: *Is it plugged in?*

<br/>

*"Nobody,"* Mark told Legacy firmly, *"needs to know about this."*

<br/>

He flipped the crystal around, re-seated it, and pressed the lever.

<br/>

*Click.*

<br/>

There was no explosion. No violent spark. No cloud of smoke.

<br/>

A steady, silent, perfectly formed needle of cool blue flame erupted from the burner nozzle, casting a bright, flicker-free light across the stone walls of the shed. The bronze casing remained completely cool to the touch, the excess feedback draining silently down the copper wire and into the dirt beneath his feet.

<br/>

Mark sat back on his stool, watching the blue light reflect off his young hands.

<br/>

In Raleigh, he had spent forty years maintaining legacy systems designed by people who thought complexity equaled brilliance. Here, in a fantasy world of wooden carts and cloaked mages, he had just fixed his first piece of hardware using basic electrical theory, a scrap file, a rock, and one sacrificed page of his own notebook.

<br/>

He picked up his charcoal stick, opened his English ledger, and wrote a quiet declaration at the bottom of the page:

<br/>

> *Log 03 (Summary): Runes identified as passive hardware logic circuits. Disassembled, repaired, and re-grounded a Guild-standard regulator unit. System operational.*
> *Conclusion: I don't need their Academies. I just need more copper.*
> *Also: a hammer. And a helmet.*

<br/>

Mark closed the ledger, tapped the actuator lever, and watched the blue flame wink out. He sat in the peaceful dark of his workshop, rubbing the lump on his head and listening to the rain begin to patter softly against the roof outside.

<br/>

Somewhere above him, in the corner of the doorframe, Legacy began quietly repairing her web, utterly unbothered by any of it.

<br/>

Mark respected that. Legacy systems always outlast the people who complain about them.
