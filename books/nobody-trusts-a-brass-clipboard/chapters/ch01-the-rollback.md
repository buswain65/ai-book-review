## Chapter 1: The Rollback

<br/>

Mark sat up.

<br/>

Or rather, he *popped* up, springing off the mossy ground like a brand-new mattress springs out of a vacuum-sealed plastic box.

<br/>

He froze instantly, sitting back on his heels, waiting for the customary structural collapse. He waited for his right knee to crack like a dry oak branch. He waited for his lower lumbar spine to emit that sharp, burning sensation that usually reminded him he’d spent thirty years slouched in office chairs. He waited for the dull, throbbing knot in his left shoulder—a souvenir from a bad whitewater kayak flip on the Green River back in 2018—to scream in protest.

<br/>

Nothing. Zip. Zero.

<br/>

He took a deep, clear breath through his nose. There was no chronic sinus pressure. No rattle in his lungs from thirty years of breathing recirculated office air. No wheeze. The air smelled aggressively fresh—like pine resin, damp soil, and an absurd, almost offensive amount of oxygen.

<br/>

He blinked, suddenly realizing something even more startling. He reached up to pinch the bridge of his nose to adjust his heavy reading glasses—and hit bare skin.

<br/>

He looked around. The world wasn't a blurry, smeared smudge of green and grey. Every single pine needle on the high branches above him was carved in crisp, razor-sharp focus. He could count the individual grains of quartz on a rock thirty feet away.

<br/>

*"Holy hell,"* Mark whispered, touching his cheekbones. *"Twenty-twenty vision. No bifocals. No astigmatism. No smudges. My dying brain actually gave me my teenage retinas back."*

<br/>

He held his hands up to his face and stared.

<br/>

The skin was smooth, unblemished, and completely devoid of age spots. His knuckles were tiny and neat, missing the dull swelling of early-stage arthritis. The thick calluses he’d earned from forty years of typing on mechanical keyboards and twenty years of chalking up at the climbing gym had vanished, replaced by the soft skin of a teenager who had never worked a single ninety-hour crunch week in his life.

<br/>

*"Aha,"* Mark murmured aloud.

<br/>

The voice that came out of his throat wasn't his usual gravelly, 60-year-old baritone—the voice he used to crush junior developers in code reviews or sigh weary disappointment at product managers. It was crisp, light, and noticeably higher in pitch, sounding like a high school freshman whose vocal cords hadn't quite committed to manhood yet.

<br/>

*"Classic,"* Mark said, testing the voice. "Massive stroke. My brain is currently flatlining on my desk in Raleigh while my dying neurons dump a bucket of endorphins and run a cheap childhood nostalgia sequence while the ICU team calls my time of death."

<br/>

He flexed his fingers, making a tight fist, then stood up and bounced twice on his toes. He felt ridiculously, unnaturally light—maybe 110 pounds instead of his usual 185.

<br/>

*"Gotta hand it to my subconscious,"* Mark remarked to the empty forest, taking in the crisp, high-definition foliage without squinting. *"Not having to push glasses up my nose every forty seconds is a fantastic perk. I'd almost forgotten what HD vision felt like without paying an optometrist two hundred bucks."*

<br/>

He knelt down and picked up a fist-sized river rock, turning it over in his hand to inspect the texture. "Basalt. Igneous. High cooling rate. Remarkably sharp detail."

<br/>

He tossed the rock from hand to hand, marveling at the seamless depth perception. Then he walked toward the sound of roaring water.

<br/>

Through a break in the thick fern brush lay a massive river—a churning, violent Class-IV rapid smashing white foam against giant granite boulders. Mark’s whitewater kayaking instincts immediately fired up. He mapped the river in three seconds flat: pillow waves on the left, an ugly undercut rock in the center slot, and a nice, wide eddy line on the far bank that offered an easy catch.

<br/>

*"Honestly? If this is my brain’s final exit sequence, I give it a solid eight out of ten,"* Mark told a nearby fir tree. "Way better than dying with Slack open on my second monitor. At least I don't have to listen to Brad ask if we can 'synergize the roadmap' anymore."

<br/>

Right on cue, a wild beast burst through the thicket on the far side of the riverbank.

<br/>

It looked like a horrific cross between an angry wild boar and a black bear, except it stood six feet tall at the shoulder, possessed four curved, obsidian-black horns curling out of its skull, and had glowing red eyes. It slammed its heavy hooves into the mud and let out a guttural, bone-rattling roar that blew a wave of damp, hot air straight across the water into Mark's face.

<br/>

Mark didn't flinch. He didn't duck. He didn't even drop his rock.

<br/>

He simply crossed his small, teenage arms over his linen tunic and watched the monster with the bored, tired detachment of an IT director watching a user explain why they spilled coffee into their laptop for the third time this month.

<br/>

*"Oh, come on,"* Mark groaned, rolling his un-spectacled eyes. *"A four-horned boar-bear? Really, brain? That’s the best mob design my subconscious could cook up? It looks like a generic asset pulled from a twelve-dollar fantasy bundle on the Unity store."*

<br/>

The beast pawed at the dirt, glaring across the rapids, snorting black steam from its snout.

<br/>

Mark waved his small hand dismissively. "Go ahead, big guy. Charge across. Let's see what kind of clunky combat routine my dying synapses put together before the ICU team calls my time of death."
