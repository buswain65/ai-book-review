## Chapter 31: Margaret

<br/>

"No," said Lyra.

<br/>

Mark hadn't said anything yet. He was standing in front of the surface with his hand halfway raised toward the small, patient glyph that said `[REGISTER]`, and Lyra was already between him and it, with her back to the column and her arms spread wide, as if the console were a door and she could hold it shut with her body.

<br/>

"No," she said again. "You read it. You *read* it, Mark. *Correction will be scheduled.* At the top. Priority one. You'll be first. Before Vespera. Before *anyone*." Her voice cracked. "*Held. Below.* For five hundred years, nobody who went below ever came back *up*—"

<br/>

"I know."

<br/>

"Then *don't*!"

<br/>

"There has to be another way," said Julian. He was standing at the mouth of the alcove, half turned toward the stair, where the silver curtain was thinning, slowly, steadily, from the point where the Auditor's rod touched it. "There's always another way. You said so. You said you just hadn't found it."

<br/>

"I haven't," said Mark. "And I've run out of time to look."

<br/>

He pointed past them, across the cavern, at the curtain. It had faded to the thinness of frost on a window now, and through it, the grey figure stood perfectly still with its rod raised, and behind the grey figure, forty silver ones waited on the stair.

<br/>

"When that comes down," Mark said, "the Auditor reaches this console in about a minute. And it reconciles. *Everything.* Every name on that list, all at once, and whatever happens to me happens anyway, registered or not." He looked at Lyra. "If I register, I get write access. If I get write access, I can change the list. I can change the *rules*. Maybe. If I'm fast. If I'm right." He paused. "If I don't, I get corrected anyway, and so does Vespera, and so does Tessa, and so does everyone else, and nothing changes, and in five hundred years another kid wakes up by a river and nobody's fixed a single line."

<br/>

Lyra stared at him. Her arms were still spread wide.

<br/>

"I've been unregistered since the river," Mark said quietly. "Every time the system ran into me, it didn't know what to do. So it skipped me. The Auditor's book. The rings. The filter. The construct. Mott. I've been an unhandled exception for months, Lyra. A null in a field where something was supposed to be." He took a breath. "I spent forty years telling junior developers the same thing. You can't fix a system you refuse to be part of. Sooner or later, you have to file."

<br/>

"That's a terrible reason," said Lyra. Her eyes were wet.

<br/>

"It's the only one I've got."

<br/>

Lyra stood there for a long moment, with her arms out.

<br/>

Then, slowly, she lowered them.

<br/>

She didn't step aside. She stepped *beside* him, close, so that her shoulder pressed against his arm, and she reached into the twelfth pocket of her apron and closed her fingers around something inside it, tight, and kept them there.

<br/>

"Then you'd better be fast," she said. "And you'd better be *right*."

<br/>

Mark pressed `[REGISTER]`.

---

The chime came first.

<br/>

It came up out of the column, and the plate, and the stone, and every crystal in the forest, exactly as it had when he'd opened the session: a single, pure, crystalline note at fourteen thousand cycles that went through his skull like a struck bell. But this time it didn't fade. It held. It hung in the cavern like a struck tuning fork that wouldn't stop, and Mark felt it settle into his bones, into his teeth, into the crystals in the cuff on his wrist.

<br/>

The surface rewrote itself.

<br/>

His record came up, the way it had before: the header with the three-glyph hole where his name should be, *unregistered entity*, *origin: external*, *river, north of Oakhaven*. And then, line by line, as he watched, it *changed*.

<br/>

`[REGISTRATION: COMPLETE]`

<br/>

`[OPERATOR: VANCE, MARK]`

<br/>

He stared at that one. He hadn't told it his name. He'd never told *anything* in this world his name except people.

<br/>

`[CLASS:                  ]`

<br/>

`[PRIVILEGES: FULL]`

<br/>

`[STATUS: CORRECTION SCHEDULED — PRIORITY 1]`

<br/>

And at the very top of the reconciliation list, where the hard white *session holder* line had been, a new line wrote itself, and replaced it, and the shrinking number beside it was gone. In its place was a single word, glowing steadily, waiting.

<br/>

`[NEXT]`

<br/>

"*Vance, Mark*," Julian read softly, over his shoulder. "It knows your name."

<br/>

"It reads the ledger, I think," said Mark. His voice sounded strange to him. "Or it reads *me*. I'm not sure there's a difference anymore."

<br/>

He touched the menu. He opened `[MEASURE]`, and found the long list of settings he'd only half-understood before, and touched one, very carefully, the same one he'd touched last time.

<br/>

The glyph lit. The value beside it unlocked, and waited for him, the way a field in a form waits for a cursor.

<br/>

No polite grey line. No *insufficient privilege*.

<br/>

He had root.

<br/>

"Mark," said Julian, in a very different voice. "*Mark*. The stair."

<br/>

Mark turned.

<br/>

At the foot of the stair, on the far side of the cavern, forty constructs had lifted their heads.

<br/>

All of them. All at once. On every step, in a long silver line from the ledge to the curtain, forty blank oval faces had turned away from the Auditor and away from Vespera and away from Torren and toward the alcove. Toward the console. Toward *him*.

<br/>

He knew what he was to them now. He'd read it on Gerald's core. *TARGET CLASS*, and three glyphs. He'd been a null in their field for months, a shape they half-recognized and couldn't quite resolve. Now the Registry had his name and his class and his record, and every construct connected to it knew exactly what was standing at the base of the Anchor.

<br/>

*Registered,* Mark thought. *I'm not an exception anymore. I'm a beacon.*

<br/>

And the curtain failed.

---

It didn't break. It didn't shatter. The last thin film of silver light at the point of the Auditor's rod simply *wasn't there* anymore, and the whole shimmering sheet of it went out all at once, like a lamp blown out, and there was nothing between the stair and the cavern floor but the cold air.

<br/>

The constructs came down.

<br/>

They didn't walk, the way the Auditor walked. They *moved*: fast and smooth and silent, long silver limbs flowing down the switchbacks of the stair in a pouring line, frost flying off their shoulders, their blank faces fixed on the alcove. The first three reached the bottom step in the time it took Mark to draw a breath.

<br/>

Torren hit the first one.

<br/>

He hit it the way he'd hit Duchess in the Syndicate warehouse: at full speed, shoulder-first, with his whole quarter-dwarf weight behind it and a roar that boomed off the cavern ceiling. The construct went down. Torren went down with it, and rolled, and came up swearing, and grabbed the second one by the leg.

<br/>

Vespera was chanting.

<br/>

She'd been chanting since the moment the curtain started thinning, the full forty-line incantation, her wrapped and blistered hands locked around the ironwood staff, and now she swept it forward on the last syllable and a wall of force tore across the foot of the stair and threw the third and fourth constructs back up the steps into the fifth and sixth, a tangle of silver limbs crashing against the rock.

<br/>

She staggered. The staff smoked in her grip. She didn't let go of it. She planted her feet and started again from the first line.

<br/>

And behind them, in the middle of the stair, the Auditor started down.

<br/>

It walked. Exactly as it always walked. One step. Pause. Next step. Its grey robe hung straight. Its book was open in one hand, its rod in the other. It didn't look at Vespera, or Torren, or the constructs crashing down the stair around it and past it. It came down the steps at its unhurried, unbreakable pace, and its blank grey face was turned, steadily, toward the alcove, and the console, and the word glowing at the top of the reconciliation list.

<br/>

`[NEXT]`

---

"*How long?*" Lyra said.

<br/>

"I don't know." Mark was already turning back to the surface, his hands moving over the glyphs. "I don't even know what I'm going to *write* yet."

<br/>

He opened `[MEASURE]` again, all the way. The settings spread out across the dark surface in long columns: thresholds, ratios, tolerances, numbers he could half-guess the meaning of. He scrolled. Down. Down. And at the very bottom of the list, set apart from the others, in a heavier stroke, he found it.

<br/>

`[DISABLE MEASURE]`

<br/>

He stopped.

<br/>

It was just a glyph. Small and plain, sitting there quietly at the bottom of five hundred years of settings, like the big red switch at the back of a server room that everyone knows is there and nobody ever touches.

<br/>

*Tear it down*, said a voice in the back of his head. It sounded very like his own voice at twenty-five, before he knew anything. *It's cruel. It's broken. It's been throttling the whole world for five hundred years. One touch, and every caster in the Empire is free. No more pinch. No more blisters. No more correction. No more* held.

<br/>

He thought about Varrow, and Tessaly, and the Nine Towns of the Lower March, and the city of Aun. He thought about Julian's paintings: rivers stopped in the middle of the current, birds frozen on the rooftops. He thought about the shed, and the frost blooming over his ledger in a single heartbeat, and Lyra by the door with her hands over her ears and her breath steaming.

<br/>

He thought about a world full of people casting clean, all at once, all ringing, like a city full of bells that never stopped. And something on the far side, listening.

<br/>

`[EXTERNAL ATTENTION: LOW]`

<br/>

*We were the ones who rang the bells. We did not know.*

<br/>

He looked at the glyph for a long moment.

<br/>

Then he took his hand away from it, very deliberately, and scrolled back up.

<br/>

"You don't rip out a load-bearing wall," he muttered, "because you don't like the color."

<br/>

"*What?*" said Lyra.

<br/>

"Nothing." His hands were moving again. "I'm not tearing it down. And I'm not keeping it the way it is. There's a third option. There's *always* a third option." He stared at the columns of settings. "I just have to find it before *that* finds me."

<br/>

Behind him, across the cavern, the Auditor reached the bottom of the stair, stepped off the last step onto the cavern floor, and began to walk toward the alcove.

<br/>

And from somewhere far above, up the stair and through the passage and across the rack room and up and up through the frozen corridors of the deep levels, faint but unmistakable, came a voice. Then many voices. Shouting one word, over and over, closer with every repetition, the way the word had gone out across the sleeping city above at the speed of small bare feet.

<br/>

"*Margaret!*"

<br/>

"*MARGARET!*"

<br/>

"*MAAAARGAAARET!*"

---

They came over the ledge like water over a weir.

<br/>

Mark saw them from the alcove, in flashes, between constructs: a ragged, shouting, torch-waving flood of people pouring out of the mouth of the dark passage onto the ledge high above the crystal forest, and stopping dead at the sight of it, and then, with a roar, pouring down the stair.

<br/>

Enforcers. Dockhands. Goat handlers. The tannery worker with the bad elbow, holding his torch in the arm that hadn't worked a month ago. The door guard from the Syndicate warehouse, turning his head to the left and to the right, left and right, wide-eyed, taking in all of it at once because he *could*. Two of the river carters. A baker with flour still on her arms. The woman from the goat line who had been offered a silver piece to forget she'd been asked about the boy who fixes blowers, and hadn't.

<br/>

The Infirmary line. Every one of them. Every sore back and frozen shoulder and bad knee Vespera had warmed in the yard of the Fish & Hammer, every man and woman who'd stood in the rain outside a door that said *CLOSED FOR AUDIT* because they didn't know where else to stand.

<br/>

And at the front of them, carrying a chair, was Brann.

<br/>

It was a heavy oak chair, the kind they had in the back room of the Syndicate warehouse, and he was carrying it in both hands over his head as he came down the switchbacks two steps at a time, and his folded ear was red with cold, and his face was set in an expression of absolute professional calm.

<br/>

The first construct he reached was halfway up the stair, grappling with Torren.

<br/>

Brann hit it with the chair.

<br/>

It was, Mark thought afterward, a technically perfect chair strike. Full extension, rotation through the hips, the weight of the chair carried through the follow-through. Thirty years of experience in every inch of it. The chair broke. The construct went sideways off the stair, cartwheeled once, and landed in a heap of silver limbs at the base of a crystal.

<br/>

Brann looked down at it. Then he looked at the broken chair legs in his hands.

<br/>

"Sorry," he said to the construct, sincerely. "Nothing personal."

<br/>

Then he dropped the chair legs, picked up the next nearest heavy object, which was one of Torren's spare bellows from his belt, and went looking for another one.

---

After that, Mark lost track.

<br/>

He had to. He turned his back on it, because the console was in front of him and his hands were on the glyphs and every second he spent watching the stair was a second he wasn't writing. But he heard it. The whole cavern rang with it.

<br/>

He heard the dockhands shouting to each other in the clipped, practical calls of men who'd spent their lives loading cargo in bad weather: *left side, left side, mind your back, lift on three*. He heard Torren roaring, and the crash of silver limbs on stone. He heard Vespera chanting, and the *whump* of her force-walls, and the hiss of the old staff in her burned hands. He heard a deep, rolling incantation he recognized, and turned his head just in time to see a broad, steady figure with flour on his sleeves drive the butt of an ironwood staff into the cavern floor.

<br/>

Hollis Grey.

<br/>

The ground rolled.

<br/>

It rolled in a long, smooth wave, the way it had rolled under Mark's feet in the arena, out from the butt of Hollis's staff across the floor of the crystal forest toward the foot of the stair, and it lifted the stone under three charging constructs and dropped it away again, and all three of them went down in a sprawling tangle.

<br/>

*"They root,"* Mark thought, with a flash of fierce delight. *"They're heavy and they root, like I did. And he knows exactly how to take a root away."*

<br/>

Beside Hollis, small and sharp and straw-haired, stood Tessa Fenn.

<br/>

She was holding her chipped wooden rod. She didn't chant for two minutes. She didn't scream. She flicked the rod and spoke three syllables, and a short, precise bolt of force snapped the leg out from under a construct just as it rose from Hollis's wave. Pause. Three syllables. Another. Pause. Another. Short and tight and economical, not a scrap of force wasted, each one placed exactly where a silver foot was about to land.

<br/>

She was, Mark realized, the second most efficient caster he'd ever seen.

<br/>

The first walked down the stair behind her.

---

Corwin came down vertically.

<br/>

He came down the switchbacks at a steady, unhurried walk, with his hands in the pockets of his Academy robe, as if he were strolling through the courtyard on the way to his favorite bench. Constructs flowed past him on either side. He didn't seem to notice. When one of them swerved toward him, he took one hand out of his pocket, flicked a single finger at the stone beneath its leading foot, murmured one quiet syllable, and put the hand back in his pocket as it went over sideways.

<br/>

He didn't break stride.

<br/>

He reached the bottom of the stair and stood on the cavern floor, looking around at the crystal forest, and the fight, and the column, with an expression of mild, faintly weary interest.

<br/>

"I've been vertical," he called across the cavern to Mark, "since the third landing."

<br/>

"*I know!*" Mark shouted back, without turning around.

<br/>

"That's *forty minutes*, Mark." Corwin flicked another finger, absently, and another construct went down. "Don't let it be for nothing."

<br/>

"*I'm working on it!*"

<br/>

"Mm," said Corwin. Then he looked down at the cavern floor at his feet, sighed, and very carefully did *not* lie down on it.

---

And then, through all of it, Garek came down the stair.

<br/>

He came last. He came slowly, because his knee was bad and the stair was steep and he was carrying something heavy, and he came down the switchbacks with the dogged, careful patience of a man who'd done his leg lifts three times a day for a month and was not going to waste them now. Constructs went past him. He didn't fight them. He just kept walking, down and down, with his arms wrapped around the thing he was carrying.

<br/>

It was the iron box. The one from the top shelf of the Fish & Hammer. With *DO NOT CHARGE* painted on the lid.

<br/>

He reached the bottom of the stair. He crossed the cavern floor, past Hollis and Tessa and Corwin, past the brawling dockhands, around the base of a crystal, and walked straight to the mouth of the alcove, and stopped.

<br/>

"You said," Garek told Mark, slightly out of breath, "protect what matters."

<br/>

He set the iron box down very carefully on the stone floor, inside the alcove, at Mark's feet.

<br/>

Then he turned around, and looked at the arch of the alcove: a single, narrow, man-height opening in the black obsidian of the column, the only way in or out of the small space where Mark stood at the console.

<br/>

A slow, deep satisfaction spread across Garek's broad, battered face.

<br/>

He stepped into it.

<br/>

He planted his feet wide. He folded his arms across his chest. He settled his shoulders into the stone of the arch until they touched both sides of the frame, the way they'd touched both sides of the Fish & Hammer door for fourteen days, and he looked out at the cavern full of crystals and constructs and chaos with the calm, flat, contented gaze of a man returning, at last, to his natural habitat.

<br/>

"I'm good in a doorway," said Garek.

<br/>

The next construct that reached the alcove found him there.

<br/>

It didn't get past.

---

"Mark," said Lyra urgently, at his shoulder. "Mark, *look*."

<br/>

He looked.

<br/>

Out across the cavern floor, through the chaos, the Auditor was walking toward the alcove.

<br/>

It was perhaps a hundred feet away. It walked straight, at its exact, unbreakable pace, through the middle of the fighting. Brawling dockhands parted around it without thinking. Constructs swerved past it. It didn't look at any of them. It came on, one step, pause, next step, its book open, its rod raised, its blank face fixed on the alcove.

<br/>

And then a carter stepped into its path.

<br/>

He was a short, broad man with a grey beard, one of the patients from the Infirmary line. Mark remembered him dimly: bad wrists, from forty years of reins. He stepped right in front of the Auditor, so that it had to stop or walk into him. And he held something up in front of its blank grey face, at arm's length, very formally, the way you'd hold up a document for a clerk.

<br/>

It was a brass clipboard.

<br/>

The Auditor stopped.

<br/>

It looked at the clipboard. It raised its silver rod, and laid the tip of it on the brass. It opened its grey book with its other hand, and looked at a page, and looked at the clipboard, and looked at the page.

<br/>

It inspected the clipboard.

<br/>

Then it wrote something in its book. `[COMPLIANT.]` It handed the clipboard back to the carter, who took it with a small, grave nod, and stepped aside.

<br/>

The Auditor took one step.

<br/>

And a goat handler stepped into its path, holding up a grip trainer.

<br/>

Leather and ash. Lyra's work. The Auditor stopped. It raised its rod. It opened its book. Page. Trainer. Page.

<br/>

`[COMPLIANT.]`

<br/>

One step.

<br/>

And the baker with the flour on her arms stepped in front of it, holding a brass clipboard of her own.

<br/>

Mark stared.

<br/>

All across the cavern floor, between the Auditor and the alcove, people were forming a *line*. Not a wall. A *queue*. Patients from the Infirmary, standing patiently one behind another in the middle of the fighting, the way they'd stood in the yard of the Fish & Hammer, each one holding something up to be inspected: clipboards, grip trainers, intake forms, a copper bracelet, a small framed saucer that Mark recognized, after a moment, from Nan Brask's parlor.

<br/>

And at the head of the line, coming down the stair with an enormous canvas sack slung over his shoulder, from which the brass corners of at least forty clipboards were sticking out in every direction, was a small, fierce, red-faced figure.

<br/>

Pip.

<br/>

"*You said*," Pip shouted across the cavern at Mark, flinging handfuls of clipboards at anyone near the stair who didn't already have one, "*too many messages at once* breaks a thing!" He threw another armful. "*You said* it was a very old problem! *You said* usually it's enemies, but sometimes it's just a lot of people being *helpful*!"

<br/>

"Pip, what are you *doing* down here, you're a *child*—"

<br/>

"*Bus Master!*" Pip shouted, scandalized. "*Bus Master*, and I'm *nearly thirteen*, and *Hollis* is *watching* me!" Hollis, mid-wave, raised a hand in confirmation without looking around. "And I'm *doing* what *you* did to *us*!" He threw a clipboard at a goat handler, who caught it neatly and stepped into the queue. "I'm *DDoSing* it!"

<br/>

Mark stood at the console with his mouth open.

<br/>

The Auditor was trapped.

<br/>

Not by force. Not by a wall. By its own procedure. It walked in order, and it audited in order, and it never, ever skipped, and between it and the alcove there was now a patient, orderly line of forty people, each one holding up a perfectly compliant object for its inspection. Every one of them had to be checked. Every one of them took time.

<br/>

Rod. Book. Clipboard. Book. `[COMPLIANT.]` One step.

<br/>

Rod. Book. Grip trainer. Book. `[COMPLIANT.]` One step.

<br/>

"*It's following the spec*," Lyra breathed. "It *has* to. It can't skip them."

<br/>

"It's an auditor," said Mark, and his voice cracked with something that was very nearly laughter, very nearly tears. "It's never skipped anything in its life." He turned back to the console, his hands flying over the glyphs. "Pip just bought me about four minutes."

<br/>

"Is that *enough*?"

<br/>

"Nothing's ever enough," said Mark. "Write that down, it's on the list."

<br/>

"It's *already* on the list," said Lyra. "Number twelve."

---

Lord Aldric Thorne came down the stair four minutes later, exactly as he had promised his wife: to stand behind the boy when the door opened.

<br/>

Mark knew the moment he arrived, without looking up from the console. The fighting changed. It didn't stop, but it *paused*, the way a room full of people pauses when a door opens and someone important walks in. The shouting faltered. Even the constructs seemed to hesitate.

<br/>

He looked up.

<br/>

Aldric stood on the ledge at the top of the stair, in his plain dark coat, with his silver hair perfectly combed, looking down over the crystal forest. On his right hand, the stone in his ring glowed a clean, pale silver-white. Behind him stood six men in dark coats, each with a silver ring on the third finger of his right hand, each stone gold.

<br/>

And beside him, half a pace back, in a plain dark cloak, stood High Inspector Valerius.

<br/>

Lord Thorne looked down at all of it for a long, unhurried moment: the crystals, the column, the brawling dockhands, the queue of patients holding up clipboards in front of the Auditor, his own son standing in the mouth of the alcove beside a scrap boy from the coal pits. If any of it surprised him, his face didn't show it.

<br/>

Then he started down.

<br/>

Corwin, standing near the foot of the stair with his hands in his pockets, watched him come and said, to no one in particular, and in a tone of faint regret, "I got three minutes."

<br/>

Mark glanced at him.

<br/>

"At the third landing," Corwin explained. "He came down with his six. I stood up. I did the minimum." He flicked a finger at a construct without looking at it. "To all seven of them. I made them trip on the same step, one after another, for three minutes." He sighed. "Then he looked at me, and said, 'You must be Corwin. My son speaks of you,' and I was so surprised that I forgot to do anything else."

<br/>

"Three minutes is a lot," said Mark.

<br/>

"It's very tiring," said Corwin. "I'm going to sleep for a month."

---

Aldric reached the bottom of the stair. He crossed the cavern floor with his six men behind him and Valerius at his side, and the fighting parted for him the way it parted for the Auditor. He didn't look at the constructs. He didn't look at the crystals. He walked straight to the mouth of the alcove, where Garek stood in the arch with his arms folded, and stopped in front of him.

<br/>

Garek looked down at Lord Aldric Thorne, Keeper of the Measure, master of a house that made toll keepers bow to empty carriages.

<br/>

He didn't move.

<br/>

"Mr. Garek," said Aldric courteously. "How is your knee?"

<br/>

Garek considered him.

<br/>

"*Doing the lifts*," he said.

<br/>

"I'm glad to hear it." Aldric looked past him, into the alcove, at Mark standing in front of the glowing surface, at the console, at the word glowing at the top of the reconciliation list. *NEXT*. "Mr. Vance. You registered."

<br/>

"I registered," said Mark.

<br/>

"I thought you might." Aldric looked at the surface for a long moment, and something passed across his face that Mark had never seen there before, not in the study, not at dinner: something hungry, almost reverent. "Five hundred years," he said softly. "Twenty generations. I stood in the vault above us with my father when I was sixteen, and I put my hand on the stone, and I begged it to answer." He looked at Mark. "And it answers *you*."

<br/>

"It answers whoever has both factors," said Mark. "That's how it was designed. You only ever had one."

<br/>

"So I learned," said Aldric. "From you, I think. I never knew." He paused. "Do you know what you've done? You've given it to us. *Write access.*" He said the words carefully, like a man learning a foreign phrase. "You can change anything now. Anything at all. And so I am going to ask you, Mr. Vance, one last time, very reasonably, to change one thing."

<br/>

He folded his hands behind his back.

<br/>

"Make House Thorne its operator. Both factors, in one House. Let us keep the Measure properly, for once, instead of sweeping its stairs. I will keep the world quiet. Competently. Carefully. Forever. You've seen the margin; you know how close it is. It needs someone who can actually *touch* it." He tilted his head. "And you, Mr. Vance, will be held. Comfortably. Somewhere safe. I give you my word. You will want for nothing. You will have books, and materials, and work, if you want it. You'll never be cold, or hungry, or frightened again."

<br/>

"Held," said Mark. "Below."

<br/>

"*Safe*," said Aldric. "You read the record. They searched for a better way for five hundred years. They found nothing. Neither will you. This *is* the better way. The *only* better way." His voice was very gentle. "Let me carry it. You've carried enough."

---

Julian stepped out of the alcove.

<br/>

He walked past Garek, under the arch, and out onto the cavern floor, and stood in front of his father.

<br/>

He wore Torren's enormous borrowed shirt. His hands were wrapped in clean white linen to the wrists, and there were fresh dark stains seeping through across the palms, from the walk and the stair and the fight. There was a pale band of skin on the third finger of his right hand, where a ring had been.

<br/>

"No, Father," he said.

<br/>

Aldric looked at him.

<br/>

"Julian," he said. Mildly. Patiently. "You're tired. You've been through a great deal. Stand aside."

<br/>

"No."

<br/>

"I let you go," said Aldric. "You understand that. I left the fields unwatched. I let you take the key. I let you walk forty miles in the rain. I *knew*, Julian. I wanted this." He gestured at the console. "You've done everything I needed you to do. Exactly as I hoped you would. Now stand *aside*."

<br/>

Julian's face went very white.

<br/>

<br/>

Every value depended on every other value. Change the return channel width, and the depth of the main trace had to change with it, and the crystal seat, and the clasp. Change one ratio, and the whole layout shifted, and the four checkpoints the Auditor used, the four places it measured, *had* to land on the right numbers, or the whole new template would fail its own inspection. It was a geometry, interlocked and exact, and he was trying to describe it from memory, number by number, while the frost crept down the crystals and the margin hovered and Torren puffed in circles through the forest with the Auditor ten paces behind him.

<br/>

"I can't," Mark said. His voice cracked. "Lyra, I can't *write* it. Not fast enough. Not *right*. I need a reference. I need a working design. Something real it can *read*. Something that's already grounded and already clean and already passes the four—"

<br/>

He stopped.

<br/>

Beside him, Lyra had taken her hand out of the twelfth pocket of her apron.

<br/>

She was holding a bracelet.

<br/>

It was slim and silver and quiet. Mark had never seen it before. It was cut in long, sweeping, beautiful First Era strokes, every channel open, every return line running not into a pinch but down into a fine silver tail at the clasp, a ground lead, so that whatever heat it gathered could go nowhere but into the earth. And at the four places, *exactly* at the four places where the Auditor's rod had rested on Tilly, at the clasp and the return and the crystal seat and the end of the main trace, the long sweeping strokes narrowed and turned and settled, for the width of a fingernail, into the exact, precise, perfect geometry of the Guild template.

<br/>

And over the main trace, almost invisible, slid a tiny shield of lead foil on a silver pin, like the switch on Mark's own cuff.

<br/>

"I solved the mute," said Lyra.

<br/>

Her voice was very steady.

<br/>

"I solved it on the step. After the rack room. It's a slider. Lead over the main trace. When it's closed, it can't pick up a thing. When it's open…" She slid the tiny pin with her thumb, and the bracelet hummed, very softly, clean and quiet, and the hum went down through the silver tail at the clasp and into the stone. "It's grounded. It's clean. It doesn't hurt. And at the four places the Auditor checks, it's *exactly* to spec. To the hair. I measured Tilly a thousand times." She swallowed. "It passes. I *know* it passes."

<br/>

"*L-2*," said Mark.

<br/>

"L-2," said Lyra.

<br/>

She looked at the silver plate inlaid beside the keyhole. The plate that had read Mark's clean cast, and opened the session, and drunk the current through its long open traces like water into a carving.

<br/>

"You need a reference," she said. "Something real. Something it can read."

<br/>

She stepped up to the console, and pressed L-2 flat against the silver plate.

<br/>

"*Use mine.*"

---

The surface rippled.

<br/>

The specification, the long, enormous, interlocking column of glyph-numbers describing the Sacred Restriction, flickered, and then began, line by line, to *change*.

<br/>

Mark watched it happen. He watched the console read Lyra's bracelet the way the Auditor had read Tilly: depth by depth, angle by angle, ratio by ratio, every long sweeping First Era stroke, every open channel, every grounded return, the fine silver tail at the clasp, the four places where it narrowed to the old geometry, the tiny shield of lead on its pin. He watched each number in the old specification dissolve and rewrite itself as the number in her bracelet. Line after line. Faster and faster. Down the whole length of the surface.

<br/>

At the top of the block, a new line appeared.

<br/>

`[REFERENCE INSTRUMENT: SUBMITTED]`

<br/>

And then, below it:

<br/>

`[PROPOSED SPECIFICATION — STANDARD FOCUS]`
`[SOURCE: SUBMITTED REFERENCE]`

<br/>

"It's reading it," Mark breathed. "It's *reading* it. All of it. Every line."

<br/>

Lyra didn't say anything. She stood with her hand pressed flat over the bracelet on the plate, and her eyes were very bright, and her lips were pressed tightly together, and Mark thought he had never in his life seen anyone look so frightened and so proud at the same time.

<br/>

"Three weeks of evenings," she said quietly. "For Tilly. And two nights on a step." She swallowed. "It's going to be the template for *everyone*, isn't it. Every staff. Every bracelet. Every lamp in the Empire."

<br/>

"Every one," said Mark.

<br/>

"*Mine*."

<br/>

"*Yours*."

<br/>

Lyra closed her eyes.

---

There was more. There was so much more, and almost no time.

<br/>

Mark wrote the rest of it as fast as he had ever written anything in his life, the way he'd once written emergency patches on red-eye deployments at three in the morning, narrating under his breath, his hands flying over the glyphs.

<br/>

"*Change one*," he muttered. "Specification. Standard focus. Replace with submitted reference. Done. *Keep the throttle.*" He checked the throttle values: untouched, unchanged, five hundred years of quiet. "Throttle preserved. Don't touch the wall. Don't touch the margin."

<br/>

He found `[RECONCILIATION]`.

<br/>

"*Change two.* Correction protocol, native." He found the line. `[PROTOCOL: RESTORATION TO SPECIFICATION]`. He touched it. "Restoration means *restore to spec*. And the spec is *changing*." He wrote it. `[PROTOCOL: RE-SPECIFICATION TO CURRENT STANDARD]`. "So anyone it corrects gets corrected to the *new* spec. To *grounded*. Not pinched. Nobody loses anything. Nobody stops being able to cast. Vespera gets *L-2*."

<br/>

Out in the cavern, Vespera, standing back to back with Valerius, with Lady Seraphine still kneeling at her feet holding her hands, looked up sharply across the crystal forest as if she'd heard her name.

<br/>

"*Change three.*"

<br/>

He opened `[ARRIVALS]`.

<br/>

The list came up. A few dozen lines, five hundred years. *Held. Held. Held. Held.* Sector 11, Year 499: *held.* And at the bottom, glowing hard and white, his own: `CORRECTION SCHEDULED — PRIORITY 1`.

<br/>

He found the protocol line. `[ARRIVALS: HOLD UNTIL A BETTER WAY IS FOUND]`.

<br/>

He looked at it for one second.

<br/>

*Let whoever reads this find a better way, and open it.*

<br/>

"The whole reason they held them," he said, very quietly, "was so they wouldn't *ring*. Arrivals cast clean, and clean casting is loud." He touched the line. "But under the new spec, clean casting *isn't* loud. It's grounded. Every bit of the energy that would have rung goes down into the dam instead. Quiet. Painless." He swallowed. "There's nothing to hold them *for*, anymore."

<br/>

He wrote it.

<br/>

`[ARRIVALS: RELEASE UPON COMPLIANCE WITH CURRENT STANDARD]`

<br/>

The line glowed for a moment. Then, beneath it, the long column of *held* flickered, every line at once, and Mark couldn't tell yet what it meant, and didn't have time to find out.

<br/>

"Julian," he said. "I need a description. For the change. The console wants one. I can't write the script."

<br/>

Julian was at his shoulder. He'd been there, Mark realized, the whole time. He looked at the empty field on the surface, a small blank box under the long list of changes, waiting.

<br/>

`[DESCRIBE CHANGE:]`

<br/>

"What do I write?" Julian asked.

<br/>

Mark looked at the margin of the surface, where, a long time ago, in Keeper shorthand, in a small, cramped, faded hand, somebody had written the same word three times, and then nothing.

<br/>

*Why? Why? Why?*

<br/>

"Write it in the margin," said Mark. "Under theirs. In shorthand. So the next Keeper who comes down here can read it." He thought about it for only a moment. "Write: *A better way. Opened.*"

<br/>

Julian picked up a sliver of charcoal from Mark's satchel, and, very carefully, with his bandaged fingers, wrote it on the surface in small, neat Keeper shorthand, in the margin, directly under the three old *whys*.

<br/>

The console took it.

<br/>

At the bottom of the surface, a single glyph lit, and glowed, and waited.

<br/>

`[COMMIT]`

---

That was when the core ran out.

<br/>

Mark heard it stop. Out in the crystal forest, the great ringing note of Gerald's heart faltered, wavered, and died, as the charge he'd passed into it bled away. And in the sudden silence, he heard Torren, very far off among the crystals, gasping, "*Sorry, Gerald*," and then the heavy sound of a large man sitting down very suddenly on cold stone.

<br/>

The Auditor stopped walking.

<br/>

Mark watched it turn. Slowly. Away from Torren, slumped against the base of Grandmother with the dead grey core in his lap. Away from the crystal forest. Its blank grey face swung round, across the cavern, through the frost and the stilled constructs and the frozen crowd, until it found the alcove, and the console, and the word glowing at the top of the reconciliation list.

<br/>

`[NEXT]`

<br/>

It drew its rod across its book one final time, and the last of Legacy's web came away, and Legacy dropped from the page to the stone and scuttled away into the dark.

<br/>

It started walking.

<br/>

Thirty feet. Twenty.

<br/>

Garek was in the doorway.

<br/>

He'd been in the doorway since he'd set down the iron box. He'd held it against three constructs, and Aldric's courtesy, and the cold. He stood there now with his feet wide and his arms folded and his shoulders touching both sides of the obsidian arch, and watched the Auditor come.

<br/>

Ten feet.

<br/>

Garek set his jaw.

<br/>

And his knee went.

<br/>

It went the way old knees go, without warning, at the worst possible moment, after a month of leg lifts and five hours in a doorway and a fight on a frozen stair. Mark heard the small, sick sound it made. He saw Garek's face change, saw the big man's weight drop, saw him grab for the edge of the arch with one hand and miss, and go down on one side, hard, onto the stone floor, clutching his leg.

<br/>

The doorway was empty.

<br/>

The Auditor stepped through it.

---

It stopped in front of Mark.

<br/>

It raised its silver rod, and held it an inch from his face, exactly as it had in the workshop, and Mark could see the fine, precise grid of lines etched across the slate-grey oval of its face, and hear the high, thin crystalline hum.

<br/>

It opened its book.

<br/>

And this time, Mark knew, the page wouldn't be blank. This time there was a record. *Vance, Mark.* Class: three glyphs. Status: *correction scheduled.* Priority one.

<br/>

*Next.*

<br/>

"*Mark*."

<br/>

It was Aldric's voice. From somewhere behind the Auditor, out on the cavern floor. Quiet and strained and very close to breaking.

<br/>

"Mark. *Don't.*"

<br/>

Mark's finger hung over the glowing glyph.

<br/>

He thought about Lyra's hand on the silver plate. About Vespera's burned palms. About Tessa's chipped wooden rod, and Valerius's rolled-up sleeve, and Ignis sobbing *sacred* on the ledge. About a girl in a too-big smock, very small in a torchlit courtyard. About thirty-one lines that all said *held*.

<br/>

About a desk in Raleigh, and a backspace key, and a man named Brad asking if it was a simple plug-and-play drop-in.

<br/>

He'd spent his whole life refusing to ship code he hadn't tested. Refusing to push to production at eight a.m. on a Friday on the say-so of a man in a fleece vest. Refusing to sign at the table.

<br/>

*Never sign at the table*, Valerius had said, through Vespera, in the warm candlelight of *The Silver Ladle*. *Whatever they offer, it will sound reasonable. Don't give your answer the same day.*

<br/>

He looked at the Auditor's rod, an inch from his face.

<br/>

He looked at the glyph.

<br/>

"Never sign at the table," Mark said quietly.

<br/>

He looked at Lyra beside him, with her hand still flat on the plate. At Julian, with charcoal on his bandaged fingers. At Garek on the floor of the doorway, gritting his teeth, looking up at him.

<br/>

"But this one," said Mark, "is *my* table."

<br/>

He pressed `[COMMIT]`.

<br/>

Everything in the cavern went silent at once.
