## Chapter 26: Executive Offsite

<br/>

The carriage had no driver, no horses, no visible means of propulsion, and, Mark discovered within the first hour, no seams.

<br/>

He had started with the floor. He'd rolled back the grey velvet mat and found smooth, black-lacquered wood underneath, joined so perfectly that he couldn't get a fingernail into it. He had rapped on every wall panel in turn and listened to the echoes like a man checking a server rack for loose screws. He had pressed his ear to the floor directly above the rear axle, where any sane engineer would have put a drive mechanism, and heard nothing but the faint, crystalline hum that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere at once, like the sound of a building's ventilation at three in the morning.

<br/>

He sat back up and looked at the crystal carafe on the seat opposite.

<br/>

He was very thirsty.

<br/>

He was also, he reflected, sitting inside a vehicle owned by a family that had locked him in an archive with a seven-foot murder automaton, and had sent the vehicle without being asked.

<br/>

*"In my old life,"* he thought, *"I would not have accepted a USB stick from a stranger in a parking lot."*

<br/>

He looked at the carafe for another ten minutes. Then he poured a glass, sniffed it, held it up to the window, and drank it.

<br/>

It was water. Very good water. Cold, clear, faintly mineral, the kind of water that costs nine dollars a bottle and comes from a glacier with a marketing department.

<br/>

He waited to die. He didn't.

<br/>

*"Vendor-supplied beverages,"* he thought, pouring a second glass. *"Acceptable."*

---

Two hours north of Oakhaven, the carriage overtook the goats.

<br/>

Mark saw them first as a long, dusty smudge on the road ahead: six wagons stacked high with bales of raw wool, moving at the unhurried pace of a business that had been bribing the same toll keepers for eleven years. Around and between the wagons trotted perhaps forty goats, roped in strings, bells clanking.

<br/>

The carriage did not slow down. It didn't honk, because it had nothing to honk with. It simply rolled up behind the convoy in total silence, and the goats noticed it.

<br/>

The effect was immediate.

<br/>

The goat at the very back of the convoy turned its head, looked at the carriage, and lay down flat in the road. The goat in front of it turned, saw the first goat lying down, saw the carriage, and lay down too. Then the next. Then the next. A ripple of collapsing goats rolled up the length of the convoy, string by string, wagon by wagon, forty goats folding their legs and dropping into the dust one after another, like a stadium crowd doing the wave in reverse.

<br/>

The wagon drivers swore. The carriage glided around them on the verge, perfectly smooth, perfectly silent, and Mark pressed his face to the dark glass as he passed.

<br/>

On the bench of the last wagon, wrapped in a blanket that was mostly wool and partly goat, sat a small, sharp-eyed figure he recognized at once.

<br/>

Wren.

<br/>

She was supposed to be in Oakhaven. Pip had assigned a boy named Dace to the convoy, a twelve-year-old who could reportedly outrun a horse over a short distance and a goat over any distance at all. Mark had approved the assignment personally. He was now looking at Wren.

<br/>

She saw him looking. She grinned enormously and waved with both hands.

<br/>

Mark sighed, very deeply, and waved back.

<br/>

*"Unauthorized personnel substitution,"* he thought. *"Unapproved by management. Executed flawlessly."* He added a note to the ledger: *Wren: replaced Dace on convoy. Method unknown. Dace presumably fine. Promote, or ground. Possibly both.*

<br/>

Behind him, as the carriage pulled away, forty goats got slowly back to their feet and looked at each other in deep embarrassment.

---

They reached the capital in the late afternoon.

<br/>

Mark had seen big cities before. He'd lived for thirty years within commuting distance of the Research Triangle and spent a week in Tokyo for a vendor summit he still had nightmares about. But he had not been ready for the way the capital rose up out of the river plain: tier after tier of pale stone, roofs of blue slate and copper gone green, bridges stacked on bridges, towers with Aether-lamps already glowing faintly at their tips in the late light like a city full of pilot lights.

<br/>

At the outer tollgate, a long line of carts, riders, and foot travelers waited to pay the city levy. The toll keeper, a thick-necked man in a leather apron with an iron cashbox, was arguing loudly with a turnip merchant about the definition of a cartload.

<br/>

The carriage did not join the line.

<br/>

It rolled straight past it, past the turnips, past a nobleman on a white horse who opened his mouth to object and then, seeing the door, closed it again. It stopped in front of the toll keeper.

<br/>

The toll keeper turned around, irritated. He saw the empty driver's bench. His eyes traveled down to the silver circle and line on the door.

<br/>

The color left his face like water out of a basin.

<br/>

He bowed. Deeply, carefully, from the waist. To the empty driver's bench.

<br/>

Then he stepped aside and raised the barrier without a word, and the carriage rolled through. As it passed, Mark saw him straighten up and look at the turnip merchant, and the turnip merchant look at him, and neither of them say anything at all.

<br/>

*"Ah,"* Mark thought, settling back against the velvet. *"So the entire city gets out of this carriage's way."*

<br/>

He had worked with executives like that. You didn't see them coming. You just noticed, after a while, that every conversation you'd been having had quietly stopped.

<br/>

He opened the ledger on his knee and wrote:

<br/>

> *Vendor Review: Thorne Carriage (Model: Unknown)*
> *Ride quality: Exceptional. Smoothest vehicle I have been in on either world.*
> *Legroom: Generous.*
> *Refreshments: Water. Glacial. Probably not poisoned.*
> *Driver: Absent.*
> *Noise: None, which is worse than noise.*
> *Effect on local goats: Total.*
> *Effect on toll collectors: Total.*
> *Would not recommend: existential dread.*
> *Rating: 3/5.*

<br/>

He looked at it, considered, and crossed out the *3*.

<br/>

*3.5*, he wrote. The water really had been very good.

---

The Thorne residence stood on the highest tier of the city, behind a wall of pale stone with a single iron gate, and it was not, Mark decided, a house.

<br/>

It was a headquarters.

<br/>

He had spent four decades walking into corporate headquarters, and he recognized the grammar of it instantly. The long, curving drive, designed so that you'd see the building for a full minute before you reached it, and feel small the entire time. The courtyard paved in stone so clean it looked unused. The front doors, three times taller than any human being needed, because the doors were not for people. The doors were for making people understand where they were.

<br/>

The carriage stopped. The door opened by itself.

<br/>

At the foot of the steps stood a tall, elderly man in an immaculate grey coat, with silver hair combed so precisely that it looked engraved, and the posture of a fence post that had been to finishing school.

<br/>

"Mr. Vance," he said, and bowed. "I am Pemberton, steward of this house. His Lordship bids you welcome. Do settle."

<br/>

"Thank you," said Mark, climbing down.

<br/>

*"One,"* he thought.

<br/>

"May I take your satchel, sir?"

<br/>

"No, thank you."

<br/>

Pemberton's expression did not change at all, which Mark understood to be a very significant change of expression.

<br/>

"Of course, sir," he said. "Do settle."

<br/>

*"Two."*

<br/>

He led Mark up the steps and through the enormous doors into a hall paved in black and white marble, where three more servants in grey coats were waiting in a line, every one of them, Mark noticed, wearing a thin silver ring on the third finger of the right hand. The stones were dark. For now.

<br/>

"His Lordship has arranged a bath for you, sir," Pemberton said. "And fresh clothing. After your journey."

<br/>

Mark looked down at himself: linen tunic, canvas apron (he'd forgotten to take it off), brown trousers, scuffed work boots, a faint dusting of road and goat.

<br/>

He recognized the move. It was the same as being offered a branded company fleece the moment you arrived for an acquisition meeting. Polite. Generous. Entirely about making sure you walked into the boardroom wearing their colors.

<br/>

"That's very kind," he said. "I'm fine as I am."

<br/>

There was a pause about the length of a heartbeat.

<br/>

"Of course, sir," said Pemberton. "Do settle."

<br/>

*"Three."*

---

Then they made him wait.

<br/>

He'd known they would. He had sat in a great many waiting rooms in his life, and he knew exactly what an hour in a beautiful room meant. It meant: *We are very busy. You are not. Consider this carefully before you ask for anything.* He had done it himself, twice, to vendors who'd deserved it, and felt bad about it afterward, but only slightly.

<br/>

The waiting room was magnificent. Tall windows, soft chairs, a fire laid in a white marble grate that had been lit by someone who knew what they were doing. A small table with a bowl of fruit so perfect that Mark suspected it of being decorative. And on every wall, from floor to ceiling, portraits.

<br/>

Mark had an hour. He used it.

<br/>

There were twenty-one portraits. All of them were Thornes. All of them had the same nose, the same severe mouth, and the same faint expression of a man who has just been told that something is his fault and has decided to forgive everyone involved. The oldest were dark with varnish, painted in a stiff, flat style with gold leaf in the backgrounds. The newest were soft and bright.

<br/>

And every single one of them wore the same ring.

<br/>

Mark started at the oldest portrait and worked forward, the way he'd once traced a stolen laptop through six departments' worth of asset records. Third finger, right hand. Silver band, worn a little thinner in each painting. A circle cut by a single line on the face. A small dark stone.

<br/>

Dark. Dark. Dark. Dark. Twenty generations of Thornes, five hundred years of portraits, and in every one of them, the stone was black.

<br/>

He reached the last portrait, hung near the door, its paint so fresh that he could still smell the linseed oil.

<br/>

Lord Aldric Thorne: silver-haired, lined, severe, seated at a heavy oak desk.

<br/>

The stone in his ring was not black.

<br/>

The painter had added, with great care and evident nervousness, a faint, warm glow of red.

<br/>

Mark stood in front of it for a long time.

<br/>

*"Chain of custody,"* he thought. *"Twenty owners. Five hundred years. Nobody ever had to look at it."*

<br/>

*"Until me."*

<br/>

"His Lordship will see you now," said Pemberton, from the doorway. "Do settle."

<br/>

Mark hadn't heard him come in. Nobody in this house made any noise at all.

<br/>

*"Four,"* he thought, and followed him out.

---

The study was on the highest floor of the house, at the end of a long, quiet corridor lined with books.

<br/>

It was smaller than Mark expected, and more used. Tall shelves on every wall, the books on them worn at the spines from actual reading. A heavy oak desk. A single window looking out over the tiers of the city, the river, and the plain beyond, gone gold and blue in the evening light.

<br/>

Lord Aldric Thorne rose from behind the desk as Mark came in.

<br/>

He was older than Mark had expected, and smaller. In the portrait he'd looked like a monument. In person he looked like a retired surgeon: lean and silver-haired and very precise, in a plain dark coat with no embroidery at all. His face was lined and severe, but his eyes, when they settled on Mark, were not unkind. They were simply very, very attentive.

<br/>

On the third finger of his right hand, the ring glowed faintly gold.

<br/>

"Mr. Vance," he said. His voice was quiet and dry, a voice that had never once in its life needed to be raised. "Thank you for coming. I know it was a long way, and I know you had reason not to." He gestured to the chair across the desk. "Do settle."

<br/>

*"Five,"* thought Mark, and sat.

<br/>

"Tea?"

<br/>

"Please."

<br/>

Aldric poured it himself, from a plain white pot, into two plain white cups. He didn't ring for a servant. He added nothing, offered nothing, and set Mark's cup in front of him with the handle turned exactly toward Mark's right hand. Mark noticed the handle, and noticed that he'd noticed, and had the distinct feeling that he was supposed to.

<br/>

"I hope the journey was comfortable."

<br/>

"Very," said Mark. "The water was excellent. Your toll keepers are very respectful."

<br/>

"They are paid to be," Aldric said. "Badly, I'm afraid. And your friend, the girl on the wool wagon? She arrived safely, I trust?"

<br/>

Mark held his cup very still.

<br/>

"Wren," Aldric said pleasantly. "Eleven. Quite fearless. She has been asking about you at a tannery two streets from my gate for the last hour. The tanner's apprentices have been hanging hides on the drying line in a way that I think is meant to be a code." He sipped his tea. "It's rather a good code. I couldn't break it. I expect it has something to do with goats."

<br/>

"Most things in Oakhaven do," Mark said.

<br/>

"So I'm told." Aldric set down his cup. "How is Garek's knee?"

<br/>

There was a short silence.

<br/>

"Improving," Mark said carefully.

<br/>

"I'm glad. He's had a hard life. Most of the men in that line have." Aldric folded his hands on the desk. "Forty-three patients last week at your Infirmary. Seven of them employed by Nan Brask, two by me, though they don't know it, and one, a man called Brann, who has hit a great many people with chairs and is, I understand, very sorry about most of them." He paused. "Your mage warms their backs. Your engraver makes them little leather springs to squeeze. And not one of them has ever paid a copper."

<br/>

"They pay in favors," Mark said. "And information."

<br/>

"Yes," said Aldric. "So do mine."

<br/>

Mark sat back in his chair.

<br/>

*"Right,"* he thought. *"So that's the opening slide. 'We know everything about you.' Classic. Establish dominance. Make the vendor feel watched."*

<br/>

The trouble was that he'd sat through that slide from a dozen executives, and with most of them it was a bluff. You could hear the gaps. The research had been done by an analyst the night before, and they'd remembered three facts and were hoping you wouldn't test them on a fourth.

<br/>

Aldric didn't have gaps. Aldric had a man called Brann and a number of chairs.

<br/>

"You're wondering why I asked you here," Aldric said.

<br/>

"I'm wondering why you asked *nicely*," said Mark. "Your family locked me in an archive with a construct the last time you wanted to talk to me."

<br/>

Aldric inclined his head, as if conceding a point in a debate.

<br/>

"My son locked you in an archive," he said. "On his own initiative, as it turns out, and with a great deal more enthusiasm than judgment. I have spoken to him about it." His face didn't change, but something in the room grew very slightly colder. "And I asked you nicely, Mr. Vance, because I have watched you very carefully for two months, and I have come to the conclusion that you are a reasonable man. And I would very much prefer to have a reasonable conversation."

---

It was, Mark had to admit afterward, an extraordinarily good conversation.

<br/>

That was the problem.

<br/>

He'd come prepared for a villain. He'd rehearsed it in the carriage: the sneer, the threats, the speech about power and destiny that he'd been planning to sit through politely while silently cataloging every lie. He'd met plenty of villains in his life. Most of them had been in procurement.

<br/>

Aldric Thorne didn't give a speech. He asked questions.

<br/>

He asked how Mark had worked out that the Sacred Restriction was a pinch in the return channel, and listened to the answer all the way through without interrupting. He asked how the reverse-drain worked on the construct, and when Mark hesitated, he said, "You don't have to tell me. I'm curious, not collecting." He asked about the grounding wire on Julian's staff in the arena, and when Mark described it, Aldric was quiet for a moment and then said, very simply, "No magic at all. Just a wire and the ground. I watched it from the gallery. It was the most elegant thing I've seen in forty years."

<br/>

And then, when Mark had answered enough questions that the tea had gone cold, Aldric started explaining.

<br/>

Not everything. Mark noticed that right away. He drew a careful boundary around the conversation the way a good executive draws a boundary around a briefing: *this is what you need, and nothing past this line*. But inside the boundary, he was clear, and patient, and honest in a way that made the back of Mark's neck prickle.

<br/>

"You've been calling it a throttle," Aldric said. "A rate limit. Julian told me. He found the word very irritating." He turned his teacup slowly on its saucer. "You're quite right. That's exactly what it is. Five hundred years ago, the Concord deliberately made every spell in the Empire worse. Less efficient. Hotter. Noisier in the hand and quieter everywhere else. Every staff, every bracelet, every lamp regulator in every smithy. Every mage who has trained since has paid for it in blisters and burned tendons and the deep conviction that magic is supposed to hurt."

<br/>

"Aether demands suffering," said Mark.

<br/>

"Archmage Ignis is a fool," said Aldric mildly, "but he is a fool who is accidentally repeating something true. The suffering is the *price*, Mr. Vance. It isn't the point. It's the cost of the point."

<br/>

"And the point is?"

<br/>

Aldric looked at him for a moment, as if deciding where the line was.

<br/>

"Clean magic is loud," he said.

<br/>

Mark said nothing.

<br/>

"Not to us. To us it's silent. You've seen that. No sparks, no heat, no chanting. A perfect, frictionless thing. But a clean spell doesn't take its energy from the caster's body, the way a dirty one does. It takes it from the world. From the air. From the heat in a room." He glanced at Mark. "From the moss at your feet. And when it does, it *rings*. Like a struck glass. You heard it, I think. In your shed. Fourteen thousand cycles."

<br/>

The cold air in the shed. The frost on the ledger. The chime in his teeth.

<br/>

"Loud things," Aldric said quietly, "are heard."

<br/>

*It doesn't like noise.*

<br/>

Mark kept his face very still. It took some effort.

<br/>

"Heard by what?" he asked.

<br/>

"That," said Aldric, "isn't mine to tell. And you would not believe me if I did." He set down his cup. "But I'll tell you what I believe, which is this. Five hundred years ago, some very frightened people made a very hard decision. They decided that every mage in the Empire would pay a little pain, every day, forever, so that the world would stay quiet. They built the throttle into the plates and the words into the language and the constructs into the cellars, and then they made sure nobody would ever have reason to look at any of it again." He spread his hands. "It has worked. For five centuries. A boy's blistered palms are the price of a quiet world." He paused. "You were an engineer, before you were a scrap boy. Weren't you? Somewhere."

<br/>

"Somewhere," said Mark.

<br/>

"Then let me ask you an engineer's question. You have a system that has been stable for five hundred years. It has a known cost. You don't fully understand why it was designed the way it was. And someone has just discovered a way to bypass its central safety feature, and has started teaching the bypass to his friends." Aldric's voice didn't change at all. "What do you do?"

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth.

<br/>

He closed it again.

<br/>

*"You rate-limit him,"* said a voice in the back of his head, a voice that had spent forty years writing incident reports. *"You lock down the bypass. You audit everyone who's used it. You don't care how clever it is. You care that nobody understands the blast radius."*

<br/>

*"That's what you'd do. That's what you've done. Twice. In 2011 and 2019."*

<br/>

He looked at Aldric Thorne across the desk, at the precise silver hair and the patient eyes and the plain white teacup turned exactly so, and felt something deeply uncomfortable settle into his stomach.

<br/>

*"I like him,"* Mark realized, with a kind of slow horror. *"He's the first executive I've met in this world who actually understands the architecture."*

<br/>

*"That's what makes him dangerous."*

<br/>

"You'd tell him to stop," Mark said at last. "Until you understood it."

<br/>

"Yes," said Aldric gently. "I would."

---

Then he brought out the contract.

<br/>

He didn't call it a contract. He called it "a proposal, for your consideration," and drew it from a drawer in the desk: three sheets of the same thick, cream-colored paper as the invitation, written in a clear and elegant hand. He laid them on the desk in front of Mark and turned them around so they faced him, the same way he'd turned the handle of the teacup.

<br/>

*"Never sign at the table,"* thought Mark. *"When they bring the contract to the meeting, it's because they've already written the parts that matter."*

<br/>

Valerius had been right.

<br/>

He read it. Slowly, the whole way through, the way he'd read every vendor agreement in his life: first for what it said, then for what it didn't.

<br/>

It was generous. It was enormously, suspiciously, beautifully generous.

<br/>

The Fish & Hammer workshop would receive a full Concord charter: licensed, protected, beyond the reach of any Chapterhouse Inspector. Torren would be named a Master Artificer of the province. Lyra would receive her mastery five years early and a workshop of her own. The Fish & Hammer Infirmary would be granted a royal patent and a stipend, so that it could go on treating anyone who came to it, forever, free. Mage Vespera would be restored to the rank she had held in the capital before she went south. Mark himself would be given a laboratory beneath this very house, with materials, assistants, and access to every First Era record the Concord had ever kept.

<br/>

In exchange, the terms asked only three things.

<br/>

*First*: that all clean-casting instruments in his possession or the possession of his associates be surrendered to the Concord, including Prototype 2.0, the instruments designated L-1 and V-1, and "one construct core, the property of the Concord, currently held in a locked iron box."

<br/>

*Second*: that clean casting, in any form, by any of them, cease.

<br/>

*Third*: that Mark Vance enter the service of the Concord as an artificer, and apply his talents to "the reinforcement of the Measure, the location and correction of drift, and the instruction of the Concord's agents in its detection."

<br/>

Mark read the third clause twice.

<br/>

*The location and correction of drift.*

<br/>

He thought of Tessa Fenn, flicking her chipped wooden rod across the sand in short, careful, efficient bursts, because she couldn't afford to burn out a focus. Of Corwin, who had spent three years learning to do the absolute minimum and had accidentally become the most economical caster in the Academy. Of Vespera's hands, warm and steady and silent on a dockhand's back.

<br/>

*Help us find the leaks.*

<br/>

"You want me to build you better detectors," he said.

<br/>

"I want you to help me keep the world quiet," said Aldric. "You see clean circuits the way my rings do. Better, I suspect. You have an engineer's eye for where a system is bleeding." He smiled faintly. "You'd be very good at it. I think you'd even enjoy it. You were, I'm told, extremely good at incident response."

<br/>

Mark looked up sharply.

<br/>

Aldric's expression didn't change. He was simply waiting, attentive and courteous, a man who had used a phrase that he couldn't possibly have known.

<br/>

*"He's quoting my ledger,"* Mark thought. *"He can't read my ledger. Nobody can read my ledger."*

<br/>

*"...Or Julian told him what I sound like."*

<br/>

He decided, very firmly, that it was the second one.

<br/>

"There's a fourth clause," Mark said. "That isn't on the paper."

<br/>

Aldric's eyebrows rose a quarter of an inch. Mark had the strong impression he was pleased.

<br/>

"Your son cast clean," Mark said. "In public. In front of four of your own men and four hundred witnesses. Under your own rules, that's drift. Julian's the leak now. Not me."

<br/>

"Yes," said Aldric quietly.

<br/>

"And the Concord will want an explanation."

<br/>

"They will want one very badly," said Aldric. "They are already asking." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "But a young man whose staff was *interfered with*, in the middle of a bout, by an outside influence, is not a heretic. He is a victim. And four hundred people saw an apprentice from the coal pits run across the sand and lay his hands and a length of common wire on my son's staff, in the very moment it failed." He looked at Mark. "It would be very easy to write that down in a way that explains everything."

<br/>

Mark felt it land, very quietly, like a stone dropping into deep water.

<br/>

The rescue. The copper wire. *Just a wire and the ground.* The one thing in the entire tournament he'd been proud of, and Aldric had turned it, in a single sentence, into evidence.

<br/>

"Of course," Aldric went on, "if you were already in the Concord's service, it would be written down rather differently. A sanctioned field trial, conducted by the Concord's newest artificer, on a willing participant. Regrettable. Unsupervised. Entirely within the rules." He spread his hands. "Nobody would be in any trouble at all. Not you. And not my son."

<br/>

*"There it is,"* Mark thought. *"The reasonable offer. Sign, and everyone's safe. Don't, and either I take the blame or Julian does. And he's left it to me to decide which."*

<br/>

It was very well designed. He'd have admired it, if he'd been on the other side of the desk.

<br/>

He slid the three sheets of cream paper back across the desk, squared their edges neatly, and folded his hands on top of them.

<br/>

"This is a very generous proposal," he said. "I'd like a copy, please. To review with counsel."

<br/>

Aldric blinked.

<br/>

"Counsel?"

<br/>

"My smith," said Mark.

<br/>

For the first time since Mark had walked in, Lord Aldric Thorne laughed: a short, surprised, genuine sound, quickly put away.

<br/>

"Of course," he said. "Pemberton will make you a copy." He rose from behind the desk. "But not tonight. Tonight you are my guest, and you will dine with my family. We can speak of business again in the morning." He paused at the door. "Do settle, Mr. Vance."

<br/>

*"Six,"* thought Mark.

---

Dinner was served in a long, candlelit room with a table that could have seated thirty and had been laid for four.

<br/>

Aldric sat at its head. To his right sat a pale, pleasant, rather fragile-looking woman in grey silk, whom Pemberton introduced as Lady Seraphine Thorne, and who smiled at Mark with such warm and immediate kindness that he felt briefly guilty about everything.

<br/>

"You must be the boy who saved Julian," she said. "From the staff." She reached across and touched his hand lightly. "Thank you. He won't say it. So I shall."

<br/>

"It was just a wire, my lady," said Mark.

<br/>

"It was *his* wire," Lady Seraphine said. "That's what matters."

<br/>

To Aldric's left, across the table from Mark, sat Julian.

<br/>

Mark had seen Julian sneering in the amphitheater, and composed and cold through the grate of a barred archive door. He had seen him on the sand at the end of the tournament, scorched and blistered and trying not to cry. He hadn't seen him like this.

<br/>

Julian sat perfectly upright, in a perfectly pressed dark coat, with his hair perfectly combed, and he looked like someone had taken the boy out of him and left the clothes. Both his hands were wrapped in clean white linen to the wrist. He held his fork awkwardly, in the crook of his bandaged fingers, the way Mott held his quill.

<br/>

And the ring was gone.

<br/>

There was a faint pale band of skin on the third finger of his right hand, where it had been, and he kept touching it with his thumb under the table, the way you'd probe a missing tooth with your tongue.

<br/>

He didn't look at Mark. He didn't look at anyone. He looked at his plate.

<br/>

"Julian," said his father pleasantly, "is recovering. Aren't you?"

<br/>

"Yes, Father."

<br/>

"His hands will heal in a month. The physicians are quite confident." Aldric shook out his napkin. "He has been relieved of his duties for the time being. Rest is very important after a failure of that kind." He said *failure* the way you'd say *server outage*: neutrally, as a category, without any blame at all, which somehow made it so much worse.

<br/>

*"He's talking about his son,"* Mark thought, *"like a decommissioned asset."*

<br/>

*"And his son is sitting right there."*

<br/>

"Do settle, sir," murmured a footman at Mark's elbow, sliding his chair in.

<br/>

*"Seven."*

---

The soup came first.

<br/>

It arrived in a white porcelain tureen, carried by a footman in a grey coat, and was ladled into shallow white bowls with great ceremony. It was a pale golden broth with a few thin slices of something green floating on top, and it smelled wonderful.

<br/>

It was also, Mark discovered with his first spoonful, stone cold.

<br/>

Not lukewarm. Not *gone off the heat a bit too long*. Cold. Cellar-cold. The kind of cold that had been arranged.

<br/>

Lady Seraphine took a sip from her own spoon and made a small dismayed sound. "Oh dear. Pemberton, I think the soup's gone cold. Could you have it—"

<br/>

"Leave it," said Aldric.

<br/>

He said it quite gently. Lady Seraphine stopped speaking at once, and set her spoon down, and looked at her bowl.

<br/>

Mark lowered his spoon slowly and looked around the room.

<br/>

There were four footmen standing against the walls. They were standing very straight, with their hands folded in front of them, in the posture of men who are entirely invisible and have been trained for it since childhood. And every one of them, on the third finger of the right hand, wore a thin silver ring.

<br/>

All four of them were watching him.

<br/>

Not looking at him. Footmen don't look. But watching, out of the corners of their eyes, with the steady, patient, entirely-not-looking attention of a security camera mounted in the corner of a lobby.

<br/>

*"Oh,"* Mark thought. *"Oh, that's good."*

<br/>

It was a test. A beautifully simple one. Put a cold bowl of soup in front of the boy who fixes things, the boy whose mage friend had once warmed a bowl of cold soup silently at *The Silver Ladle* and gone pink about it for a week, and see if he couldn't help himself. Just a little warmth. Just a little, silent, clean, zero-loss nudge of heat into a bowl of broth. Anybody would. It would be so easy. It would be *polite*.

<br/>

And four rings would light up gold.

<br/>

He could feel it: the faint, familiar tingle at the edge of his fingertips. The ambient Aether in the room, rich and still. A spoonful of heat would cost him nothing at all.

<br/>

He looked down at the soup.

<br/>

Then he picked up his spoon, and ate it.

<br/>

All of it. Cold. With enormous enthusiasm.

<br/>

"This is *delicious*," he announced, scraping the bottom of the bowl. "Chilled! What a bold choice. Very sophisticated. Very refreshing after a long journey." He set the spoon down with a satisfied clink. "Where I come from, we call this gazpacho."

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"*Gaz*-pacho," repeated Lady Seraphine, delighted.

<br/>

"And where is that, Mr. Vance?" Aldric asked mildly. "Where you come from?"

<br/>

"Downriver," said Mark.

<br/>

He held out his empty bowl toward the nearest footman.

<br/>

"Could I have some more?"

<br/>

The footman looked at the bowl. He looked at Mark. He looked, very briefly, at Lord Thorne, and Mark could have sworn he saw a flicker of something on the man's carefully expressionless face that was almost betrayal.

<br/>

"Of course, sir," the footman said. "Do settle."

<br/>

*"Eight,"* thought Mark, and accepted a second bowl of ice-cold soup, and ate every drop.

<br/>

Across the table, for the first time that evening, Julian looked up from his plate.

<br/>

He looked at Mark, and at the second empty bowl. And something happened at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile, and was gone almost before it arrived.

<br/>

> *Vendor Review (mental): The Thorne Residence, Soup Course*
> *Temperature: Cold. Deliberately. Hostile.*
> *Flavor: Excellent, honestly. Saffron, I think. Some sort of leek.*
> *Service: Four footmen, all wearing security cameras on their hands.*
> *Ambience: Psychological warfare, with candles.*
> *Would order again: Yes. Out of spite.*
> *Rating: 4/5.*

<br/>

---

<br/>

The main course arrived hot, which Mark took as an admission of defeat.

<br/>

It was a roast of some kind of game bird in a dark, rich sauce, and it was the best thing he'd eaten in either world, and he resented that slightly. Lady Seraphine asked him about Oakhaven and listened with real interest while he described the Fish & Hammer sign, and Torren's naming of the blowers, and the goat with the floating problem. She laughed at Duchess. She laughed at Edna. She asked, with great seriousness, how Bessie was.

<br/>

"Running beautifully," said Mark.

<br/>

"I'm so glad," said Lady Seraphine, and Mark found that he meant it when he smiled back.

<br/>

Aldric ate slowly and said little. Julian ate almost nothing.

<br/>

They were halfway through the bird when Pemberton came in.

<br/>

He did not hurry. Nobody in this house hurried. But he crossed the room a fraction faster than he had all evening, and he bent to Lord Thorne's ear and murmured something, and held out a small folded letter on a silver tray.

<br/>

The letter was sealed with wax. Even from across the table, Mark could see the impression: a circle, cut by a line. The same seal as the invitation. Except that the wax wasn't dark red, like the Thorne seal.

<br/>

It was grey.

<br/>

Aldric looked at it for a moment without touching it. On his right hand, the gold stone in his ring flickered once, like a candle in a draft.

<br/>

"Forgive me," he said. He rose, took the letter, and set his napkin down beside his plate with great precision. "A matter of business. I won't be long. Seraphine, my dear, perhaps you would see about the dessert."

<br/>

"Of course," said Lady Seraphine, already rising. She touched Julian's shoulder lightly as she passed. Julian didn't move.

<br/>

The door closed behind them both.

<br/>

The footmen remained, against the walls. But they had been trained not to hear, and for a moment, the long candlelit room was almost private.

<br/>

Mark looked across the table.

<br/>

Julian was looking at his plate. His bandaged hands were resting on either side of it. His thumb was turning, slowly and endlessly, around the pale place on his finger where the ring had been.

<br/>

"How are your hands?" Mark asked quietly.

<br/>

"They'll heal." Julian didn't look up. "The physicians are quite confident."

<br/>

"Your ring—"

<br/>

"He took it." Julian's voice was very flat. "The night of the tournament. In the carriage. He didn't say anything. He just held out his hand." The thumb went around again. "I'm not a Keeper anymore. I'm a problem. A problem that needs to be *explained*."

<br/>

Mark said nothing. He knew the word. He'd been on both ends of it.

<br/>

Julian sat still for a long moment. Then, very quietly, without raising his eyes from his plate, he spoke again.

<br/>

"He's telling you the truth."

<br/>

Mark went still.

<br/>

"Upstairs. Everything he said to you. The price. The noise. The quiet world." Julian's voice was barely above a whisper. "I don't know exactly what he told you. But I know what he tells *everyone*. And it's true. All of it. Every word."

<br/>

He finally looked up at Mark, and his eyes were red-rimmed and very tired and terribly young.

<br/>

"That's the worst part," he said.

<br/>

From the corridor came the faint, unhurried sound of footsteps returning.

<br/>

Julian looked down again at once. His thumb stopped moving. He picked up his fork in his bandaged fingers.

<br/>

And just before the door opened, so quietly that Mark almost thought he'd imagined it, he said:

<br/>

"Don't take it."

<br/>

---

<br/>

Aldric Thorne sat back down at the head of the table and laid his napkin across his lap, and for a fraction of a second, before he arranged it again, Mark saw his face.

<br/>

He'd seen that face before. He'd seen it in a hundred conference rooms on the other side of the world: on the faces of competent, decent, overworked directors walking back into a meeting after a call from upstairs. It was the face of a man who had been running his own department perfectly well for years and had just been told that the board would like a word.

<br/>

It was only there for a moment. Then it was gone, and Aldric was perfectly composed again.

<br/>

"My apologies," he said. "A message from my superiors."

<br/>

Mark blinked. "Your *superiors*?"

<br/>

"Everyone has them, Mr. Vance." Aldric picked up his fork. "House Thorne keeps the Measure. We have kept it for five hundred years, faithfully, in this province and three others. But the Measure isn't ours. It was entrusted to us." He cut a piece of bird with great precision. "The glyph that lit on the Oakhaven Anchor the night of my son's tournament has been read by people who rank considerably above this house. They are attentive. They are not patient." He ate the bite of bird. "And they are sending someone."

<br/>

"Sending who?"

<br/>

"Someone," said Aldric, "whom I would very much prefer you never met."

<br/>

There was a short silence. Lady Seraphine came back in with a footman carrying a tray of small, perfect pastries, saw her husband's face, and quietly sat down without saying anything.

<br/>

Mark looked at Lord Aldric Thorne, Keeper of the Measure, master of a house that made toll keepers bow to empty carriages, and saw him, quite suddenly and quite clearly, for exactly what he was.

<br/>

*Middle management.*

<br/>

A very senior, very capable, very frightening director, sitting at the top of his own org chart, who had just discovered that it was not, after all, the top of the org chart. Somewhere above him, there was another level. And that level had just sent him a memo sealed in grey wax.

<br/>

*"Oh,"* Mark thought, with a strange, unexpected pang of something that was almost sympathy. *"Everybody has a Brad."*

<br/>

"I had intended to give you a month to consider my proposal," Aldric said. "I find I can no longer give you a month." He set down his fork. "The silver moon is new in eighteen days. I will need your answer by then. After that…" He paused, and for the first time all evening, chose his words with visible care. "After that, I'm afraid the matter will pass out of my hands. And I would not like it to."

<br/>

"Eighteen days," said Mark.

<br/>

"Eighteen days," Aldric agreed. "Pemberton will give you your copy in the morning. Take it to your counsel." The faintest flicker of a smile. "Your *smith*."

<br/>

He rose. Dinner was over.

<br/>

At the door, he paused and looked back at Mark, standing beside his chair in his linen tunic and his work apron and his scuffed boots, with a faint ring of cold soup on his sleeve.

<br/>

"My son tells me you saved his life," Aldric said quietly. "I'm grateful. Truly." He held Mark's gaze. "It is the reason I'm making you an offer, Mr. Vance. Rather than a different arrangement."

<br/>

He turned to go. Then he added, as if it had only just occurred to him:

<br/>

"Goodnight. My regards to the cheese."

<br/>

And he was gone.

<br/>

---

<br/>

The guest room was enormous, silent, and roughly the temperature of the soup.

<br/>

Pemberton showed him in, turned down a bed large enough to land a carriage on, lit a single lamp, and asked whether there would be anything else.

<br/>

"No, thank you."

<br/>

"Very good, sir. Do settle."

<br/>

*"Nine,"* thought Mark, and the door closed without a sound.

<br/>

He stood in the middle of the room for a while. Then he did what he'd done in every hotel room he'd ever stayed in on a business trip, out of forty years of habit: he walked the perimeter. Window latches. Wardrobe. Under the bed. Behind the curtains. The fireplace, which was laid but unlit, and which he did not light, and would not have lit for any amount of money. He checked the walls for anything that might be a ring, or a rune, or a very small footman.

<br/>

He found nothing. That proved nothing. He went to the window anyway.

<br/>

The guest room looked out over the lower tiers of the city, where the roofs stepped down toward the river in a jumble of slate and chimney and washing lines, and the Aether-lamps glowed in long blue chains along the bridges. Two streets down from the Thorne wall, Mark could see a long, low building with a yard full of wooden frames and vats, and a smell rising from it that he could detect even through the glass.

<br/>

*Osric's tannery*, he thought. *Alone in the city is not the same as alone.*

<br/>

As he watched, a lantern was lit in the tannery yard. Then two small figures ran out with a long pole, and began to hang something on the drying line.

<br/>

Hides. Big, flat, stretched hides, pale in the lantern light. One. Then a second beside it. Each one had something painted on it, very large, in what looked like whitewash and enthusiasm.

<br/>

Mark leaned closer to the glass and squinted.

<br/>

The first hide had a goat on it.

<br/>

He recognized the goat. It was the same goat that sat at the top of Nan Brask's knitted organizational chart, in the box connected to everything else, the one Mark had asked about on the day he met her and earned her terrifying smile. In the lower town, he had learned since, the goat meant *ours*. It meant *one of us*. It meant *are you all right*.

<br/>

The second hide had a large, lopsided, carefully painted yellow wedge.

<br/>

Mark stared at it.

<br/>

Then he put his forehead against the cold glass and started, very quietly, to laugh.

<br/>

*Goat. Cheese.*

<br/>

*Ours. Notify Lyra.*

<br/>

*"Are you all right? Lyra wants to know."*

<br/>

He wanted to signal back. He had no lamp he could safely move. He had no mirror. He had no magic he dared use in a house full of rings. Anything he did in this window, he had to assume four footmen and a lord would see.

<br/>

He thought about it for a moment.

<br/>

Then he reached into his satchel, took out the small waxed-paper parcel Lyra had pressed into his hand in the armory yard, and unwrapped it. A neat, pale, perfect wedge of Cheese the Third.

<br/>

He stood in the center of the window, in full view of the lamplight, facing the tannery.

<br/>

And he ate it.

<br/>

Slowly. Deliberately. One bite at a time, holding it up to the glass between bites so that there could be no mistake about what it was. It was very good cheese. He made sure to look like he was enjoying it, which was not difficult.

<br/>

When he'd finished, he held up the empty waxed paper, folded it carefully into a small square, and put it in his pocket.

<br/>

Down in the tannery yard, there was a short pause.

<br/>

Then one of the small figures ran out with the pole and reached up and flipped the goat hide over, so that its blank side showed.

<br/>

*Received.*

<br/>

A third figure appeared beside the other two, smaller than either of them, and Mark could see even at that distance that she was jumping up and down.

<br/>

He waved at the window, once. Then he drew the curtain, lay down on the enormous cold bed fully dressed, with his boots on and his satchel under his arm and a length of copper wire in his pocket, and stared up at the canopy.

<br/>

*"My regards to the cheese,"* he thought.

<br/>

*Aldric had known about the cheese.*

<br/>

*Aldric had known about the code. And the tannery. And Wren, and her age. And Garek's knee. And Brann's chairs. And the locked iron box on the top shelf of the workshop.*

<br/>

*And he'd let Mark see him know it.*

<br/>

He thought about the eighteen days. About grey wax. About *someone whom I would very much prefer you never met*. About Julian's thumb, turning and turning around the pale place on his finger.

<br/>

*He's telling you the truth. That's the worst part.*

<br/>

*Don't take it.*

<br/>

He didn't sleep much. He lay there in the dark, running the conversation over and over in his head, branch by branch, the way he'd once run a production incident at three in the morning. And every time he reached the end of it, he found himself in the same place, which was the most uncomfortable place an engineer can ever find himself.

<br/>

He agreed with Aldric about the problem.

<br/>

He just didn't agree about the solution.

<br/>

And he had eighteen days to work out the difference.

<br/>

---

<br/>

In the morning, Pemberton brought him breakfast on a tray, which was hot, and a thick envelope of cream paper, which contained a perfect copy of the proposal in the same elegant hand. Aldric did not come down to see him off. Lady Seraphine sent a small basket of the pastries from dinner, wrapped in a linen napkin, with a note that said, in a round, kind hand: *For the journey. And for your smith.*

<br/>

Julian was nowhere to be seen. Mark looked for him at every window on the way out. Every window was empty.

<br/>

The carriage was waiting at the foot of the steps, black and silent and driverless, its door already open.

<br/>

Pemberton accompanied him down. At the bottom of the steps, he paused and bowed.

<br/>

"His Lordship wishes you a safe journey, sir," he said. "Do settle."

<br/>

*"Ten,"* thought Mark.

<br/>

He climbed in. The door began to close by itself.

<br/>

"Pemberton," Mark said.

<br/>

The door stopped. Pemberton looked up.

<br/>

"Can I ask you something? Professionally?"

<br/>

"Of course, sir."

<br/>

"How many times a day do you say 'do settle'?"

<br/>

Pemberton considered the question with the full gravity of a man who had served one family for forty years and had never once been asked anything about himself.

<br/>

"I couldn't say, sir," he said at last. "I have never counted." He paused. "I would estimate… a great many."

<br/>

"It's a good phrase," said Mark. "Very versatile."

<br/>

"Thank you, sir." The faintest crease appeared at the corner of the steward's mouth. It might, in a less disciplined man, have been the beginning of a smile. "Do settle."

<br/>

*"Eleven,"* thought Mark, as the door clicked shut, and the carriage began to roll.

<br/>

---

He wrote the entry somewhere past the toll gate, with the cream-paper envelope on the seat beside him and Lady Seraphine's pastries on his knee.

### *Stinger: The Fish & Hammer — Oakhaven*

<br/>

At almost exactly the same hour that Mark was eating cold soup with great enthusiasm, two men in dark coats walked up the lane to the south armory yard.

<br/>

They were tall and well-dressed and very quiet. On the third finger of each man's right hand, a silver ring caught the light of the yard lamps. The stones were gold. One of them carried a folded writ, sealed in red wax with a circle and a line.

<br/>

They crossed the yard toward the workshop, past the cold forge, past Edna humming in her corner, past the painted sign of a fish holding a hammer.

<br/>

They stopped.

<br/>

The workshop doorway was full.

<br/>

Garek stood in it. He had been standing in it, more or less without a break, for two days. He stood with his feet planted wide, his arms folded across his chest, and his shoulders touching both sides of the frame, so that the doorway was not so much guarded as *occupied*. Above his head, in the corner of the frame, Legacy sat motionless in the center of her web, every one of her eight eyes fixed on the visitors.

<br/>

"Evening," said Garek.

<br/>

The first man held up the writ. "By order of House Thorne, we are here to inspect the premises—"

<br/>

"Doing my leg lifts," said Garek.

<br/>

The man paused. "I'm sorry?"

<br/>

"Leg lifts." Garek lifted one knee very slowly, held it, and lowered it again. "For the knee. Twice a day. Standing guard counts as rest." He lifted the other one. "Doctor's orders."

<br/>

The two men looked at each other.

<br/>

Behind Garek, in the lamplight of the workshop, Torren appeared at his shoulder, wearing his good apron and holding a brass clipboard.

<br/>

"Visitors!" he announced warmly. "Lovely. You'll want to sign in." He held out the clipboard, with a pen tied to it on a piece of string. "Name, House, purpose of visit, time in. Very important. We're *very* serious about the visitor log." He tapped the clipboard. "Safety."

<br/>

The first man looked at the clipboard.

<br/>

Nobody, in this world or any other, has ever questioned a brass clipboard.

<br/>

He took the pen and signed it. So did the second man. Torren examined both signatures with deep satisfaction and wrote, beside them, in the column headed *Purpose of Visit*, the word *Inspection*, and beside that, in the column headed *Outcome*, the word *Pending*.

<br/>

"Right," Torren said cheerfully. "Inspector'll be with you shortly." He looked over his shoulder. "*Lyra!*"

<br/>

From the back room came Lyra's voice, very clear and very calm.

<br/>

"The Fish & Hammer workshop is a sanctioned research site under Imperial Statute 402," she called, "with a registered supervising mage and a licensed journeyman engraver. Any inspection of its premises requires the countersignature of the supervising mage. She is currently away. Please leave your writ with the doorway." There was a short pause. "The *large* doorway."

<br/>

The two men looked at Garek.

<br/>

Garek lifted his left knee again, slowly, and held it, and looked back at them.

<br/>

After a long moment, the first man folded his writ and put it back inside his coat.

<br/>

"We'll return," he said.

<br/>

"Lovely," said Torren. "I'll put you down for a follow-up." He wrote something on the clipboard. "Mind the goat on your way out."

<br/>

There was no goat. But both men looked down anyway.

<br/>

They walked back across the yard and out into the lane, and the yard was quiet again. Edna hummed. Legacy, very slowly, relaxed one leg.

<br/>

Garek put his knee down.

<br/>

"How was that?" he asked.

<br/>

"Perfect," said Torren, and patted him on the shoulder, and went to lock the visitor log in the iron box on the top shelf, next to the thing that must never, under any circumstances, be charged.

<br/>

### *Stinger: Deep Vault 3 — Oakhaven Chapterhouse*

<br/>

Fifty feet beneath the cobblestones, in the cold and the dark, the obsidian pillar of the Oakhaven Anchor stood exactly as it had for five hundred years.

<br/>

Two glyphs burned on its face. One red. One gold.

<br/>

For many days, the gold glyph had pulsed slowly and steadily, like a resting heart.

<br/>

Tonight, without any sound at all, it brightened.

<br/>

It brightened until it was not gold but white-gold, until the whole vault was lit with it, until the iron-bound archive cases cast long, hard shadows across the floor. Then it steadied.

<br/>

And beneath it, slowly, a new line of First Era script etched itself into the stone, one sharp, deliberate glyph at a time:

<br/>

`[ESCALATION APPROVED]`

<br/>

`[AUDITOR CLASS DISPATCHED]`

<br/>

`[DESTINATION: SECTOR 04 — LOCAL GRID]`

<br/>

The light dimmed back to gold, and pulsed, and waited.

<br/>

Somewhere very far away, something that did not need a carriage had begun to travel.
