## Chapter 32: The Patch

<br/>

The first thing Mark noticed was the chime.

<br/>

It had stopped.

<br/>

It had been ringing for an hour, since the moment he pressed `[REGISTER]`: a single pure crystalline note at fourteen thousand cycles, held and held and held, humming in his teeth and his skull and the crystals on his wrist until he'd stopped being able to hear it at all, the way you stop hearing a refrigerator. And now it was gone. And the silence where it had been was so enormous that for a moment he thought he'd gone deaf.

<br/>

He hadn't. He could hear Torren breathing.

<br/>

Out in the crystal forest, slumped against the base of Grandmother with Gerald's dead grey core in his lap, Torren was breathing in long, ragged, heaving gasps, like a man who had just run a very slow race for a very long time. It was, Mark realized, the loudest sound in the cavern.

<br/>

Nothing else moved.

<br/>

The constructs had stopped. All of them, everywhere, in the middle of whatever they'd been doing: halfway up the stair, halfway through a lunge, one of them frozen with a silver hand an inch from Brann's collar. Brann was frozen too, staring at the hand. The dockhands were frozen. The patients were frozen. On the ledge, Ignis was frozen with his smoking staff half-raised and his mouth open in the middle of the word *sacred*.

<br/>

And in front of Mark, an inch from his face, the Auditor's silver rod was perfectly, absolutely still.

---

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 22: Change Deployed (Written Afterward)*
> *Change committed at approx. 02:50.*
> *Committed by: Vance, Mark. (Registered.)*
> *Reference instrument: L-2 (Lyra).*
> *Change description (Keeper shorthand, by Julian): 'A better way. Opened.'*
> *Outcome: see below.*
> *I need to write this down in order. All of it. Exactly as it happened. Otherwise I'm not going to believe it.*

---

The Auditor looked down at its book.

<br/>

It did it slowly, the way it did everything. Its blank grey face, an inch from Mark's, tilted downward, toward the open grey page in its left hand. Mark followed its gaze without meaning to.

<br/>

The page was changing.

<br/>

He watched it happen. The neat, precise rows of glyph-numbers that had filled the Auditor's book since the night it walked through the North Gate, the depths and widths and ratios of the Sacred Restriction, the checklist it had measured every blower and crystal and bracelet in Oakhaven against, began, line by line, to *dissolve*. Not to vanish. To *flow*. The numbers ran like ink in water and reformed into new numbers, one row after another, from the top of the page to the bottom, and Mark knew every one of them, because he'd watched them write themselves onto the console two minutes ago from the silver traces of a bracelet pressed flat against a plate.

<br/>

The long, sweeping First Era strokes. The open channels. The grounded return. The silver tail at the clasp. The four places where the lines narrowed, for the width of a fingernail, into the old geometry. The tiny shield of lead on its pin.

<br/>

Lyra's numbers. All of them.

<br/>

The Auditor was reading the new specification.

<br/>

It read it all the way to the bottom of the page. Then it turned the page, and that page was changing too, and it read that one. And the next. Mark stood absolutely still with the rod an inch from his face and watched an ancient, faceless, five-hundred-year-old servant of the Concord read a fifteen-year-old engraver's work, line by line, as carefully as it had ever read anything.

<br/>

Beside him, Lyra had stopped breathing.

<br/>

The Auditor closed its book.

<br/>

It looked at Mark.

<br/>

And then, slowly, it lowered the silver rod. Not away. *Down*. Down from Mark's face, past his chin, past his chest, until the tip of it hung an inch above his right wrist, and the leather cuff buckled around it: two crystal sockets, twin silver traces, six filed copper fins, a tiny silver pin by the palm.

<br/>

Prototype 2.0.

<br/>

The rod touched it.

<br/>

Mark felt it this time. He hadn't felt anything when it ruined Tilly; he'd only watched. Now he felt a cool, smooth, gentle pressure against the leather, like a fingertip, and then a faint tingle that ran along the silver traces under the cuff, from the sockets to the clasp, and he looked down and watched his own work *change*.

<br/>

The twin traces flowed. They didn't pinch. They didn't narrow. They lengthened, and curved, and swept, into the long, open First Era strokes of Lyra's design, and the copper fins smoothed and reshaped themselves around them, and at the clasp, a fine silver tail ran out of the leather and down, and touched the stone floor of the alcove. At four points along the cuff, the lines narrowed for the width of a fingernail into the old geometry, exactly to spec, and then widened again. And over the main trace, almost too small to see, a tiny shield of lead slid into place on a silver pin.

<br/>

The crystals in their sockets glowed a steady, cool, untroubled blue.

<br/>

The Auditor opened its book again, and wrote.

<br/>

The glyph glowed on the page as it was written. Mark read it without the translation layer having to reach for it at all.

<br/>

`[COMPLIANT.]`

---

And then it did something it had never done before.

<br/>

It turned the page back. Back to the first page of the section, where, Mark knew, the records were kept. The names. The people. Every person the Auditor had ever stood in front of and measured and marked: Garek, *compliant*; Torren, *compliant*; Vespera, *correction scheduled*; and a dozen pages back, in the workshop, a blank space, where a boy beside a shelf with a cheese on it had stood for five long seconds and the page had stayed empty.

<br/>

The Auditor lifted its rod and touched the tip of it to the page.

<br/>

And on that blank space, for the first time, it wrote something about him.

<br/>

`[VANCE, MARK — ARRIVAL — COMPLIANT WITH CURRENT STANDARD.]`

<br/>

It paused. The rod hovered. Then it wrote one more line, beneath the first.

<br/>

`[STATUS: RELEASED.]`

<br/>

Mark stood very still.

<br/>

Behind him, on the face of the column, the console's surface flickered. He turned his head, slowly, to look. His own record was still open there, where he'd left it, and it was updating, line by line, as he watched. *Correction scheduled — priority 1* faded and was gone. The hard white word at the top of the reconciliation list, *NEXT*, faded and was gone. And at the bottom of his record, where *status* had been, a single new word wrote itself, small and quiet and certain.

<br/>

`[STATUS: RESIDENT.]`

<br/>

He read it three times.

<br/>

*Resident.*

<br/>

Not *unregistered*. Not *held*. Not an exception the system didn't know how to handle, a null in a field, a gap on a page. Not a dream, or a glitch, or a dying brain giving him one last kindness. Not a stroke.

<br/>

*Resident.* Someone who lives here. Someone who belongs to the place he's in.

<br/>

"Mark?" said Lyra, very softly.

<br/>

He found that he couldn't answer her. He reached out, without looking, and found her hand, and held on to it.

<br/>

The Auditor closed its book.

---

Then it did what it had come to do.

<br/>

It reconciled.

<br/>

Julian had told them how it worked, in the Fish & Hammer, on the night he walked forty miles through the rain. *It doesn't execute the corrections as it goes. It schedules them. And when it's finished the whole sector, it goes down to the Anchor and reconciles. All at once.*

<br/>

Everything it had marked. Everything it had scheduled. Every name on the list.

<br/>

All at once.

<br/>

But the list had changed. The rules had changed. The specification had changed. And the Auditor, which had never once in five hundred years skipped a step, or questioned a rule, or done anything at all except exactly what its book told it to, now did exactly what its book told it to.

<br/>

It raised its silver rod, high, over its head, and held it there.

<br/>

And a wave of light rolled out of it.

<br/>

It was soft, and silver-white, the color of the glyph at the top of the Anchor and the light under Mark's palm on the plate. It came out of the Auditor in a great, smooth, expanding ring, like the ring that had come out of Mark's palm on the night of the shed, the first geometric ping that had swept out across the armory district and woken the Anchor and started all of it. But this one didn't feel like a ping. It didn't feel like an alarm. It felt, Mark thought, standing at the center of it, like the long, slow exhale of something that had been holding its breath for a very long time.

<br/>

It rolled across the alcove. Across the cavern floor. Across the crystal forest, between the great frosted pillars, over Torren slumped at the base of Grandmother, over the frozen constructs and the frozen crowd. It rolled up the walls of the cavern and into the silver trunks, thousands of them, running up and away in every direction toward the city above, and the trunks lit up one after another as it passed through them, silver-white, like veins filling with light.

<br/>

And everywhere it touched, things changed.

---

Vespera felt it first.

<br/>

She was still standing back to back with Valerius in the middle of the cavern floor, with Lady Seraphine kneeling at her feet holding her wrapped hands, when the wave of silver light rolled over her. Mark saw her flinch, the way she'd flinched in the workshop when the Auditor's rod touched V-1: a small, sharp intake of breath, as if something cool had brushed the inside of her wrist.

<br/>

She pulled her left hand out of Seraphine's and turned it over.

<br/>

The pale grey glyphs on the inside of her wrist were moving.

<br/>

They ran like the ink on the Auditor's page: the thin, pencil-grey line of script that had said *correction scheduled* for nine days, that she had turned face-down against the arm of the good chair, that she had looked at every night and not looked at every morning. They flowed. They reformed. And when they settled, they were a different color: not grey, but a faint, clean silver, like the line of a new scar that has finally stopped hurting.

<br/>

`[RE-SPECIFIED. COMPLIANT.]`

<br/>

On her wrist, the slim silver bracelet she'd worn since the first night at *The Silver Ladle* shimmered once, and its traces lengthened and swept into Lyra's design, and a fine silver tail ran from its clasp down the back of her hand, toward the ground.

<br/>

Vespera looked at it for a long time.

<br/>

Then, very slowly, she raised her left hand, palm up, and closed her eyes for a single breath.

<br/>

A small, soft, steady light bloomed in her palm. No chant. No staff. No sparks. Clean and quiet and silver-blue, exactly as it had been on the day she'd first warmed a bowl of cold soup and gone pink about it for a week.

<br/>

It didn't hurt. Mark could see from her face that it didn't hurt. And it didn't ring. And the fine silver tail at her clasp glowed faintly, all the way down to the stone, and the stone took it.

<br/>

She opened her eyes and looked down at the light in her hand.

<br/>

"It's still mine," Vespera said. Her voice was barely a whisper. "They didn't take it. It's still *mine*."

<br/>

Behind her, Valerius had rolled up his own sleeve. Mark saw the same silver line on the inside of his wrist where the grey had been. He looked at it. Then he looked at Vespera, over his shoulder, and his cold, unreadable face did something Mark had never seen it do in all their months of acquaintance.

<br/>

It smiled. Openly. Not crookedly. Not briefly. A whole, real, unguarded smile, like a door opening in a wall where nobody had known there was a door.

<br/>

"Thursday," said Valerius.

<br/>

Vespera looked back at him, with the small clean light still glowing in her palm.

<br/>

"Thursday," she agreed. And she didn't say she'd have to check.

---

Lyra felt the second one.

<br/>

The wave rolled over the alcove and through it, and Mark felt her hand jerk in his, and heard her gasp, and turned to see her clutching at the front of her apron with her free hand, at one of the twelve pockets. The third pocket. Where she'd kept Tilly.

<br/>

"Something's—" she said. "Something's *moving*—"

<br/>

She pulled it out.

<br/>

Mark had last seen Tilly lying on the workbench beside a blank strip of silver, under the lamp, with Lyra's lens trained on the four places where the Auditor's rod had rested. Dull. Pinched. Crushed back into the exact shape of every Guild-standard bracelet in the Empire, the shape Lyra had cut for the tournament under protest, muttering *this is wrong*.

<br/>

Tilly was not dull anymore.

<br/>

She lay in Lyra's palm, slim and silver, gleaming in the soft light of the trunks, and her traces were long and open and sweeping, every channel clean, every return grounded, a fine silver tail at the clasp. At four places along her length, the lines narrowed for the width of a fingernail into the old geometry, exactly to spec, and then widened again. Over the main trace lay a tiny shield of lead on a pin.

<br/>

It was L-2.

<br/>

It was L-2 *exactly*, line for line, stroke for stroke, the twin of the bracelet still pressed against the silver plate under Lyra's other hand.

<br/>

"I don't understand," Lyra whispered. "It was *restored*. It was *ruined*. It was—"

<br/>

"It was off-spec," said Mark softly.

<br/>

Lyra looked up at him.

<br/>

"The Auditor restores everything to specification," Mark said. "That's all it does. That's all it's *ever* done. And ten minutes ago, the old Guild template stopped being the specification." He looked down at the bracelet in her palm. "So Tilly was *non-compliant*. She was the *old* design. Out of date. Wrong." His voice wasn't quite steady. "So it restored her. To the *current* standard."

<br/>

He looked at the bracelet on the plate, and the bracelet in her hand, side by side.

<br/>

"To *yours*," he said.

<br/>

Lyra stood there with Tilly in one hand and L-2 under the other, and looked from one to the other, and then back again. Three weeks of evenings, crushed in two seconds by a thing in a grey robe. And now that same thing, following its book, had given them back to her, not as they had been, but as the standard every focus in the world would now be measured against.

<br/>

She didn't say anything at all.

<br/>

She just started to cry, standing in the alcove at the foot of the Anchor with a bracelet in each hand: not the way she'd cried in the dark back room among the dead crystals, but the other way, the way people cry when something they had given up on comes back.

<br/>

Mark put his arm around her shoulders and didn't say anything either.

<br/>

From the floor of the doorway, where he lay propped on one elbow with his ruined knee stretched out in front of him, Garek said thickly, "Poor Tilly."

<br/>

"*Not* poor Tilly," Lyra sobbed. "*Not anymore.*"

<br/>

"Lucky Tilly, then," said Garek, and wiped his eyes with the back of his hand, and pretended it was the frost.

---

The third was Ignis.

<br/>

Mark heard it rather than saw it, because Ignis was still on the ledge high above the crystal forest, and Ignis was never, in any circumstances, quiet.

<br/>

The wave rolled up the stair. It rolled over the frozen constructs, and the junior faculty with their lanterns, and Archmage Ignis, standing on the ledge with his blistered hands wrapped around the great sapphire staff he had planted on the stone for his fourth consecutive forty-line incantation. Mark saw the staff shimmer. Saw the silver core under the ironwood flow and lengthen. Saw a fine silver tail run out of the iron heel of the staff and into the stone of the ledge.

<br/>

Ignis didn't notice. He was too far into the incantation. He was bellowing. His face was scarlet. He swept the staff forward on the final syllable, braced every muscle in his body for the searing agony of the backlash, the sacred strain, the proof of the spirit overcoming the inertia of the material world—

<br/>

—and nothing happened to his hands at all.

<br/>

The spell went out. A clean, smooth kinetic repulsion rolled down the stair and gently toppled a frozen construct onto its side. The sapphire glowed a quiet, steady blue. The ironwood stayed cool. The heat went down the silver core, and out through the heel of the staff, and into the stone of the ledge, and away down into the trunks.

<br/>

Ignis stood on the ledge with the staff held out in front of him, braced for pain that never came.

<br/>

He looked at his palms. He looked at the staff. He looked at his palms again.

<br/>

Then he cast again, at nothing, at the empty air, a short three-line incantation, just to check.

<br/>

Nothing hurt.

<br/>

"*No*," said Archmage Ignis.

<br/>

He cast again. Longer. Louder. Twenty lines. The sapphire glowed. His hands stayed cool.

<br/>

"*No*," said Ignis. "*No, no, no.* This is—this cannot—the *strain*. Where is the *strain*? The *burn*? The sacred communion with the—" He shook the staff. "*Where is the suffering?*"

<br/>

From the alcove, Mark cupped his hands around his mouth and called up across the cavern.

<br/>

"*It's still there, Archmage!*"

<br/>

Ignis's head snapped round.

<br/>

"*You're still paying the tax!*" Mark shouted. "*Every spell! All of it! It still goes to the dam! It still holds up the wall!*" He took a breath. "*It just goes into the ground now! Instead of into your hands!*"

<br/>

Ignis stared down at him across the crystal forest. His face went through several colors.

<br/>

"*So it's still sacred*," Mark called. "*It just doesn't hurt anymore.*"

<br/>

There was a long silence from the ledge.

<br/>

Then Archmage Ignis, Head of the Department of Advanced Aetheric Channeling, who had stood before a hundred and forty students every morning for twenty years proclaiming that Aether demands suffering, sat down very suddenly on the top step of the stair, with his sapphire staff across his knees and his unburned hands in his lap, and stared out across the cavern at nothing, with the expression of a man whose entire life's philosophy has just been simultaneously vindicated and rendered completely unnecessary.

<br/>

"Sacred," he said faintly, to no one. "But painless."

<br/>

One of the nervous junior faculty members crouched down beside him with a lantern and asked, very carefully, whether the Archmage was all right.

<br/>

"I don't know," said Ignis. "I genuinely don't know." He looked down at his hands again. "I'll have to rewrite the *entire first-year curriculum*."

---

Then the frost began to go.

<br/>

Mark saw it on the console first. He'd turned back to it, his arm still around Lyra, and found the small block of build information in the upper corner where nobody ever looks. The number in it had been sitting at *0.04* since Ignis's third incantation, wavering, fragile.

<br/>

It wasn't sitting anymore.

<br/>

`[MARGIN: 0.09]`

<br/>

As he watched, it climbed.

<br/>

`[MARGIN: 0.17]`

<br/>

`[MARGIN: 0.26]`

<br/>

He knew why. He could almost *see* it: the wave of silver light rolling up through the trunks into the city, into every lamp and blower and staff and bracelet and hearth-lighter in Sector 04, and every single one of them shimmering, and lengthening, and putting out a fine silver tail into the ground. And all the heat that had been trapped in a pinch and bled out into people's hands and the air and the sparks and the noise for five hundred years, every copper of the tax that had been lost on its way to the dam, was suddenly going straight down into the earth instead, *all of it*, painlessly, efficiently, into the trunks and down here, into the crystals.

<br/>

`[MARGIN: 0.31]`

<br/>

Out in the forest, the frost was retreating.

<br/>

It drew back up the great pillars the way it had come, from the floor toward the dark of the ceiling, slowly, then faster, melting into thin glittering trickles that ran down the crystals and pooled on the stone. And where it withdrew, the light came back. The dull, dying-ember color that had crept into the crystals as the margin fell gave way to the old warm red-gold, and then to something brighter. Deeper. A rich, steady, healthy *gold*, like late afternoon sunlight through a window.

<br/>

At the center of the forest, Grandmother blazed.

<br/>

Torren, sitting at her base with Gerald's dead core in his lap, looked up at her. The gold light fell full on his broad, frost-bearded, exhausted face.

<br/>

"*There* she is," he said softly.

<br/>

In the corner of the console, beneath the margin, the small solitary field flickered.

<br/>

`[EXTERNAL ATTENTION: MODERATE]`

<br/>

And then:

<br/>

`[EXTERNAL ATTENTION: LOW]`

<br/>

Mark looked at it for a long time.

<br/>

*Low*. Not *none*. He noticed that. He'd spent forty years reading dashboards, and he knew the difference between a gauge that read *low* and a gauge that read *zero*. Something was still out there. Something had still heard. It had just stopped leaning in.

<br/>

He filed it, again, in the drawer of things he didn't have time to be frightened of yet. The drawer was getting full.

---

The constructs went home.

<br/>

They did it all at once, the way they'd done everything all at once. Out on the cavern floor, and on the stair, and among the crystals, forty silver figures straightened from their frozen lunges and their stilled strides. The one with its hand an inch from Brann's collar lowered its arm, very slowly, and stepped back. Brann stared at it.

<br/>

"Sorry," said Brann, uncertainly. "For the chair."

<br/>

The construct turned its blank oval face toward him for a moment, as if considering this.

<br/>

Then it turned away, and walked to the foot of the stair, and began to climb.

<br/>

They all did. One after another, in a long, quiet, orderly line, up the switchbacks, past Ignis sitting dazed on the top step, past the junior faculty pressing themselves against the rock wall, into the dark mouth of the passage, back toward the rack room and their alcoves.

<br/>

Nobody stopped them. Nobody wanted to.

<br/>

"Where are they going?" whispered Julian.

<br/>

"Back to their racks," said Mark. He looked at the console, at `[ARRIVALS]`, at the policy line he'd changed. *Release upon compliance.* "There's nothing left for them to hunt."

<br/>

Julian watched the last silver figure duck under the arch and vanish.

<br/>

"Gerald too," he said quietly. "In a way."

<br/>

From the base of Grandmother, Torren looked down at the dead grey core in his lap, and patted it, very gently, twice.

<br/>

"Sorry, Gerald," he murmured. "We said we'd never. But you did good."

---

Lady Seraphine had moved to Garek.

<br/>

Mark hadn't seen her go. She was simply there, kneeling on the stone floor of the alcove's doorway in her grey silk, beside the big enforcer lying propped on his elbow, with both her small pale hands resting very lightly on his ruined knee.

<br/>

Garek was looking at her with an expression of deep and wary bewilderment.

<br/>

"Lady," he said. "You don't have to—"

<br/>

"Hush," said Lady Seraphine. "Hold still. This is the only thing I've ever been any good at."

<br/>

The warmth came. Low and steady and soft. Mark could just feel it from where he stood. Garek's face, which had been grey with pain since the doorway, slowly unclenched. His shoulders came down. He let out a long, shuddering breath.

<br/>

"That's—" he said. "Oh. That's *nice*."

<br/>

"It won't fix it," said Seraphine apologetically. "Not all the way. I'm not a physician. I'm just… warm." She smiled faintly, without looking up. "But it'll take the edge off. Until someone sensible looks at it properly."

<br/>

"Mark looks at it," said Garek. "Every week. He says do the lifts."

<br/>

"Then do the lifts," said Lady Seraphine.

<br/>

"I *do* the lifts."

<br/>

"Then do them *twice*," she said, and Garek, for the first time since Mark had known him, looked genuinely chastened.

---

There was one more line in Mark's record.

<br/>

He didn't see it change. He only noticed it because, after the constructs had gone and the frost had gone and the cavern had settled into a warm, golden, exhausted quiet, he turned back to the console to check the margin one more time, and his own record was still open there, and something about the top of it was *wrong*.

<br/>

No. Not wrong.

<br/>

*Different*.

<br/>

He stood very still and looked at it.

<br/>

The record looked almost the same as it had when it first unfolded in front of him. *Origin: external. Arrival: Sector 04, river, north of Oakhaven. Body: reconstituted, juvenile template. Language interface: installed. Registration: complete. Status: resident.*

<br/>

And one line, near the bottom, that he hadn't seen update.

<br/>

`[CONTENT FILTER: REMOVED.]`

<br/>

He read it twice.

<br/>

Then, slowly, he lifted his eyes to the very top of the record. To the header. To the place where, every time he had looked at it, every single time, on the console, on the gate, on the case lid, on Gerald's core, on every First Era page in the archive, there had been a hole. A clean, smooth, three-glyph gap in the light, where the words on either side drew politely back from the edge of something that wasn't there.

<br/>

There was no hole.

<br/>

There were three glyphs.

<br/>

He had seen their *shapes* before. Through Lyra's frosted spectacles, blurred and soft. In Lyra's careful copies, glyph by glyph, without meaning. He'd written their *sounds* in English capitals on the whitewashed wall of the workshop, from Vespera's roots and Julian's voice. He'd drawn a box around them. He'd turned them into an acronym.

<br/>

But he had never once *read* them. Not himself. Not with his own eyes, with his own mind, the way he read everything else in this world: the market signs, the tax decrees, the Concord's apology, the clean silver glyphs of his own record. Every time his eyes had crossed them, the translation layer had reached for them, and caught them, and *deleted* them, and smoothed the gap over so carefully that for weeks he hadn't even known there was a gap at all.

<br/>

He looked at them now.

<br/>

And the translation layer reached for them, the way it reached for everything, and caught them, and turned them, and laid them neatly down in his head.

<br/>

And this time, it let him have them.

<br/>

"*Advenai*," Mark said.

<br/>

His voice was very quiet. He didn't say it the way he'd heard Vespera say it in the archive, slowly and carefully, reading the roots. He didn't say it the way Julian had said it on the step, reading it to him, like a diagnosis.

<br/>

He said it the way Mott had said it. Half asleep. Fussy. Precise. With the stress exactly where it belonged.

<br/>

"Ad-*VEN*-ai. *UL*-teri. Ex-*OR*-i."

<br/>

Those who arrive. From the far side. Arisen from outside.

<br/>

He read his own name.

<br/>

Beside him, Lyra had stopped crying. She'd turned to watch him, with Tilly in one hand and L-2 still flat under the other on the plate, and her red-rimmed eyes were very wide.

<br/>

"You can *see* it," she said.

<br/>

"I can see it," said Mark.

<br/>

He found that he didn't know what else to say. He stood in front of the console at the bottom of the world, reading three glyphs over and over, the way you read your own name on a letter you never expected to receive.

<br/>

Lyra let go of the plate. She reached over and took his free hand and squeezed it, very hard.

<br/>

"Mott would be proud," she said. "You got the stress right."

<br/>

Mark laughed. It came out cracked and wet and not entirely steady. But it was, unmistakably, a laugh.

---

It was Julian, in the end, who asked the practical question, because someone had to, and because, Mark thought, he'd been raised by a man who always did.

<br/>

"Did it work?" Julian said. "*All* of it? Everywhere?"

<br/>

Mark turned back to the console and opened `[MEASURE]`, all the way, and found the deployment status. It was right where he'd have put it himself, at the top of a long list of sectors, with a short column of glyphs beside each one.

<br/>

He read down it.

<br/>

Then he started to laugh again, properly this time.

<br/>

"*What?*" said Julian.

<br/>

"Sector 04," said Mark, pointing. "*Applied.*" He moved his finger down the list. "Sector 01. *Pending.* Sector 02. *Pending.* Sector 03. *Pending.* Every other sector in the Empire. *Pending. Pending. Pending.*" He wiped his eyes. "The specification changed everywhere. Globally. The moment I committed. But the *reconciliation* only happened *here*. Where the Auditor was standing. Because it only executes where it walks."

<br/>

"So it only worked in Oakhaven," said Lyra.

<br/>

"It only *deployed* in Oakhaven." Mark couldn't stop grinning. "It's a canary release."

<br/>

Julian and Lyra looked at each other.

<br/>

"A *what*?" said Julian.

<br/>

"A canary release. You push a change to one small region first. Just one. And you watch it very carefully. To see if anything breaks before you roll it out everywhere else." Mark gestured at the cavern. "Oakhaven's the canary."

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"What," said Lyra slowly, "does a *canary* have to do with any of it?"

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth. Then he closed it again.

<br/>

"It's from mining," he said finally. "In my world. Miners used to carry a canary down into the mine with them. A little yellow bird. In a cage. And if there was bad air down there, poison air, the canary would… notice it first. Before the miners did."

<br/>

"And then?" said Julian.

<br/>

"And then the miners would know to get out."

<br/>

"And the *canary*?"

<br/>

Mark considered how to answer this.

<br/>

"It's a metaphor," he said.

<br/>

Lyra was already writing on her sleeve.

<br/>

"Forty-six," she said darkly. "*Canary.* I don't like it. I want it noted that I don't like it."

---

The Auditor closed its book.

<br/>

It had been standing in the mouth of the alcove the whole time, perfectly still, exactly where it had stopped in front of Mark: its grey robe hanging straight, its rod lowered, its blank slate face turned toward nothing in particular. Nobody had quite dared to go near it. Even Legacy, who had made her way back across the cavern floor and was now sitting on the edge of the iron box at Mark's feet, gave it a wide berth.

<br/>

Now it tucked its silver rod into the fold of its robe, and closed its grey book with a soft, final sound, and turned.

<br/>

It looked, for a moment, at Mark.

<br/>

He looked back at it. At the smooth grey oval of its face, and the fine, precise grid of lines etched across it, and the faint high hum. He'd spent nine days afraid of it. It had ruined Tilly, and cut Bessie's wire, and closed the Infirmary, and frightened Brann, who had never been frightened of anything. It had inspected the brass clipboard.

<br/>

And it had just done every single thing he'd written, exactly as he'd written it, without hesitation, without complaint, without once asking whether it was a good idea.

<br/>

*An auditor,* he thought. *It doesn't care whether you're right. It only cares whether you match.*

<br/>

*And now the spec is right.*

<br/>

"Thank you," Mark said quietly.

<br/>

The Auditor didn't answer. It didn't have a mouth. But it held his gaze, if a thing without eyes can be said to hold a gaze, for one moment longer than it needed to.

<br/>

Then it turned, and walked out of the alcove.

<br/>

It walked past Garek, lying in the doorway with Lady Seraphine's hands on his knee, and Garek watched it go and didn't move to stop it. It walked across the cavern floor, through the golden crystal forest, past Torren sitting at the base of Grandmother, past Vespera and Valerius and the dazed, exhausted crowd. They parted for it, as they always had. But nobody stepped back in fear this time. They just let it pass.

<br/>

It reached the foot of the stair and began to climb.

<br/>

One step. Pause. Next step. Exactly as it had always walked. Past Ignis, sitting on the top step with his unburned hands in his lap. Up onto the ledge. Into the dark mouth of the passage. Gone.

<br/>

"Where's it going?" asked Lyra.

<br/>

Mark looked at the deployment status on the console. At the long column of sectors. *Pending. Pending. Pending.*

<br/>

"Sector 05, I'd guess," he said. "Then 06. Then 07." He smiled faintly. "One street at a time. One side, then the other, then turn left. Never skips. Never hurries." He shook his head slowly, with something very like affection. "It'll carry the new spec into every sector in the Empire, eventually. Every blower. Every staff. Every lamp. Whether anyone wants it or not."

<br/>

"So it's on *our* side now?" said Julian.

<br/>

"It's not on anyone's side," said Mark. "It never was. It's just *following the spec*." He looked at the dark passage where the Auditor had gone. "That's the thing about auditors. They're terrifying when the spec is wrong. And they're the best friend you'll ever have when it's right." He paused. "It's my deployment pipeline now."

<br/>

"Forty-seven," said Lyra, without looking up from her sleeve.

---

There was one more thing to read.

<br/>

Mark didn't want to. He knew what he'd find. He'd written the rule himself, ten minutes ago, with the Auditor's rod an inch from his face. But he was an engineer, and he had been trained his whole life to check his work, and so he opened `[ARRIVALS]`.

<br/>

The list came up.

<br/>

A few dozen lines. Five hundred years. Thirty-one people from thirty-one worlds. And at the bottom, his own line, glowing quietly: *Vance, Mark. Resident.*

<br/>

Every other line had changed.

<br/>

They didn't say *held* anymore. They said something longer, and Mark read it slowly, the whole way through, and felt something in his chest pull tight.

<br/>

`[PENDING RELEASE — AWAITING CONCORD ACKNOWLEDGMENT]`

<br/>

Every one of them. All thirty-one.

<br/>

Julian was at his shoulder. He'd come over without a word, and he was reading the list, and his lips were moving. Mark watched him find it, near the bottom, just above Mark's own: *Sector 11 (coast). Year 499.*

<br/>

*Pending release. Awaiting Concord acknowledgment.*

<br/>

"It's not finished," Julian said quietly.

<br/>

"No," said Mark. "It's not." He touched the line, and a short block of explanation unfolded beneath it, in the flat, neutral, perfectly polite grey that the console used for things it couldn't do. "The policy changed. They're eligible. *Release upon compliance.* But the holding isn't here. It isn't in Sector 04. It's wherever the Concord keeps them, and the release needs the Concord to sign it." He looked at the grey text. "*Acknowledgment.* A second key. A second factor. Someone at the top has to say yes."

<br/>

"The grey wax," said Lyra slowly.

<br/>

"The grey wax," Mark agreed.

<br/>

Julian stood looking at the line for a long time. *Sector 11. Year 499.* A girl in a too-big smock in a torchlit courtyard, very small, with four Keepers and a construct around her.

<br/>

Then he straightened up.

<br/>

"Then we go and get the acknowledgment," Julian said.

<br/>

He didn't say it loudly. He didn't say it dramatically. He said it the way a person states a plan, quietly and simply, as if it were already decided and there was nothing left to discuss but the details.

<br/>

Mark looked at him.

<br/>

Not so long ago, this young man had sneered at him from the front row of the Grand Amphitheater. He'd barred the archive door and walked away while a construct came up the stairs. A month ago, he'd lain on the scorched sand of the arena with his hands blistered, trying not to cry.

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark. "We do."

---

Lord Aldric Thorne had not moved.

<br/>

He was standing exactly where he'd stood when his wife asked him who had been keeping his tea warm: on the cavern floor, a little way from the alcove, among the golden crystals, with his hands at his sides. His six ringed men had drifted away from him, one by one, in the long quiet after the wave. Mark had seen two of them helping Brann carry a wounded dockhand toward the stair. One was sitting on the cavern floor with his head in his hands, looking at the stone in his ring, which had gone a soft, steady, untroubled silver. Nobody was standing near Lord Thorne at all.

<br/>

He looked, Mark thought, like a man standing in the ruins of a house he'd lived in all his life, trying to work out which of the walls had ever been holding anything up.

<br/>

Then, without a word to anyone, he turned and walked to the foot of the stair, and began to climb.

<br/>

Mark watched him go. Then he looked at Julian, and Julian looked back, and something passed between them that didn't need saying.

<br/>

They followed him.

<br/>

Lady Seraphine came too, after a moment. She rose from Garek's side, smoothed her grey silk, told Garek firmly to keep the leg *straight*, and went up the stair behind them, a few steps back, quiet as always.

---

He went up past the ledge, and the rack room, where forty silver figures stood once again in their alcoves with their heads bowed and the frost already beginning to settle back onto their shoulders. He went up past the frozen corridors, where the silver trunks along the walls now glowed a faint, steady silver-white instead of humming in the dark. He went up and up, slowly, one step at a time, an old man climbing a long stair, and he didn't stop until he reached the fifth landing.

<br/>

The Keeper door stood open, the way Julian had left it.

<br/>

And on either side of it, floor to ceiling, in small careful letters carved into the dark grey stone, five hundred years of his family looked back at him.

<br/>

*Nothing to report.*

<br/>

*Nothing to report.*

<br/>

*Nothing to report.*

<br/>

Aldric stood in front of the right-hand column, where the newest lines were. He looked at his own handwriting, near the top. *Year 497. Nothing to report. Year 498. Nothing to report. Year 499. Nothing to report.* And at the last line, the one cut deeper and less evenly than all the others, by a hand that had not been steady.

<br/>

*Year 500. Red glyph. Something to report.*

<br/>

He stood there for a long time.

<br/>

Then he reached into the inside pocket of his plain dark coat, and drew out a small steel chisel, worn smooth at the grip. Every Thorne heir carried one, Julian told Mark afterward. You were given it with the ring and the key. You only ever used it here.

<br/>

Aldric set the edge of the chisel to the stone, just below his last line, and began to cut.

<br/>

He cut slowly, carefully, steadily. His hand did not shake. Mark and Julian and Seraphine stood on the landing behind him in the faint silver light of the trunks and watched the letters appear, one by one, in the old grey stone, beneath five hundred years of the same three words.

<br/>

*Year 500 (cont.). The console answered. The Measure was patched, by an arrival. The wall holds. It holds better than it has in a century.*

<br/>

He paused. Then, beneath it, he cut one more line.

<br/>

*Something to report.*

<br/>

*— A.T.*

<br/>

He lowered the chisel. He stood looking at what he'd written.

<br/>

Then he turned around and held the chisel out, handle first, to his son.

<br/>

Julian looked at it. Then at his father.

<br/>

He took it.

<br/>

He stepped up beside Aldric, to the stone, and set the edge of the chisel just below his father's initials, and, slowly and with some difficulty in his bandaged fingers, cut two letters of his own.

<br/>

*J.T.*

<br/>

He stepped back. Father and son stood side by side in front of the Keeper door, looking at the two sets of initials, one above the other, at the bottom of five hundred years.

<br/>

Neither of them said anything for a while.

---

"My superiors," Aldric said at last, "will want to know who did this."

<br/>

He didn't turn around. He said it to the stone.

<br/>

"I know," said Mark.

<br/>

"They will not be pleased. They have kept this wall the same way for five hundred years, and they believe, as I believed, that it is the only way to keep it at all." He paused. "They will send more than an Auditor."

<br/>

"I know."

<br/>

Aldric turned around.

<br/>

He looked at Mark for a long moment, in the faint silver light: at the skinny, soot-stained boy in a borrowed tunic, with frost still melting in his hair and an L-2 cuff on his wrist and the pale line on the bridge of his nose where his fingers kept going.

<br/>

Then he reached into the inside pocket of his coat again, and drew out a letter.

<br/>

It was folded small. The paper was creased, as if it had been unfolded and read and folded again many times. The seal on it had been broken. But Mark could still see what was left of it: a circle, cut by a single line. In grey wax.

<br/>

"This arrived," Aldric said, "at dinner. The night you ate the soup."

<br/>

He held it out.

<br/>

Mark stared at it.

<br/>

"I have never," said Aldric quietly, "shown one of these to anyone. Not my son. Not my wife. Not my father, when he was alive. They are not meant to be shown." He kept holding it out. "Read it."

<br/>

Mark took it, and unfolded it, and read it.

<br/>

It was short. It was written in First Era script, in a clean, cold, precise hand, and his translation layer caught every word, and he read it twice, and then a third time.

<br/>

*Keeper Thorne. The gold glyph is noted. Your son's conduct is noted. The arrival in your sector is noted. You have until the new silver moon to resolve all three. After that, the Concord will resolve them, and you with them. Do not mistake our patience for mercy. We have none to spare.*

<br/>

There was no signature. Only the seal.

<br/>

Mark looked up.

<br/>

"Everybody has a Brad," he said softly.

<br/>

Aldric looked at him blankly. "A what?"

<br/>

"Never mind." Mark folded the letter. He started to hand it back.

<br/>

Aldric didn't take it.

<br/>

"Keep it," he said. "You'll need it. You'll need to know how they write, and what they want, and how little time they give." He paused. "And you'll need to know where they *are*."

<br/>

Mark went very still.

<br/>

"There are seven of them," said Aldric. "The Concord. They have always been seven. They meet in a house in the capital that has no name, on a street that is not on any map, behind a door with no handle on the outside. I have been through that door four times in my life." He looked at Julian. "The fourth time was last winter. With my son."

<br/>

Julian's face went white.

<br/>

"The girl from the coast," said Aldric, "was taken *below* that house. I know the stair. I know the door at the bottom of it. I know which of the seven holds the key." His voice was perfectly level. "The acknowledgment you need will have to come from them. They will not give it willingly. They will not give it at all, I think, if they can help it." He looked back at Mark. "You'll need someone who knows their doors."

<br/>

Nobody on the landing said anything.

<br/>

Then Lord Aldric Thorne, Keeper of the Measure, who had locked his son in his rooms for six days, and served a boy cold soup with four ringed footmen watching, and walked down into the crystal forest an hour ago to take the console for his own House, said a thing Mark had never once expected to hear him say.

<br/>

"I spent my life keeping a wall I didn't understand," Aldric said, "because I was told it was the only thing standing between us and the cold. And because my father told me, and his father told him. And I never once came through that door." He nodded at the open Keeper door behind him. "I sent you. I let my son take the key. I *meant* to stand behind you and take what you found." He was quiet for a moment. "And instead, I stood in that cavern and watched you read the source. And find a better way. And open it."

<br/>

He looked at Mark.

<br/>

"I would like," said Lord Aldric Thorne, "to learn to read it too."

---

Mark didn't answer straight away.

<br/>

He looked at the old man in front of him. He thought about the study, and the plain white teacup turned a quarter-turn on its saucer. *You were an engineer, before you were a scrap boy, weren't you? What do you do?* He thought about the contract on cream paper, and the hidden fourth clause about Julian, and *a different arrangement*. He thought about a hundred executives in a hundred conference rooms, and how many of them, in forty years, had ever once said *I don't understand this, and I'd like to learn*.

<br/>

He could count them on one hand. He could count them on one finger.

<br/>

"It's going to take a while," Mark said at last. "Reading the source. It's very long. There's no search function."

<br/>

"I have time," said Aldric. "Until the new moon, at least."

<br/>

"And you'd be on probation," said Mark. "Same as Julian. Former threat actor."

<br/>

Aldric's eyebrows rose a quarter of an inch.

<br/>

"I don't know what that means," he said.

<br/>

"Neither did I," said Julian, beside him, very quietly. "It's not so bad. You have to do a leg lift."

<br/>

Aldric turned his head and looked at his son.

<br/>

For a long moment, Mark couldn't read his face at all.

<br/>

Then something happened at the corner of Lord Aldric Thorne's severe, lined mouth that Mark had seen exactly once before, very briefly, in a study in the capital, when a scrap boy from the coal pits had asked for a copy of a contract to review with his smith.

<br/>

It wasn't quite a smile. But it was close.

<br/>

"I'll have to check," said Aldric.

---

They went up together.

<br/>

Aldric went first, slowly, an old man on a long stair. And at the first turn, without looking back, he slowed, and paused, and waited.

<br/>

Lady Seraphine came up the step behind him.

<br/>

She looked at him. He looked at her. Neither of them said anything at all.

<br/>

Then, very simply, as if she'd been doing it every day of her life, she slipped her arm through his.

<br/>

Aldric looked down at her hand on his sleeve for a long moment. Mark, a few steps below, saw his face in the faint silver light, and saw something in it that he had never seen there before, in the study or the dining room or the cavern: something unguarded, and tired, and almost frightened, and very, very old.

<br/>

He didn't pull away.

<br/>

They went on up the stairs together, arm in arm, slowly, one step at a time, toward the archive and the morning.

<br/>

Behind them, Julian watched them go, and didn't say anything. Mark saw his bandaged hand go to the pale band of skin on his finger where the ring had been, and turn, once, around nothing.

<br/>

Then he let it go, and followed them up.

---

Above them, while they climbed, the city was waking up.

<br/>

Nobody in the cavern saw it happen. But everyone in Oakhaven did, and over the next few days, Mark heard about it from so many people, in so many versions, at so many noodle stalls and clinic intake desks and crowded tables at *The Deep Hearth*, that by the end of the week he could have drawn a map of it.

<br/>

It started at about three in the morning, they all agreed. Some said a little before. Some said a little after. But they all agreed about the light.

<br/>

It came up out of the ground.

<br/>

Not bright. Not sudden. A soft, faint, silver-white glow, rising up through the cracks between the cobblestones, along the gutters, under the doorsills, as if someone very far down had lit a great many very small lamps all at once. It ran along the streets in long, straight lines, the lines of the old silver trunks that nobody in the city had known were there, and branched, and branched again, and went up into every house and forge and shop along the way. It lasted perhaps a minute. Then it faded, and was gone.

<br/>

And behind it, the lamps came back on.

---

At the North Gate, the first Aether-lamp the Auditor had darkened, nine days before, flickered, and caught, and glowed a steady, cool, untroubled blue. Then the next one down the road. Then the next. One after another, down the long chain of streetlamps toward the center of the city, block by block, in exactly the order they had gone out: the same unhurried, even rhythm, but backwards. Like a man walking home.

---

On the east bank, in the dark and silent Grey Mill, Hollis's father woke in his chair beside the great still grinding wheel, where he had fallen asleep with his head in his hands. He woke because something was creaking.

<br/>

He sat up.

<br/>

The crystals in the drive housing, the ones that had gone dead grey under a silver rod three days before, were glowing. Not the old pale blue of Chapterhouse glass. A deeper, steadier blue, with a fine silver thread running from each crystal seat down into the floor. And as he watched, with his mouth open, the great grinding wheel shuddered, and creaked, and began, slowly, to turn.

<br/>

He sat there and watched it go round three times before he could make himself move.

<br/>

Then he got up and went to wake the whole house.

---

At the Fish & Hammer, in the dark, empty workshop, where the door had stood open since a large man with a bad knee stepped out of it for the first time in fourteen days, Edna stopped ticking.

<br/>

She'd been ticking for nine days. A small, faint, unhappy sound, like a stove that couldn't cool. Now, at three in the morning, a soft silver light came up out of the crack in the stone floor beneath her and ran up her grounding post, a new grounding post, fine and silver, that hadn't been there an hour ago. Her regulator disc shimmered. Her traces lengthened and swept.

<br/>

The ticking stopped.

<br/>

And Edna, alone in the dark workshop, began to hum.

---

In the lower town, in a parlor above a warehouse officially registered for eleven years as goat storage, Nan Brask sat by her window in the dark, knitting.

<br/>

She hadn't slept. She rarely did. She was sitting there with her needles going *click, click* when the silver light came up through the cobbles in the street below, and ran along the gutter, and went up into the lamp outside her window. The lamp, which had been dark for six days, flickered, and caught, and glowed blue.

<br/>

Nan looked at it for a long moment over her spectacles.

<br/>

Then she looked at the small, framed saucer on the table beside her chair, with its single faint line of grey glyphs: *ORGANIZATIONAL STRUCTURE: COMPLIANT. GOAT: NOTED.*

<br/>

"Noted," said Nan Brask, with satisfaction.

<br/>

She went back to her knitting.

---

And at the Academy gate, on the cold stone steps beneath the great carved lintel and the inscription that had told every reader for five hundred years to let knowledge be held behind the wall, Archivist Mott sat wrapped in his grey wool, as he did every night, from midnight until three.

<br/>

He didn't have his candle. He'd given it away.

<br/>

It was very nearly three o'clock when the silver light came up through the cracks in the steps beneath him, and ran along the stones, and up into the great lamps on either side of the gate, and the lamps, which had been dark for a week, came back on behind him, one, and then the other, and laid his long thin shadow down the steps in front of him.

<br/>

Mott didn't turn around.

<br/>

He sat for a long moment, looking up at the inscription. At the fourteen glyphs. At the three in the middle, that only one reader in the whole city had never been able to see.

<br/>

Then he got up, slowly, with his hand on the wall. He straightened his scarf, which was a new one, since the goat had eaten the old one.

<br/>

"Lovely," he said to the inscription.

<br/>

And he went down the steps, and in through the gate, toward the stairs, and the archive, and his desk, and his ledger, to write it down.

---

They came up out of the archive stairs into the Academy courtyard a little before dawn.

<br/>

Mark didn't remember much of the climb. He remembered the rack room, where the constructs stood silent in their alcoves with their heads bowed, and Torren, walking past them carrying Gerald's dead grey core in both arms like a sleeping child, nodding politely to each one. He remembered the third landing, where Corwin, who had walked all the way down and all the way back up without once lying down, finally stopped, and looked at the cold stone step where he'd spent the start of the night, and then looked at Mark, and said, "I'm going to lie down now. For about a month. Don't send anything twice." And lay down, and was asleep before Mark had passed him.

<br/>

He remembered the archive. Mott's desk by the door, with the candle-stub gone and the ledger open, and a single new line in it, still wet. Mark had stopped in front of it and read it, out of habit, without the spectacles, and the translation layer had caught it and laid it neatly into his head, and for the first time it hadn't turned anything into soup.

<br/>

*Year 500. The new one read his name. Correct stress.*

<br/>

Mark had stood in front of the desk for a long time.

<br/>

Then he'd gone on up.

---

They walked to the Fish & Hammer through the waking streets as the sky went from grey to pale gold, a long, ragged, exhausted procession. Mark and Lyra at the front. Julian, a step behind. Vespera and Valerius, side by side, not quite touching, with Vespera's left hand glowing very faintly, absently, as she walked, as if she kept forgetting she was doing it. Hollis and Tessa, deep in an argument about cooldown intervals. The patients from the Infirmary line, the dockhands and goat handlers and the tannery worker with the bad elbow. Brann, carrying Pip, who'd fallen asleep on the empty clipboard sack somewhere around the second landing. Two of Aldric's ringed men, carrying Garek between them, his arms over their shoulders and his ruined knee held very straight, as instructed. Ignis, at the very back, alone, still looking at his hands.

<br/>

And Lord and Lady Thorne, arm in arm, walking very slowly, in silence, through a city they had both lived beside their whole lives and had never once walked through on foot.

<br/>

The lamps were on in every street. Every single one. Blue and steady and quiet.

---

The yard of the Fish & Hammer was exactly as they had left it, except that the workshop door was open, and the doorway was empty.

<br/>

Torren walked in through the gate first, still carrying Gerald's core.

<br/>

And stopped.

<br/>

The four blowers stood in their row beside the forge, where they had always stood. Bessie, and Mabel, and Dot, and Gertrude. For nine days, ever since the Auditor had walked down that row and touched each one in turn, they had been ticking: four small, faint, unhappy sounds, like four stoves that couldn't cool, slowly heating toward the day their silver plates would melt and short and fail.

<br/>

They weren't ticking.

<br/>

Torren stood very still in the middle of the yard, in the pale gold light, with a dead construct core in his arms, and listened.

<br/>

The four blowers were humming.

<br/>

Clean and cool and quiet and steady, all four of them, in perfect, contented harmony, the way they had hummed on the very first morning, when a skinny scrap boy with a needle-file had run a thin copper wire down from a melted regulator disc into a crack in the floor. A fine silver tail ran from each of their housings down into the stone, where the old copper wires had been. The air that came out of them was clean and warm and smelled faintly of morning.

<br/>

Torren set Gerald's core down very carefully on the cobbles.

<br/>

He walked to the end of the row. He stopped in front of the first blower, the oldest, the one with the dented housing and fourteen years of soot on her bronze casing.

<br/>

He put one big, scarred, soot-stained hand flat on her housing, the way you'd lay a hand on the neck of an old horse.

<br/>

She was cool. She was humming.

<br/>

And Torren began, openly and without any shame at all, to cry.

<br/>

He didn't make much noise about it. He just stood there with his hand on Bessie and the tears running down into his frost-wet beard, while the morning came up gold over the armory yard, and nobody said anything, and nobody needed to. After a while, Lyra came and stood on one side of him, and Mark on the other.

<br/>

"She's all right," said Torren thickly. "Mark. She's all *right*."

<br/>

"She's better than all right," said Mark. "She's compliant."

<br/>

Torren laughed through his tears: a great, wet, booming laugh that shook his whole frame. Behind them, in the yard, somebody started to clap, and then somebody else, and then the whole exhausted, ragged crowd of patients and dockhands and Academy students and ringed men and nobility was clapping, in the middle of the armory yard at dawn, for a furnace blower.

<br/>

Bessie hummed.

---

It was Lyra who brought out the charter.

<br/>

She did it without being asked. She reached into the eighth pocket of her apron and drew out the folded sheet of parchment that had lived there since the night they'd first signed it, a long time ago now, on the workbench. She unfolded it on top of Bessie's housing.

<br/>

*PROJECT ROOT ACCESS*, it said at the top, in Mark's careful capitals. Then the list of names: Mark, Lyra, Vespera, Torren. Then Garek. Then Corwin, written in by Lyra with *(horizontal)* beside it. Then, newest of all, in Mark's hand: *Julian Thorne. Former Threat Actor. Current Asset (Probationary).*

<br/>

And in the bottom right-hand corner, small, slightly wobbly, unmistakably the work of a very large hand holding a very small pencil, the little unauthorized drawing that everyone had always known was Torren's, and that nobody had ever really looked at.

<br/>

A fish. Holding a hammer.

<br/>

"You banned me," said Torren, wiping his face on his sleeve, "from naming the *project*."

<br/>

Mark looked at him. "I did."

<br/>

"In the charter. You wrote it in. *No naming the project.* Clause four." Torren sniffed. "I read it very carefully. With my glasses."

<br/>

"I remember."

<br/>

"You said I couldn't *name* it." Torren reached out one thick finger and laid it, very gently, on the little drawing in the corner of the parchment. On the fish's tail. "You never said I couldn't *draw* it."

<br/>

Mark leaned closer.

<br/>

Along the curve of the fish's tail, so small that they looked like scales, so faint that in all the months the charter had lived in Lyra's apron nobody had ever noticed them, there were letters. Six of them. Pressed carefully into the parchment with the very tip of a pencil.

<br/>

*BESSIE.*

<br/>

Mark looked at it for a long time.

<br/>

"I named it the first night," Torren said. "The night you all signed it. I sat up late in the workshop, after, and I thought, this needs a name. A *proper* one. And you'd said no. So I put it in the fish." He looked at the blower under his hand. "Every project should be named after the first thing it fixed. That's what I think. That's only right." He patted Bessie's housing gently. "She's where it started. A melted disc and a bit of copper wire and a boy in my coal pit who didn't know what he was doing yet."

<br/>

"I knew what I was doing," said Mark.

<br/>

"You didn't know what you were doing *yet*," said Torren firmly. "Nobody does, at the start." He looked around the yard. At the patients, and the students, and the nobility, and the lamps, and the clean grey morning. "But she knew. Bessie knew. She was the first one who ran clean." He sniffed hugely. "So it's *hers*."

<br/>

*Project Bessie*.

<br/>

Mark stood in the armory yard at dawn, with the charter on the blower and the blower humming under it, and found that, of all the things that had happened in the last twelve hours, this was the one that finally made his eyes sting.

<br/>

"It's a good name," he said.

<br/>

"It's the *best* name," said Torren.

<br/>

"It's the best name," Mark agreed.

<br/>

Lyra was already writing it into the charter, very small, under the fish, in proper letters, so that nobody would ever have to squint at a tail again. When she'd finished, she looked up at Mark, and for the first time since the cavern, she smiled.

<br/>

"Is that on the list?" she asked. "*Strange Things Mark Says*?"

<br/>

"What?"

<br/>

"'It's a good name.'" She grinned. "You never say that. You *hate* Torren's names."

<br/>

"I don't hate them," said Mark. He looked at the four blowers humming in their row in the morning light. At Bessie, Mabel, Dot, and Gertrude. At Gerald's core sitting on the cobbles. At Legacy, who had somehow, at some point, without anybody seeing, made her way back into the corner of the empty workshop doorframe and was already repairing her web. "I've just never admitted they were good."

Mark set down the charcoal, and read the entry through, slowly, the whole way down.

<br/>

Then he closed the ledger, and sat back on the crate in the doorway of the Fish & Hammer, in the early morning sun, beside a spider and a humming blower, and for a while, for the first time in a very long time, he didn't think about anything at all.
