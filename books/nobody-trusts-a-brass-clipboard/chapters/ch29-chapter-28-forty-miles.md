## Chapter 28: Forty Miles

<br/>

Nobody in the Fish & Hammer had the faintest idea what to do with a Thorne.

<br/>

Julian stood just inside the workshop door, dripping onto the flagstones. He had stopped in the exact spot where Garek had stood for five hours and eleven minutes that morning, and he seemed to sense, without being told, that the spot carried some significance, because he kept glancing down at it as if he might be standing on someone's grave.

<br/>

Rain ran off the hem of his coat in a steady trickle. It pooled around his ruined boots. It dripped from his hair onto the linen around his hands, and where it touched the linen, the dark stains on his palms spread a little wider.

<br/>

Everyone looked at him. He looked at everyone. For a long moment, the only sound in the workshop was the rain, and the soft, unhappy ticking of Edna in her corner.

<br/>

Then Torren stood up.

<br/>

"Right," said Torren. "Noodles."

<br/>

Julian blinked. "I'm sorry?"

<br/>

"You've walked forty miles," Torren said. "In the rain. Through *fields*." He said *fields* the way another man might have said *a burning building*. "You'll want noodles."

<br/>

"I'm not—" Julian began, with an automatic, faintly aristocratic stiffness, and then stopped, as if he'd heard himself and didn't recognize the voice. He swallowed. "I don't know what noodles are."

<br/>

The workshop went very quiet.

<br/>

"You don't know," said Torren slowly, "what *noodles* are."

<br/>

"We didn't—at home, we didn't have—" Julian looked helplessly around the room. "Is it a kind of soup?"

<br/>

Torren looked at Mark with an expression of profound, almost religious horror.

<br/>

"There's a pot from the river stall," Mark said gently. "Cold, by now."

<br/>

"Then I'll *heat* it," said Torren.

<br/>

There was a pause in which everyone in the room thought about heat, and how things were heated, and what had happened to the last thing in this building that had made heat.

<br/>

"With the *small forge*," Torren clarified, with dignity. "The old way. Flint and tinder and bellows. Like my grandmother did." He was already pulling the cover off the little side hearth. "Nobody's going to audit a *bellows*."

---

It took Torren twenty minutes to coax the small forge into life without a single crystal, puffing and swearing gently at the tinder, and he enjoyed every one of them. By the time the pot of river noodles was steaming on the coals, the workshop smelled of woodsmoke and broth and wet wool, and it was a little less like a room full of people who had once been enemies.

<br/>

Nobody had told Julian to sit down. He was still standing on Garek's spot.

<br/>

Garek, for his part, had returned to the doorway, where he leaned against the frame with his arms folded and his eyes fixed on the Thorne boy with the flat, patient, professional suspicion of a man who had been paid by Thorne money before and remembered every copper of it. Above his head, Legacy had adopted the same posture, insofar as a spider could fold her arms.

<br/>

"Sit," said Lyra at last, from the workbench, without looking up from her graver. "You're dripping on the floor. We just swept it."

<br/>

Julian looked around. There was the good chair, which was Vespera's. There was Mark's stool, and Lyra's. And there were crates.

<br/>

He chose a crate. He sat on it the way he had sat on every chair in his life: back straight, knees together, hands folded on his lap, as if a portrait painter might arrive at any moment. On a crate, in a workshop, in sodden clothes, it looked so deeply wrong that Corwin, lying on the long bench under the window, opened one eye to look at it, and then opened the other.

<br/>

"You're sitting on a crate," Corwin observed, "like it owes your family money."

<br/>

Julian looked down at himself. "How else does one sit on it?"

<br/>

"Badly," said Corwin. "Comfortably. Like this." He demonstrated by not moving at all. "It's an acquired skill."

<br/>

"You," said Julian slowly, looking at him properly for the first time. "You were on the steps. Under the gate. The night I—" He stopped. "You were lying on the steps."

<br/>

Corwin's eyebrows rose a quarter of an inch, which for Corwin was an expression of total astonishment.

<br/>

"You *saw* me?"

<br/>

"Of course I saw you. You were six feet away."

<br/>

"Nobody sees me," said Corwin. "It's my one talent." He considered Julian for a long moment, with new and genuine interest. "Huh."

<br/>

Torren arrived with a steaming bowl and set it into Julian's bandaged hands, and Julian looked down at it in the firelight: a tangle of long pale noodles in a dark, fragrant broth, with a few bits of green and something that might have been river fish.

<br/>

"Fork," Torren said, pushing one into the crook of Julian's linen-wrapped fingers. "Twirl it. Slurp. It's allowed. It's *encouraged*."

<br/>

Julian twirled. Julian slurped, very cautiously, as if expecting to be reprimanded.

<br/>

Then he stopped.

<br/>

He looked down at the bowl. He looked up at Torren. He looked down at the bowl again.

<br/>

Then he ate the entire thing, without stopping once, so fast that broth ran down his chin onto his ruined coat, and when he was finished he sat holding the empty bowl in both hands and stared at it with an expression that Mark recognized, after a moment, from his own face in a tavern in Oakhaven on his first night in this world. The look of someone who has been cold and hungry for a very long time and has only just noticed.

<br/>

"More?" said Torren softly.

<br/>

Julian nodded. He didn't seem to trust his voice.

<br/>

Torren took the bowl back to the forge.

---

Lyra stopped engraving.

<br/>

She had been working at the bench since the Auditor left, with her graver and a blank silver strip and a small, frowning intensity that nobody had dared to interrupt. Now she set the graver down, reached into the third pocket of her apron, and took out a small roll of oiled leather wrapped around a springy core of ash.

<br/>

She walked over and set it on the crate beside Julian, next to his second bowl of noodles.

<br/>

He looked at it. "What is it?"

<br/>

"Grip trainer," said Lyra. "For your hands. When they heal. Squeeze it ten times every morning, or your fingers will stiffen up around the scars and you'll never hold a staff properly again." She paused. "Ask Garek."

<br/>

From the doorway, without unfolding his arms, Garek said, "It's good."

<br/>

Julian picked up the trainer in his bandaged fingers and looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at Lyra, who had been locked in an archive by him, and chased up a staircase by a seven-foot construct because of him, and whose best work had been crushed to nothing that afternoon by a thing his family served.

<br/>

"Thank you," he said quietly.

<br/>

Lyra shrugged, and went back to her bench, and picked up her graver. But Mark noticed that she'd turned her stool very slightly, so that she could keep half an eye on the crate.

---

Vespera did his hands.

<br/>

She couldn't heal them. Everyone in the room knew it without having to say so. The grey glyphs on the inside of her left wrist said *correction scheduled*, and the thing that had scheduled it was six streets away, walking its grid in the dark. One warm, gentle, silent pulse into those blistered palms, and she'd as good as walk out into the street and wave.

<br/>

So she did it the old way.

<br/>

She brought a basin of clean water from the pump, and a roll of fresh linen, and a small clay pot of salve that smelled of beeswax and lavender, and she knelt in front of Julian's crate and took his hands, one at a time, into her lap.

<br/>

The old bandages had stuck. She soaked them loose with warm water from the forge kettle, patiently, a little at a time, talking quietly to him while she worked, the way she talked to the dockhands in the Infirmary line. When the linen finally came away, Mark saw Julian's palms properly for the first time since the arena, and had to look away for a moment. The burns ran in long, angry stripes, exactly where the ironwood of his staff had lain against his skin, raw and weeping and red, crossed with smaller blisters where the walk had broken them open again.

<br/>

*"Thermal bleed,"* Mark thought, with a cold, flat anger that surprised him. *"Through the insulation. Exactly as designed."*

<br/>

Vespera cleaned each palm. She spread the salve with her fingertips, very lightly. She wrapped each hand in fresh, clean linen, neatly, firmly, without hurrying. It took a long time. Julian sat absolutely still through all of it, with his jaw tight, and watched her do it.

<br/>

When she'd finished the second hand, he didn't take it back right away.

<br/>

"You didn't use the bracelet," he said.

<br/>

"No."

<br/>

"You could have healed them in a minute."

<br/>

"I could," said Vespera. "And then a thing in a grey robe would have walked into this room and taken me away to be corrected." She tied off the last knot. "So I used water, and linen, and lavender. The way my grandmother did it, before she ever knew what a Chapterhouse was."

<br/>

Julian looked down at his clean white hands.

<br/>

"Nobody's ever done that," he said slowly. "Helped me. Without magic. In my whole life. There was always a physician with a staff, or a Keeper with a ring, or…" He trailed off. "I didn't know it could just be *done*."

<br/>

"It can always just be done," said Vespera, getting up off her knees. "It's only slower."

<br/>

She went to put the basin away. Julian sat on his crate, staring at his hands, and Mark, watching him from across the room, thought that he looked about four years younger than he had at dinner in the Thorne house, and about ten years older than he had in the arena.

---

Pip arrived a little before three, having apparently been unable to sleep with a Thorne on the premises, and stood in the doorway beside Garek with his arms folded in exact imitation, glaring.

<br/>

"He needs a code," Pip announced.

<br/>

Everyone looked at him.

<br/>

"For the bus," Pip said impatiently. "If he's—" he jerked his chin at Julian, "—going to be *around*, the runners need a word for him. We can't go shouting *Julian Thorne* across the market. Everyone knows who that is." He narrowed his eyes. "And he can't be *Gerald*. Gerald's for danger."

<br/>

"Is he danger?" said Garek, to nobody in particular.

<br/>

"*Undetermined*," said Pip darkly.

<br/>

Torren, who was stirring the noodle pot, brightened at once. "Oh! I'll name him!"

<br/>

"*No*," said Mark, Lyra, and Vespera, at exactly the same moment.

<br/>

Torren ignored them. He looked at Julian with the warm, assessing, slightly squinting expression he wore when inspecting a newly arrived blower.

<br/>

"Cheese the Second," he said.

<br/>

There was a short, appalled silence.

<br/>

"*Torren*," said Lyra.

<br/>

"What? He's sort of pale. And a bit—"

<br/>

"Nobody," said Lyra, with great intensity, "says *Cheese the Second*. *Ever*. We agreed. After what happened."

<br/>

"What happened?" asked Julian.

<br/>

"*Don't ask about Cheese the Second*," said four voices at once.

<br/>

Julian closed his mouth.

<br/>

"Percival," said Corwin, from the bench, with his eyes closed.

<br/>

Everyone turned to look at him.

<br/>

"Percival Ashdown," Corwin elaborated. "Heir to the Western March. Rode his own spell over the ring wall into the front row." He yawned. "Nobody will ever suspect the bus is talking about anyone important if they hear the word *Percival*."

<br/>

Pip considered this with the gravity of a man approving a major infrastructure change. Then he nodded.

<br/>

"*Percival*," he said. "Percival it is. Message bus, out." He turned and left, presumably to inform every runner in Oakhaven before dawn.

<br/>

Julian sat on his crate, holding a bowl of noodles in his clean bandaged hands, with his new code name hanging in the air.

<br/>

"Percival," he repeated, faintly.

<br/>

"He'll be flattered," said Corwin. "Percival's flattered by everything."

---

By the third bowl, the shaking had mostly stopped. Julian set it down on the crate beside him, wiped his mouth on the back of his bandage, and looked up at Mark.

<br/>

Mark had been waiting. He'd learned, a long time ago, that the person who walked into the room in the middle of the night always had something to say, and that they'd say it sooner if you let them eat first.

<br/>

"I came," said Julian, "to tell you that my father is right."

<br/>

The workshop went still.

<br/>

Lyra's graver stopped. By the window, Vespera turned. In the doorway, Garek straightened, very slightly, and his hands came out of his folded arms.

<br/>

"About the dam," Julian said. "About the price. About all of it. Everything he told you in his study. I know what he tells people. I've heard him do it." He looked at his hands. "It's true. Every word. That's what I came to tell you."

<br/>

He raised his eyes again, and they were clear and red-rimmed and very steady.

<br/>

"And you *still* can't take his offer," Julian said. "That's the other thing I came to tell you."

<br/>

Mark looked at him for a long moment.

<br/>

"You'd better start at the beginning," he said. "And you'd better tell me how you got out."

---

"My father locked me in my rooms," Julian said. "The night you left."

<br/>

He said it simply, without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather.

<br/>

"He didn't shout. He doesn't. He walked me up the stairs himself, after dinner, and he said, 'You'll rest here until this is settled,' and he closed the door, and I heard the key turn." Julian looked down at the grip trainer in his hand and squeezed it once, carefully. "There was a man outside the door after that. Day and night. One of the ones with the rings."

<br/>

"For how long?" Mark asked.

<br/>

"Six days." Julian squeezed the trainer again. "They brought meals. A physician came to change the bandages. My father came once, on the third day. He stood in the door and asked how my hands were. I said they were healing. He said, 'Good.' And he left." A pause. "That was all."

<br/>

Nobody in the workshop said anything.

<br/>

"And then, on the sixth night," Julian went on, "the door opened, and it was my mother."

---

Lady Seraphine Thorne had come into her son's room a little after midnight, in a plain grey cloak with the hood up, carrying a candle and a covered basket. The man with the ring was no longer outside the door. Julian never found out where he'd gone, and he hadn't asked.

<br/>

She hadn't said much. She'd put down the candle, and taken a second cloak out of the basket, a heavy, waterproofed traveling cloak of oiled wool that had belonged to one of the grooms, and wrapped it around his shoulders herself, the way she'd wrapped him in blankets when he was very small. She'd fastened the clasp at his throat. She'd tucked a small packet of food into the inside pocket. Then she'd taken his face in both her hands, very gently, careful of his bandages, and looked at him for a long moment.

<br/>

"Go to the boy who saved you," she'd said. "He'll know what to do. I don't."

<br/>

She'd walked him down the servants' stair in the dark, past the kitchens, to the small scullery door that opened onto the kitchen gardens, and the garden wall, and the fields beyond. At the door, she'd stopped.

<br/>

"There are two more things," she'd said.

<br/>

She'd pressed something into his hand, heavy and cold, wrapped in a scrap of velvet.

<br/>

"I took this from your father's desk. He keeps it in the left-hand drawer. He doesn't think I know." She'd folded his bandaged fingers around it. "You'll know what it's for. I don't want to."

<br/>

"And the other thing?"

<br/>

His mother had hesitated. Then, for the first time that night, she'd almost smiled.

<br/>

"Tell the boy," she'd said, "that the soup was not my idea."

---

Julian reached into the inside pocket of the groom's cloak, which was steaming gently by the forge, and drew out a small bundle wrapped in dark blue velvet. He laid it on the workbench and unfolded the cloth with his clumsy bandaged fingers.

<br/>

Inside lay a key.

<br/>

It was about the length of Mark's hand and astonishingly heavy for its size. It was made of black obsidian, polished so smooth it looked wet, and its bow was carved into a perfect circle cut through by a single straight line. Along the shaft, fine as hairs, ran a row of First Era glyphs.

<br/>

Mark felt the translation layer reach for them and catch them, the way it caught everything.

<br/>

`[DEEP VAULT 3 — KEEPER ACCESS]`

<br/>

"Every Keeper House has one," Julian said quietly. "One key. It's handed down with the ring. It opens the Deep Vault under the Oakhaven Chapterhouse. Where the Anchor is." He looked at Mark. "Where the Auditor is going. In eight days."

<br/>

Lyra had come over from the bench without anyone noticing. She was bent over the key, her lens screwed into her eye, not touching it.

<br/>

"It's beautiful work," she murmured. "Whoever cut those glyphs was better than anyone alive."

<br/>

"It's five hundred years old," said Julian.

<br/>

"I know," said Lyra. "That's what I said."

<br/>

Mark looked down at the black key in its nest of blue velvet for a long time.

<br/>

Then he looked up at Julian.

<br/>

"Your mother," he said, "has excellent judgment."

<br/>

"About the key?"

<br/>

"About the soup," said Mark. "It was delicious. But it was absolutely somebody's idea."

<br/>

For the first time since he'd walked through the door, Julian Thorne laughed. It was a short, startled, cracked little sound, as if it had come out of him by accident and he wasn't sure whether he was allowed to keep it.

<br/>

He kept it.

---

Mark sent a runner for Hollis Grey at first light.

<br/>

He didn't explain why. He just asked Wren, who had appeared at dawn with the uncanny instinct of a child who knows something interesting has happened overnight, to go to the Academy and tell Hollis that the Fish & Hammer needed him, and that it was about Julian.

<br/>

Hollis arrived forty minutes later, at a run, still in his Academy robes, with flour on his sleeves and his face set hard. He came across the yard fast, and he was already talking as he reached the door.

<br/>

"Wren said it's about Julian. Is it his father? Did they—has something—"

<br/>

He stopped in the doorway.

<br/>

Julian was sitting on his crate by the small forge, in a borrowed shirt of Torren's that hung on him like a sail, with his clean bandaged hands wrapped around a fourth bowl of noodles.

<br/>

Hollis stared at him.

<br/>

Julian stared back.

<br/>

Neither of them said anything. Mark, standing by the workbench, recognized the silence. He'd seen it before in his old life, in hallways and parking lots and the doorways of conference rooms: two people who used to be friends, and then stopped, and had each privately rehearsed the first thing they'd say if they ever met again, and had now forgotten all of it.

<br/>

"You look terrible," Hollis said at last.

<br/>

"I walked from the capital," said Julian.

<br/>

"*Walked?*"

<br/>

"Through the fields."

<br/>

"In *those* boots?"

<br/>

Julian looked down at the ruins of his boots. "They were very good boots," he said, a little defensively, "when I started."

<br/>

Hollis made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

<br/>

Then he didn't seem to know what to do next. He stood in the doorway, broad and floury and uncertain, with his big hands opening and closing at his sides, and Julian sat on his crate, small and pale in Torren's enormous shirt, with his bowl in his lap, and for a long moment, neither of them moved at all.

<br/>

Torren, who had watched all of this from the forge with deep and tender interest, picked up a second bowl, ladled it full of noodles, walked across the workshop, and put it firmly into Hollis's hands.

<br/>

"Sit," he said. "Eat. Next to him."

<br/>

Hollis looked down at the bowl. He looked at Torren. Then, slowly, he walked over and lowered himself onto the crate beside Julian's, which creaked alarmingly, and sat there, holding his noodles.

<br/>

They ate in silence for a while, side by side. The forge crackled. Edna ticked in her corner.

<br/>

"You stopped sitting with me," Julian said at last, to his bowl.

<br/>

"You started being horrible," said Hollis, to his.

<br/>

"I know."

<br/>

"You were horrible to *everyone*. To the first-years. To Tessa Fenn. You called her rod *firewood*. In front of the whole class."

<br/>

"I know."

<br/>

"You used to *help* the first-years, Julian. With their chants. You were *funny*. You did that voice. The Ignis voice." Hollis's own voice wavered. "And then you went away for two weeks, and you came back with that ring, and you just… closed. Like a door. And you never said why. And I didn't *know* why, so I stopped—" He broke off. "I stopped trying. I should have kept trying."

<br/>

Julian set down his bowl on the crate beside him. He looked at his clean bandaged hands for a long moment.

<br/>

"I'll tell you why," he said. "If you still want to know."

<br/>

Hollis looked at him.

<br/>

"I've wanted to know," he said quietly, "for a year."

---

The workshop was very quiet while Julian talked.

<br/>

Lyra stopped engraving and turned all the way around on her stool. Vespera set down her tea. Torren sat down on the edge of the small forge, which was still warm. Even Corwin rolled onto his side on the bench and opened both eyes, which was unprecedented.

<br/>

"My father took me to the capital last winter," Julian began, "for my initiation. Every Thorne heir is initiated at sixteen. You learn what the ring is. You learn what the House does. What the Measure is for." He paused. "You learn what the Keepers keep."

<br/>

"For two weeks," said Hollis.

<br/>

"For two weeks. Lessons, mostly. Very old books. Very old men. Paintings." Julian's voice was steady and flat. "And on the last night, they showed me a correction."

<br/>

Vespera's hand went, very slightly, to the inside of her left wrist.

<br/>

"There was a girl," Julian said. "In a fishing village on the coast, three days' ride from the capital. The Concord had been watching her for some time, I think. They'd brought her in a few days before." He looked at the forge. "I only saw her once. From across a courtyard. In the torchlight. It was a long way."

<br/>

"What was she like?" asked Lyra softly.

<br/>

"Young," said Julian. "Sixteen, maybe. My age. Thin. Ordinary. She was wearing a fisherman's smock, too big for her." He swallowed. "They told me nobody in the village knew where she'd come from. She'd just… turned up. One season. Nobody could say how. She spoke oddly, they said. They didn't say how, and I didn't ask." He was quiet for a moment. "I wish I'd asked."

<br/>

Mark, by the workbench, found that he had gone very still.

<br/>

He couldn't have said why. It wasn't anything Julian had said, exactly. Plenty of people turned up in fishing villages.

<br/>

His hand drifted up toward the bridge of his nose. He noticed it, and lowered it, and folded both hands on the workbench in front of him, and kept them there.

<br/>

"She was frightened," Julian said. "That's what I remember. More than anything. Not her face. I couldn't see her face, really. Just the way she was standing. Like she was trying to be very small." His bandaged fingers tightened in his lap. "There were four Keepers around her. And a construct. A small one. And my father."

<br/>

"What did they do?" asked Hollis, very quietly.

<br/>

"They told me what she was." Julian's voice didn't change at all. "They told me she wasn't a girl. They said she was *Advenai*. Something from the far side, that had come through. Wearing one." He paused. "That was how they said it. *Wearing* one. Like a coat."

<br/>

Nobody spoke.

<br/>

"And then they took her below," Julian said. "Down the stairs, under the Concord house. Into the vaults. The construct went first. She didn't fight. She just went." He was looking at the forge, but Mark didn't think he was seeing it. "She didn't come back up."

<br/>

The forge crackled softly.

<br/>

"My father put his hand on my shoulder," Julian said. "Afterward. In the courtyard. He said, 'It's a mercy, Julian. For her, and for us.' And then he took the ring out of his pocket and put it on my finger." He turned his right hand over and looked at the pale band of skin where the ring had been. "And I came home, and I tried for a year to believe him. That it was a mercy. That she was a thing wearing a girl." His voice finally cracked, very slightly. "I tried so *hard*, Hollis."

<br/>

Hollis didn't say anything. He reached over, slowly, and put one big floury hand on Julian's shoulder, the way Julian's father had in the courtyard, and left it there.

<br/>

Julian shut his eyes.

<br/>

"That's why I locked you in the archive," he said to Mark, without opening them. "When the old plates woke up. I knew what the construct was for. I thought, if it came for you, and you were just a boy, a clever scrap boy, and it went past you… then I'd know they were wrong about what it hunts. And if it *didn't* go past you…" He stopped. "Then I'd never have to find out about her. Because I'd know they were right."

<br/>

"It didn't go past me," said Mark quietly.

<br/>

"No." Julian opened his eyes. "It came for you. Because you were casting clean. That's what I told myself. That's all it was. The clean casting." He looked up at Mark. "I didn't want to find out anything else."

<br/>

Mark held his gaze, and said nothing.

<br/>

*A frightened girl,* he thought, *and a frightened institution.*

<br/>

That was all it was. A girl from nowhere, in a village where nobody asked enough questions, picked up by an organization that had been afraid of the same thing for five hundred years and had stopped checking its own assumptions centuries ago. He'd seen it a hundred times. A false positive in a system with no appeal process. It was monstrous, and it was ordinary, and he'd been angry about smaller versions of it his whole career.

<br/>

That was all it was.

<br/>

"I'm sorry," he said to Julian. "For her. I'm sorry you saw that."

<br/>

"So am I," said Julian. "Every night." He wiped his face carefully with the back of one bandage. "But I didn't walk forty miles to tell you about her. I came to tell you about the dam. About what's on the other side of it." He looked around the workshop, at all of them. "And what happens in eight days, when the Auditor goes down the stairs."

---

Mark took the charcoal off the shelf and went to the long whitewashed wall at the back of the workshop, the one where Torren chalked up orders, and wiped a broad clean space with his sleeve.

<br/>

He'd briefly reached for the brass clipboard first. Then he'd looked at it, and remembered the slate-grey rod sliding down its edge, and put it back on the shelf. He wasn't ready to hold it yet.

<br/>

"Go slowly," he said to Julian. "I'm going to write it down as you go. Not what you say. What it *means*. If I get it wrong, tell me."

<br/>

Julian looked at the charcoal, and the wall, and then at Mark, with the faintly bewildered expression of every person who had ever been briefed by Mark in either world.

<br/>

"Means to *whom*?"

<br/>

"To me," said Mark. "I think in diagrams. It's a condition."

<br/>

"It's on the list," Lyra said, from her stool. "Strange Things Mark Says. Number forty-one."

<br/>

"Forty-three," said Vespera.

<br/>

"Forty-three," Lyra corrected.

<br/>

Julian looked at them all, and then, slowly, as if lowering himself into cold water, he began.

---

"The words first," Julian said. "Since you've been chasing them. *Advenai. Ulteri. Exori.*"

<br/>

Mark wrote the three words across the top of the wall in large capitals.

<br/>

"*Advenai* you have," said Julian. "Those who arrive. Strangers. Not guests: the ones nobody invited. The Concord changed the stroke, a long time ago, so that it reads 'honored guests' in the graduation oath. My father thinks that's very funny. He's never told anyone why."

<br/>

Mark wrote *THOSE WHO ARRIVE* under the first word.

<br/>

"*Exori* you have too. Arisen from outside. Or *come up* from outside, the way water comes up through a floor." Julian paused. "*Ulteri* is the middle one. The one they took out of the language. It means the far side. The other side of a wall. The place beyond the place you can see."

<br/>

Mark's charcoal stopped on the wall.

<br/>

"*Ulteri*," he repeated slowly. "*Ulterior.*" He shook his head. "Of course. Of *course* it is."

<br/>

"What's *ulterior*?" asked Torren.

<br/>

"A word from where I come from. It means *hidden*. *Beyond what's stated.*" Mark wrote *THE FAR SIDE* under the middle word, and then, after a moment, underlined it twice. "It's the same root. Two languages, two worlds, and somebody, somewhere, built the same word out of the same piece." He looked at it for a moment longer than he meant to. Then he moved on. "So. *Advenai Ulteri Exori.*"

<br/>

He wrote it out in full across the wall, under the three words.

<br/>

*THOSE WHO ARRIVE FROM THE FAR SIDE, ARISEN FROM OUTSIDE.*

<br/>

They all looked at it for a while.

<br/>

"That's a terrible name," said Corwin, from the bench. "For anything. Very long. You'd never get it out in an emergency."

<br/>

"The Keepers say *Exori*," said Julian. "When they're in a hurry."

<br/>

"Exori," Mark repeated, and wrote it smaller in the corner, the way he'd once written the short name for a long service in the margins of a system diagram. Then, without quite meaning to, he wrote three letters beside it.

<br/>

*A.U.E.*

<br/>

"What's that?" said Lyra.

<br/>

"An acronym," said Mark. "It's what you do with a name that's too long to say in an emergency. You take the first letter of each word." He looked at the three letters. "In my old job, everything important had one. Usually several. Usually nobody could remember what they stood for."

<br/>

"Is that on the list?" said Vespera.

<br/>

"Forty-four," said Lyra, writing.

---

"Now," said Julian, "the history. As the Keepers teach it." He took a breath. "In the First Era, magic was clean. The way you cast. No pinch. No heat in the hands. Everything open, everything grounded, everything flowing. The plates you found in the archive were from then. They were *beautiful*." He glanced at Lyra. "You know. You copied them."

<br/>

Lyra nodded slowly. Her hand had gone, without her noticing, to the dull bracelet on her wrist.

<br/>

"And it was loud," Julian said.

<br/>

Mark wrote *FIRST ERA: CLEAN* on the wall, and, underneath it, *LOUD*.

<br/>

"Not loud the way a staff is loud," Julian said. "Sparks and shouting. The other way. The way you heard in your shed, Lyra said. That ringing. Every clean spell rings. And a world full of people casting clean, for hundreds of years, every day, everywhere…" He searched for the words. "The Keepers say it was like a city full of bells, that never stopped. And the wall between this world and the far side—"

<br/>

"*Ulteri*," said Mark.

<br/>

"—is thin. It was always thin. And the ringing wore it thinner." Julian's voice dropped. "And things heard. On the other side. And things came through."

<br/>

"What kind of things?" asked Hollis, very quietly.

<br/>

"*Advenai*," said Julian. "That's all the books say. That's all the Keepers ever say. They don't describe them. They don't draw them. They just say *Advenai*, and lower their voices." He hesitated. "But they have paintings. From the end of the First Era. In the Concord house. They showed me." He looked at the cold forge. "Cities. Whole cities. Frozen solid. Not snow. Not ice. Just… cold. Everything. People in the streets. Birds on the roofs. Rivers stopped in the middle of the current. As if all the heat in the whole place had been taken out of it at once."

<br/>

Nobody said anything.

<br/>

Mark looked down at the charcoal in his fingers, and remembered, very clearly, the night in his shed with Prototype 2.0. The hundred-pound iron block cracked in half. The air dropping twenty degrees in a heartbeat. The frost blooming over his workbench, over his files, over his ledger. Lyra by the door with her hands over her ears and her breath steaming.

<br/>

*The circuit pulled ambient thermal energy out of the room,* he'd told her. *Energy can't come from nowhere.*

<br/>

He'd been very pleased with that explanation at the time.

<br/>

"The Keepers call it the Arrival," said Julian. "The end of the First Era. And after it, a group of the mages who were left, the ones who'd survived, got together and made a decision." He looked at Mark. "You know the rest. You found the decree."

<br/>

"*By order of the Concord, in the first year of the Measure*," Mark quoted softly.

<br/>

He wrote it on the wall.

<br/>

*THE MEASURE.*

<br/>

"They made every spell worse," Julian said. "On purpose. Pinched every channel. Trapped every bit of heat in the caster's own hands. Every staff and bracelet and lamp in the Empire. So that magic would be *quiet* again. Quiet everywhere except in the body of the person casting it." He looked down at his own bandaged palms. "The pain is the silence, Mark. That's what my father means. Every blister is the sound of a bell that didn't ring."

<br/>

Mark stood in front of the wall for a long moment, with the charcoal in his hand.

<br/>

Then he drew it.

<br/>

He drew a long horizontal line across the wall. Above the line, he wrote *THIS WORLD*. Below it, *ULTERI*. He drew small wavy marks rising up from the world toward the line: *noise*. He drew the line thinning where the marks touched it. He drew a thick, solid band across the top of the line, and labeled it *MEASURE (RATE LIMIT)*. He drew little boxes along the band and labeled them *constructs*. He drew a larger box over all of it and labeled it *AUDITOR*. And then he stood back and looked at the whole thing.

<br/>

"It's a firewall," he said.

<br/>

"A *what*?" said Torren.

<br/>

"A wall that decides what gets through." Mark tapped the band. "The Concord built a firewall around the entire world. It keeps the noise down, so nothing on the far side hears us. And anything that does come through from outside…" He tapped the boxes. "…gets hunted by the constructs. That's what was etched into Gerald's core. *TARGET CLASS: three glyphs.* The constructs weren't built to hunt clean casting. They were built to hunt *Advenai*. Clean casting just sets them off because it's loud. It's a… secondary signature."

<br/>

Julian nodded slowly. "That's what the Keepers say. Yes."

<br/>

"Default deny," said Mark. "Five hundred years. Everything off-spec gets restored. Everything from outside gets quarantined." He put down the charcoal. "It's not even a bad design. Honestly. For the threat model they had, it's quite good. I'd have built something like it." He paused. "In 2009. Before I knew better."

---

Vespera spoke for the first time in a long while.

<br/>

"Correction," she said.

<br/>

Everyone turned. She was sitting very straight in the good chair, with her left arm resting on the arm of it, turned palm up, so that the pale grey glyphs on the inside of her wrist were clearly visible in the forge light.

<br/>

"You haven't said what *correction* means," she said evenly. "For someone like me. Or Valerius." She didn't look at the glyphs. "I'd like to know."

<br/>

Julian looked at her wrist. Mark saw him take in the glyphs, and saw his face change.

<br/>

"For a native caster," Julian said carefully, "someone born here, who's learned to cast clean, it's… they call it *restoration*." He hesitated. "The same as the bracelets. The same as Lyra's. They restore you to specification. The clean paths you've learned, the open channels in your nerves, the ones that don't hurt…" He stopped. "They close them. Pinch them back. To the Guild template."

<br/>

"So I would cast the old way again," Vespera said. "With the staff. With the pain."

<br/>

"If you could cast at all," said Julian quietly. "The Keepers say it doesn't always… take cleanly. On people. Not like on silver. Sometimes they close too much." He looked at the floor. "Sometimes people can't cast at all afterward. Not even dirty. Not even a candle."

<br/>

The workshop was very quiet.

<br/>

Vespera sat very still. Then she looked down at her wrist, finally, at the thin grey line of glyphs below her bracelet, and Mark watched something move behind her eyes: the thought of a dockhand's stiff shoulder, and a bowl of soup, and a long line of patients in the rain, and all of it *closed*, forever, back to the way it had been before.

<br/>

She didn't say anything. She turned her wrist over, so the glyphs faced the arm of the chair, and folded her other hand on top of it.

<br/>

"And Advenai?" Lyra asked. Her voice was a little rough. "What's correction for *them*?"

<br/>

Julian shook his head slowly.

<br/>

"I don't know," he said. "Nobody's ever told me. They took her below, and she didn't come back up. That's all I know." He looked up. "I don't think anybody who isn't a Concord elder knows. I don't think my *father* knows."

<br/>

Mark found that he didn't want to look at the wall anymore. He looked at the forge instead.

<br/>

"And when?" he asked. "For Vespera. For Valerius. *Correction scheduled.* Scheduled for when?"

<br/>

"That's the last thing," said Julian. "That's the thing I walked forty miles to tell you." He leaned forward on his crate. "The Auditor doesn't execute the corrections as it goes. It *marks* them. It schedules them. It writes them all down in its book. And when it's finished the whole sector, every street, every house, every person, it goes down the stairs to the Anchor. And it reconciles." He paused. "All at once."

<br/>

"All at once," Mark repeated.

<br/>

"Everything it's marked. Every correction it's scheduled. In the whole sector. At the moment it reaches the Anchor." Julian's bandaged hands tightened on his knees. "Vespera. Valerius. Anybody else it's marked that we don't know about." He looked at Mark. "And anything else the Anchor has on its list."

<br/>

"Eight days," said Corwin, from the bench. He'd sat up at some point, which nobody had noticed, and was sitting with his elbows on his knees, looking at the wall. "I recalculated. It's a little faster than I thought. It likes straight streets."

<br/>

"Eight days," said Mark.

<br/>

He looked at the diagram on the wall. The thin line. The wavy marks. The thick band of the Measure. The little boxes. The big box over all of it.

<br/>

And under the line, in his own handwriting, *ULTERI*. *The far side.*

---

"So we take the offer," said Torren slowly. "Don't we? That's what it comes to. If his lordship's right about all of it, and the wall's real, and the frozen cities are real, then he's right. We stop casting clean. We give him the bracelets. You go and work for them. And maybe he can stop the Auditor doing Vespera." He looked around at them, troubled. "That's what *reasonable* looks like. Isn't it?"

<br/>

Nobody answered.

<br/>

Mark stood in front of the wall for a long time.

<br/>

He thought about Aldric Thorne in his study, turning his teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer. *You have a system that has been stable for five hundred years. It has a known cost. You don't fully understand why it was designed the way it was. What do you do?*

<br/>

*You stop the bypass,* he'd said. *Until you understand it.*

<br/>

He'd meant it. He still meant it.

<br/>

"Aldric's right about the dam," Mark said at last.

<br/>

Everyone looked at him.

<br/>

"He's right that it's real. He's right that it's needed. He's right that a world full of clean casting rings like a bell and wears the wall thin, and that something on the far side hears it. I believe that. I believed it in the shed, when the room froze around us, before I knew any of the words." He turned around to face them. "I'm not going to tear down the dam. I'd be the worst engineer in either world if I did. You don't rip out a five-hundred-year-old load-bearing wall because you don't like the color."

<br/>

"Then—" Torren began.

<br/>

"But a firewall," Mark said, "that silently deletes anything it doesn't recognize, with no logs, and no appeal, and no human being who's ever checked the rules in five hundred years…" He shook his head. "That's not safety. That's just a different kind of danger, that nobody's looking at."

<br/>

He picked up the charcoal again. Under the diagram, he wrote, in large, careful capitals:

<br/>

*WHO WROTE THE RULES?*

<br/>

*WHEN WERE THEY LAST REVIEWED?*

<br/>

*WHAT'S ON THE LIST?*

<br/>

"A girl from a fishing village," Mark said quietly, "got quarantined because she spoke oddly and nobody knew where she came from. Vespera's going to be 'restored' for warming a dockhand's shoulder. Lyra's bracelet got crushed because the template's been wrong for five hundred years and nobody was ever allowed to say so." He tapped the wall. "Aldric doesn't want to fix the firewall. He wants me to help him run it harder. And that's the one thing I won't do."

<br/>

"So what *do* we do?" asked Hollis.

<br/>

Mark looked down at the workbench, at the black obsidian key in its nest of blue velvet.

<br/>

"You don't remove the firewall," he said. "You audit the rules."

<br/>

He picked up the key. It was heavy and very cold.

<br/>

"In eight days, the Auditor goes down the stairs and reconciles everything on its list. Including Vespera. Including Valerius. Including whatever else is down there." He turned the key over in his fingers. "So before it gets there, *we* go down. With this. We find the Anchor. We read the source, before the Auditor rewrites it. We find out what's on the list, and who put it there, and why." He set the key down. "And then we decide what to do. Together. With the actual information. Like engineers."

<br/>

There was a long silence.

<br/>

"That's not *reasonable*," said Torren slowly.

<br/>

"No," Mark agreed. "It's correct. It's a different thing."

<br/>

Torren considered this for a long moment. Then he nodded, very firmly, as if a great weight had been lifted off him.

<br/>

"I *like* correct," he said. "I always liked correct better."

---

Julian asked to join a little after noon.

<br/>

He'd slept for two hours on a row of crates by the forge, under the groom's cloak, so deeply that Torren had tiptoed around him for the entire time and shushed anyone who so much as set down a tool. When he woke, he sat up slowly, looked around the workshop as if he'd forgotten where he was, remembered, and then sat for a while with the grip trainer in his hand, squeezing it, ten times, carefully, the way Lyra had told him.

<br/>

Then he stood up and walked to the workbench, where Mark was going through the ledger.

<br/>

"I want to help," Julian said. "Properly. Not just the key." He paused. "I know the vault stairs. I've been down to the second landing below the archive, twice, with my father. I know what the Keeper doors look like. I can read a little of the old script, not like you, but some." His jaw tightened. "And I know how my father thinks. Better than anybody here."

<br/>

Mark looked at him for a long moment.

<br/>

"That's not my decision," he said. "It's a team. We vote."

---

They voted around the workbench, because that was where they'd signed the charter, a long time ago now, on a sheet of parchment that Lyra kept folded in her apron, in the eighth pocket.

<br/>

She took it out and unfolded it on the bench. *PROJECT ROOT ACCESS*, it said at the top, in Mark's careful capitals, with a list of names underneath and a small, unauthorized drawing of a fish in the corner that everyone knew was Torren's work.

<br/>

Julian looked at the names. Then he looked at the drawing of the fish. He didn't say anything about either, which Mark thought showed real promise.

<br/>

"All in favor," said Mark, "of adding Julian Thorne to Project Root Access."

<br/>

Lyra raised her hand at once.

<br/>

Everyone looked at her, surprised.

<br/>

"What?" she said. "He walked forty miles to tell us what we needed to know, and he brought us a *key*. And he's had the worst week of anyone in this room." She paused. "Except me. And Tilly." She kept her hand up. "Yes."

<br/>

Vespera raised her hand, quietly, without comment.

<br/>

Hollis, who had not technically been asked, raised his too, and nobody objected.

<br/>

Torren raised both of his. "Yes," he said. "Obviously. He likes noodles."

<br/>

Corwin, on his bench, said, "Mm."

<br/>

"Is that a yes?" asked Julian.

<br/>

"It's a 'Mm,'" said Corwin. "It's very significant. I don't give them out to just anybody."

<br/>

That left Garek, in the doorway.

<br/>

Garek hadn't moved. He stood with his arms folded and his feet planted and his shoulders touching both sides of the frame, and he looked at Julian Thorne for a long, flat, unblinking moment.

<br/>

"I abstain," he said.

<br/>

Julian's face fell, very slightly.

<br/>

"For now," Garek added. He unfolded one arm and pointed at the floor in front of him. "Do a leg lift."

<br/>

There was a short pause.

<br/>

"A what?" said Julian.

<br/>

"A leg lift." Garek demonstrated: one knee, slowly, up to the height of his belt, held for a count of three, and lowered again. "For the knee. I do them twice a day. Three times, today." He folded his arm back. "Do one. Properly. And I'll vote."

<br/>

Julian looked at Mark. Mark made no expression at all, which he had found over forty years to be the most powerful expression available to him.

<br/>

Julian looked back at Garek.

<br/>

Then, slowly, in Torren's enormous shirt and his ruined boots, with his bandaged hands held carefully out at his sides for balance, Julian Thorne, heir to House Thorne and Keeper of the Measure, raised his right knee to the height of his belt, held it there, wobbling, for a count of three, and lowered it again.

<br/>

Garek watched with the critical eye of a master craftsman inspecting an apprentice's first weld.

<br/>

"Your standing leg's locked," he said. "Bend it a little. And you're leaning back. Lean forward."

<br/>

Julian did it again. Bent the standing leg. Leaned forward. One, two, three.

<br/>

Garek considered it for a long moment.

<br/>

"Better," he said. He raised one big hand, slowly, about halfway. "Yes. On probation."

<br/>

Julian stood very still in the middle of the workshop, slightly out of breath, and looked as if he'd just been knighted.

---

"Then it's carried," said Mark. He picked up the charcoal stick and leaned over the charter. "Name and title."

<br/>

He wrote, under the last name on the list:

<br/>

*JULIAN THORNE: Former Threat Actor. Current Asset (Probationary).*

<br/>

Julian leaned over to read it. Mark watched him work through it, frowning, his lips moving slightly on *threat actor*, and on *probationary*.

<br/>

"I don't know what half of that means," Julian said at last.

<br/>

"It means you used to be on the other side," said Mark. "And now you're on this one. And we're watching to make sure you stay there."

<br/>

Julian looked at the line for a long moment.

<br/>

"That's fair," he said quietly. And then, a little unsteadily: "Thank you."

<br/>

"And the *key*," said Torren.

<br/>

Everyone turned to look at him. He was standing over the black obsidian key in its velvet, with his reading spectacles on, gazing down at it with enormous warmth.

<br/>

"It needs a name," Torren said. "It's very important. It's very old. It's been through a lot. It's going to *open* things." He looked up. "It needs a name."

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth.

<br/>

He thought about the charter, which banned Torren from naming the *project*, and which said nothing whatsoever about *keys*.

<br/>

He closed it.

<br/>

"Go on," he said.

<br/>

Torren looked down at the key: black, heavy, cold, carved with the same circle-and-line seal as the ring on a construct's hand and the ring on a lord's.

<br/>

"*Gerald's Cousin*," he said.

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"Because it's from the same family," Torren explained. "As Gerald. The construct. Same people made them. Same seal." He patted the velvet tenderly. "But it's on *our* side."

<br/>

Nobody could argue with that, so nobody did.

<br/>

Mark wrote it down.

---

Lyra didn't go home that night.

<br/>

She sat at the workbench under the lamp with her graver and a blank strip of silver, and the dull, ruined bracelet that had been L-1 lying beside it, and she worked. Every so often she picked up Tilly and turned her over under the lens, and looked very carefully at the four places where the Auditor's rod had rested: the clasp, the return channel, the crystal seat, the end of the main trace. Then she put Tilly down and went back to the blank strip.

<br/>

Mark watched her from the other end of the bench for a while. He didn't ask what she was doing. He recognized the look on her face. He'd worn it himself, at three in the morning, more times than he could count: the look of an engineer who has been handed a specification she knows is wrong, and has decided, quietly and absolutely, to meet it anyway, in exactly the places it checks, and nowhere else.

<br/>

He went back to his ledger.

### *Stinger: The Thorne Residence — The Regional Capital*

<br/>

The study was dark except for a single lamp, and the warm gold glow of a ring.

<br/>

Lord Aldric Thorne stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, looking out over the lower tiers of the city toward the river, and the plain beyond it, and the long pale line of the north road vanishing into the night.

<br/>

Behind him, the door opened very softly.

<br/>

Lady Seraphine came in carrying a tray with a plain white teapot and two plain white cups. She set it down on the corner of the desk. Her hands were not quite steady, and the cups chimed faintly against each other on the tray.

<br/>

"He's gone, Aldric," she said.

<br/>

"I know." He didn't turn from the window. "I left the north fields unwatched."

<br/>

She stared at his back.

<br/>

Then something crossed her face that she couldn't quite hide: something fragile and astonished, something very like hope.

<br/>

"You let him go," she said.

<br/>

"I let him take the key." Aldric turned his ring a quarter-turn on his finger, slowly, and watched the gold light move across his knuckles. "Five hundred years, Seraphine. Twenty generations of my family have stood in front of that pillar. Read every line on it. Kept every seal on every door. And not one of us, not my father, not his father, not one, has ever been able to make it *answer*."

<br/>

"Aldric—"

<br/>

"The rings don't flare at that boy." He said it quietly, almost wonderingly. "Did you notice? Not once. Not at dinner. Not in the arena, with four of my best men watching him for three hours. Not in this house, with the soup in front of him and every footman I own counting his breaths." He paused. "Not even on the stairs."

<br/>

"He's a *child*."

<br/>

"He's *something*." Aldric looked back out at the dark road north. "I don't know what. I don't think he knows what. But in eight days, whatever he is will walk down those stairs to the Anchor, because he has no other choice. He'll go to save the mage. And Valerius. And whatever else he thinks is on that list." The faintest movement at the corner of his mouth. "He's very reliable that way. It's his best quality."

<br/>

He picked up one of the white cups, and looked into it, and set it down again without drinking.

<br/>

"And when the door opens for him," said Lord Aldric Thorne, "I intend to be standing right behind him."

<br/>

Lady Seraphine looked at her husband for a long, long time.

<br/>

She didn't say anything. She didn't argue. She didn't cry.

<br/>

She left the tea where it was, untouched, and turned, and went out of the study without a word, and closed the door very softly behind her.

<br/>

Aldric stood alone at the window.

<br/>

On his right hand, the gold stone in his ring pulsed slowly, steadily, like a resting heart, and he watched the north road until the lamp burned down.
