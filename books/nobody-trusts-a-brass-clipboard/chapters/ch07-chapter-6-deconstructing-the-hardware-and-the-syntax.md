## Chapter 6: Deconstructing the Hardware and the Syntax

<br/>

The common room of *The Pine & Anvil* was loud, warm, and thick with the scents of roasting pork, stale ale, and woodsmoke. Mutton tallow candles flickered in iron sconces along the walls, but Mark's attention was drawn to the central hearth.

<br/>

Instead of a pile of kindling and flint, the innkeeper used a smooth copper rod mounted near the stone mantle. He tapped a small iron button on the base and mumbled a quick two-word phrase.

<br/>

Nothing happened.

<br/>

The innkeeper let out the weary sigh of a man who had performed this exact ritual ten thousand times, smacked the copper rod twice with the flat of his palm, and repeated the phrase louder and slower—the way a tourist speaks to a foreign waiter. A neat, smokeless blue flame erupted beneath the oak logs.

<br/>

*"Have you tried turning it off and on again,"* Mark thought, feeling an unexpected pang of something close to homesickness. *"Universal. Absolutely universal."*

<br/>

He leaned back in his chair, taking a slow sip of cold cider. Beside him, Garin was already halfway through a plate of stew, eating with the grim focus of a man who suspected the bowl might be confiscated at any moment. Vespera sat upright, cutting her bread into perfectly even squares.

<br/>

"Aether-assisted ignition," Mark noted aloud, pointing toward the hearth with his spoon. "Copper housing, iron actuator button, hardcoded rune on the nozzle. And a voice password." He frowned. "A two-word voice password that he shouts across a crowded tavern. Anyone in this room could light that fire."

<br/>

Vespera looked at him blankly. "Why would anyone want to light someone else's fire?"

<br/>

"Arson," Mark said. "Pranks. Teenagers." He considered it. "Mostly teenagers."

<br/>

"He's got a point," Garin said through a mouthful of stew. "The Miller boys lit it at three in the morning last winter. Burned through a whole cord of oak before anyone woke up."

<br/>

"Textbook security vulnerability," Mark said, nodding sagely.

<br/>

Vespera set her knife down, giving him a long, measuring look. "It is a standard fire-stone fitting, Mark. Craftspeople have made them for a hundred years."

<br/>

"Sure," Mark said, his software-architect brain picking apart the design. "Because it isolates the user from the danger. The copper acts as a heat sink, the iron button acts as an electrical switch, and the rune acts as a read-only script. The innkeeper doesn't need to know how to manipulate Aether; he just presses a button and executes a pre-packaged program."

<br/>

Garin laughed. "I don't know what half those words mean, kid, but you make a simple hearth-lighter sound like a war engine."

<br/>

"It's about abstraction layers," Mark muttered, mostly to himself.

<br/>

"Abstraction layers," Garin repeated slowly, testing the words like a mushroom he didn't trust. "Is that like a cake?"

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth to say no. Then he paused. "...Actually, yes. Exactly like a cake. You don't need to know how the flour was milled to eat it."

<br/>

Garin nodded, deeply satisfied, and returned to his stew. "I understand everything now."

<br/>

He didn't. But Mark had sat through enough executive briefings to know that one stakeholder who *felt* he understood was worth three who actually did.

<br/>

He turned to Vespera. "Which brings me back to mages. Tell me about your staff."

<br/>

Vespera instinctively rested a hand on the carved timber leaning against the table. "My staff was shaped from hundred-year-old ironwood, cured in salt, and set with a core of refined silver. It took three years at the Academy to attune it to my blood."

<br/>

Mark winced. "Three years? For a single piece of hardware?"

<br/>

"It is a sacred process."

<br/>

"Where I come from, onboarding took two weeks, and my workstation still didn't work at the end of it," Mark said. "I'm not saying yours is worse. I'm saying it's longer."

<br/>

"Workstation?"

<br/>

"Loom," Mark said quickly. "A very expensive, very disappointing loom."

<br/>

Vespera narrowed her eyes but let it go.

<br/>

"Right," Mark said, leaning forward. "Let's look at the materials. Ironwood has high density and high thermal resistance—it's an insulator. Silver has exceptionally high electrical and thermal conductivity—it's a wire. So when you cast a spell, the silver core draws and routes the heavy Aether current, while the ironwood surrounds it to keep the thermal feedback from burning your hands."

<br/>

Vespera blinked. She opened her mouth to object, but paused, mentally reviewing the physical construction of her weapon. "It... protects the caster's body from the strain, yes. Without a conduit, high-order magic would rupture the veins in your arms."

<br/>

"So the staff is an external heatsink and a directional antenna," Mark concluded, nodding slowly. "It's hardware. It's a crutch because human flesh is a terrible conductor for raw voltage."

<br/>

"It is not a crutch!" Vespera said, pulling the staff an inch closer to her chair, the way someone might shield a horse that had just been insulted. She caught herself and lowered her voice. "It is a necessary tool! No human can channel fifty units of Aether through their bare nerves without dying of shock!"

<br/>

"Careful, kid," Garin warned without looking up from his bowl. "Last fellow who called her staff a stick woke up with a beard growing out of his ears."

<br/>

"That is not true," Vespera said primly. She took a sip of tea. "It was his eyebrows."

<br/>

Mark decided to file that under *Known Risks* and moved on. "Fifty units. What's a unit?"

<br/>

"One unit is the force required to lift one Imperial measure of water by one Imperial span."

<br/>

"And how long is an Imperial span?"

<br/>

"The length of the Emperor's forearm."

<br/>

"The current Emperor?"

<br/>

Vespera hesitated. "...Each Emperor's."

<br/>

Mark set his spoon down very slowly. "So your base unit of measurement changes every time someone dies."

<br/>

"It is tradition."

<br/>

*"Somewhere,"* Mark thought, *"the metric system is quietly weeping."*

<br/>

He took a long, steadying drink of cider. "Okay. Setting aside the measurement crisis for later. What if you figured out how to ground the excess energy through your body into the earth before it cooks your organs?"

<br/>

Vespera stared at him as if he were suggesting jumping off a cliff to learn how to fly. "That is insane. You would turn your own nervous system into ash."

<br/>

"Only if you don't manage the load," Mark replied casually, taking another bite of stew. "Now, what about the incantations? Why High Imperial?"

<br/>

"Because High Imperial is the language of the First Mages," Vespera stated, as if reciting from a textbook. "The cadences and vowels are precise. A single mispronounced syllable ruins the focus."

<br/>

"How long is the chant to light a candle?"

<br/>

"Eleven syllables."

<br/>

"To mend a wagon axle?"

<br/>

"Forty."

<br/>

"And the most powerful spell the Academies teach?"

<br/>

Vespera hesitated. "The Rite of the Sundered Sky. It takes a full day of continuous chanting. The last master who attempted it lost his voice for a year." She paused. "He communicates exclusively with a small chalkboard now. He's become quite sarcastic."

<br/>

"So you have a spell with a built-in timeout error," Mark said. "Got it."

<br/>

He leaned forward, tapping the table. "Here's my point. It's a syntax constraint. Human brains aren't good at parallel processing. If you try to hold four variables in your head at once—how much energy to pull, where to direct it, what shape to give it, and how fast to release it—your working memory crashes. So you use spoken words as a sequence. The chant forces your brain to execute the instructions line by line, in a specific order, at a specific speed."

<br/>

Vespera looked at him, genuinely unnerved. "You speak of the sacred Art as if it were a machine."

<br/>

"Everything is a machine if you look close enough," Mark said. "Magic isn't divine intervention, Vespera. It's an operating system. And right now, everyone in this world is using a clunky command-line interface with huge hardware safety buffers because nobody has bothered to write a cleaner runtime environment."

<br/>

Garin shook his head, pushing his empty bowl away. "I'm going to bed. Listening to you two makes my skull ache. Room four is ours, Mark. Don't let the mage turn you into a frog while I'm asleep."

<br/>

"I don't do frogs," Vespera said.

<br/>

Garin pointed at her as he stood. "See, that's what worries me. You said *don't*. You didn't say *can't*."

---

An hour later, the inn had quieted down. Garin was snoring from his cot on the far side of the room.

<br/>

It was not an ordinary snore. It had the volume and rhythm of a lumber mill running double shifts. Each one built to a rattling crescendo, paused just long enough for Mark to wonder whether the merchant had died, and then resumed with a wet snort that shook the shutters. Mark had once shared a hotel room with a sales engineer at a three-day tech conference in Las Vegas. He had believed, until tonight, that he'd survived the worst humanity had to offer.

<br/>

*"Fine,"* Mark thought. *"Background noise. Real deployments never happen in quiet rooms."*

<br/>

He sat barefoot on the cold, unvarnished pine floorboards near the small window, clad only in his linen trousers.

<br/>

Outside, the two moons cast pale silver and green light across the room. Down below, the street was quiet, save for the soft hum of the Aether streetlamps and, now and then, a dog barking back at Garin.

<br/>

Mark closed his eyes and began his breathwork. *Inhale for four, hold for four, exhale for four.*

<br/>

He didn't grab a stone. He didn't hold a stick. He placed both palms flat against the wooden floorboards, establishing a direct physical connection to the building's timber frame, which ran all the way down to the stone foundation set in the dirt.

<br/>

*"First problem: Hardware,"* Mark thought. *"I need a ground."*

<br/>

He felt the ambient Aether in the room—that familiar, static prickle in the air. Instead of pulling it into his chest and holding it, he envisioned his body as a simple wire. He drew a tiny trickle of Aether down through his shoulders, down his arms, and straight into the floorboards, letting the excess dissipate harmlessly into the building's structure.

<br/>

His arms tingled, a dull ache throbbing in his elbows, but there was no violent spark, no stinging shock. The energy flowed through him and out into the wood.

<br/>

Slowly, the floorboards beneath him grew pleasantly warm.

<br/>

*"Radiant floor heating,"* Mark noted. *"I paid a contractor eleven thousand dollars for this back in Raleigh, and it never once worked in the guest bathroom."*

<br/>

He allowed himself a small, smug smile. *"Grounding established. Current is stable."*

<br/>

Now came the hard part. The software problem.

<br/>

On the small wooden table beside his cot sat a single unlit tallow candle. He wanted to light the wick.

<br/>

Normally, a mage would hold a staff, point at the candle, and chant: *"Ignis-parvus, flamma orietur."* The spoken words would pace their mind, step by step.

<br/>

Mark refused to speak.

<br/>

Instead, he tried to construct a **pre-compiled mental model**.

<br/>

In his mind, he built a single, unified mental object—a compressed block of logic. He mapped the variables simultaneously:

<br/>

1. **Source:** Ambient Aether in a six-inch radius around his right hand.
2. **Conversion:** Friction to thermal output at around four hundred and fifty degrees Fahrenheit.
3. **Target:** The cotton wick four feet away.
4. **Safety:** Hard cut-off the moment ignition occurs.

<br/>

Holding all four variables in active memory without the pacing of spoken words felt like trying to balance a bowling ball on the tip of a pencil while calculating tax returns in his head.

<br/>

Then Garin hit a particularly operatic snore, and Mark's concentration wobbled.

<br/>

Variable three slipped.

<br/>

*Fssst.*

<br/>

The candle didn't so much as flicker. Instead, Garin's left boot, sitting beside the cot, let out a faint hiss and a thin curl of steam. The smell of hot, damp wool and forty miles of road filled the room like a chemical weapon.

<br/>

Garin snorted, mumbled, "Not the cheese," and rolled over.

<br/>

Mark stared at the boot. *"Targeting error,"* he thought grimly. *"Wrong object in scope. In my defense, it's a very large object."*

<br/>

He reset. Breathed. Rebuilt the block from scratch, this time pinning the target variable down so hard it felt like holding a door shut against a hurricane.

<br/>

His temples throbbed. A sharp, piercing headache bloomed behind his left eye. His 14-year-old brain screamed under the sheer cognitive load. Sweat broke out across his forehead, and his right arm began to tremble violently as the unchanneled Aether tried to break execution and scatter.

<br/>

*"Focus,"* Mark gritted out behind clenched teeth. *"Don't execute line-by-line. Run the compiled block."*

<br/>

He slammed the mental instructions together, releasing the grounded circuit and focusing the energy into a silent needle point directed at the candle.

<br/>

*Pfft.*

<br/>

A tiny, brilliant blue bead of light flashed on the tip of the candle wick. A thin wispy thread of grey smoke rose into the moonlight.

<br/>

The wick didn't catch fire—the energy output was too low, the focus too loose—but the heat had charred the thread black.

<br/>

Mark collapsed backward onto his elbows, gasping for air as if he'd just finished a five-mile trail run. His head pounded with a brutal tension headache, and his right hand felt numb, like he'd fallen asleep on it.

<br/>

He looked at the smoking candle wick in the moonlight, then down at his trembling hands.

<br/>

No staff. No incantation. Just a massive, exhausting spike of mental processing power.

<br/>

*"Technically,"* Mark thought, *"that's not a lit candle. That's a candle at five percent completion."* He wiped the sweat from his eyes, a tired grin breaking across his face. *"Brad would have called it 'lit' and announced it at the all-hands."*

<br/>

Across the room, Garin's boot was still steaming gently.

<br/>

*"Silent execution works,"* Mark thought, closing his eyes and letting his breathing settle. *"The compiler is slow, the cognitive load is brutal, and my mental RAM is way too small right now... but the code runs."*
