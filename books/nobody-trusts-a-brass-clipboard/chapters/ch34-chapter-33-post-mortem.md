## Chapter 33: Post-Mortem

<br/>

"Every incident," said Mark, "ends with a post-mortem."

<br/>

He was standing in front of the long whitewashed wall at the back of the Fish & Hammer, where, not so long ago, he had drawn a firewall in charcoal and written three questions underneath it. The firewall was still there. Torren had refused to let anyone wash it off. He said it was *historical*.

<br/>

Beside it, Mark had drawn three new columns, and labeled them in large, careful capitals:

<br/>

*WHAT WENT WELL.*

<br/>

*WHAT WENT WRONG.*

<br/>

*ACTION ITEMS.*

<br/>

It was three days after the cavern. The back room was full. Lyra on her stool, with Tilly on one wrist and L-2 on the other, because she had not yet been able to bring herself to take either of them off. Vespera in the good chair, with her hands healed and a faint silver line on the inside of her left wrist. Torren by the small forge, with his reading spectacles on, because this was an official meeting. Julian on a crate, sitting on it, for the first time, like it didn't owe him money. Garek in the doorway, with his knee strapped in a brace Lyra had made from leather and ash and two brass hinges, leaning on a stick. Legacy in the frame above him. Corwin on the long bench under the window, horizontal, as promised.

<br/>

And on a second crate, a little apart from everyone else, sitting very straight, in a plain dark coat, with his hands folded on his knees: Lord Aldric Thorne.

<br/>

He had come in at the start of the meeting, alone, on foot, and stood just inside the door looking around at the room with the faintly bewildered expression of a man who had never in his life attended a meeting he wasn't chairing. Nobody had known what to say to him. Then Torren had handed him a bowl of noodles, and he'd sat down on the nearest crate, and that, apparently, was that.

<br/>

"What's a post-mortem?" asked Torren.

<br/>

"It's what you do after something goes wrong," said Mark. "Or right. Or both. You get everyone in a room, and you go through what happened, and you write down what worked and what didn't, and what you're going to do differently next time." He tapped the wall. "And it's *blameless*."

<br/>

"What does *blameless* mean?" said Julian.

<br/>

"It means," said Mark, "we don't ask *who* broke it. We ask *why the system let it break*. Nobody gets in trouble for telling the truth. Not in here." He looked around the room. "That's the only rule."

<br/>

There was a short silence while everyone in the room tried to imagine a meeting in which nobody got in trouble for telling the truth.

<br/>

"I like that," said Vespera slowly.

<br/>

"So do I," said Torren. "That's a good rule. We should have that rule all the time."

<br/>

"We should," Mark agreed. "Most places don't." He picked up his charcoal. "Right. *What went well.*"

---

"Naming," said Torren at once.

<br/>

Mark looked at him.

<br/>

"*Naming*," Torren repeated firmly, folding his arms. "Bessie. Edna. Duchess. Gerald. Gerald's Cousin. *Grandmother*." He counted them off on his thick fingers. "*Project Bessie*. All very good names. All went very well."

<br/>

Mark considered objecting. Then he remembered the rule, and wrote *NAMING (Torren)* in the first column.

<br/>

"The DDoS," said a voice from the doorway.

<br/>

Pip had been leaning against Garek's good leg, with his arms folded in exact imitation of Garek's. He'd been made, by unanimous vote of the bus, Bus Master *permanent*, and had taken to standing near Garek in all official settings for reasons of dignity.

<br/>

"The clipboards," Pip said. "Everyone lining up. It *worked*. It couldn't skip anyone." He paused. "That was *my* idea."

<br/>

"It was a very good idea," said Mark sincerely, and wrote *DDoS (Pip)*.

<br/>

"The doorway," said Garek.

<br/>

Mark wrote *DOORWAY (Garek)*.

<br/>

"The *chair*," said someone from the yard outside, through the open door.

<br/>

Everyone turned. Brann, who had come by to have his shoulder looked at and had stayed to listen, was standing in the yard with his arms folded, nodding seriously.

<br/>

"The chair went well," Brann said. "Very clean strike. I've been thinking about it."

<br/>

Nobody argued. Mark wrote *CHAIR (Brann)*.

<br/>

"Grounding," said Vespera quietly.

<br/>

"Lyra," said Julian.

<br/>

"L-2," said Lyra, at the same moment, and then flushed, and then said, more firmly, "Well, it *did*."

<br/>

"It did," said Mark, and wrote them both down. Then, after a moment, under them, he wrote *IGNIS (screaming)*, and the room laughed.

---

"*What went wrong*," said Mark.

<br/>

The room went quiet.

<br/>

It was a different kind of quiet. The kind he knew from a hundred real post-mortems: the moment where everyone remembers that the point of the meeting is not only the good part.

<br/>

Lyra looked at him.

<br/>

"You registered," she said, "without asking the team."

<br/>

Mark stopped, with the charcoal raised.

<br/>

"You stood there," said Lyra, and her voice was steady, but her hands had tightened in her lap, "with the button in front of you. And I said no. And Julian said there had to be another way. And you said you'd run out of time to look. And you *pressed* it." She held his gaze. "You didn't ask. You didn't *vote*. We have a charter. We *vote*. You voted on whether *Julian* could join. And then you went and put yourself at the top of a list of people to be *held below forever*, by yourself, because you'd decided it was the right thing to do." She swallowed. "You didn't ask us."

<br/>

Mark stood in front of the wall for a long moment.

<br/>

It was blameless. He'd said so himself. Nobody gets in trouble for telling the truth.

<br/>

And it was true.

<br/>

"You're right," he said quietly.

<br/>

He turned to the wall, and wrote it in the second column, in the same careful capitals as everything else.

<br/>

*MARK REGISTERED WITHOUT ASKING THE TEAM.*

<br/>

He looked at it for a moment. Then, beneath it, smaller, he added:

<br/>

*Why the system let it break: Mark has spent forty years being the only person in the room who understood the system. Mark is not used to having a team. Mark is learning.*

<br/>

Lyra read it. Her hands, in her lap, slowly unclenched.

<br/>

"Is that blameless?" she asked.

<br/>

"It's honest," said Mark. "That's what blameless is *for*."

<br/>

"*Gerald*," said Torren, from the forge, very softly.

<br/>

Everyone looked at him.

<br/>

He was looking at the top shelf, where, in a place of honor next to Cheese the Fifth, sat a dull, dark grey orb the size of a man's two fists, on a small, carefully folded square of blue velvet that had once wrapped a key. Lyra had painted a small sign in front of it, at Torren's request. It said: *GERALD. WE SAID WE'D NEVER. HE DID GOOD.*

<br/>

"We charged him," Torren said. "We said we'd never charge him, and we charged him. And then he ran out." He sniffed. "That went wrong."

<br/>

Mark wrote *GERALD* in the second column.

<br/>

Then, after a moment, he drew a line from it across the wall, and wrote it in the first column too.

<br/>

Torren looked at that for a long time, and then blew his nose very loudly, and said nothing at all.

---

"There's one more thing," said Garek, from the doorway, "before action items."

<br/>

Everyone turned.

<br/>

Garek had shifted on his stick, so that he was facing, very deliberately, the crate where Lord Aldric Thorne sat with his bowl of noodles.

<br/>

"He's on the charter," Garek said. "Isn't he? Like the boy." He nodded at Julian. "*Former threat actor. Current asset. Probationary.*"

<br/>

"Lyra wrote him in yesterday," said Mark. "Pending."

<br/>

"Pending," Garek agreed. "Pending one thing." He pointed at the floor in front of him with his stick. "*Leg lift*."

<br/>

The room went completely silent.

<br/>

Lord Aldric Thorne looked up from his noodles.

<br/>

Then, very slowly, he looked at Julian.

<br/>

Julian had put his face in his hands.

<br/>

"It's the condition," said Garek. "The boy did one. Properly. Standing leg bent. Leaning forward." He waited. "Your lordship."

<br/>

For a long moment, Mark genuinely did not know what was going to happen. He watched Aldric look at the big, battered enforcer in the doorway, the man whose knee he had asked after so courteously in a candlelit study in the capital, whose name he had known before Mark had ever said it. He watched him look at the bowl of noodles in his hands. He watched him look around the room: the smith, the engraver, the mage, the boy on the bench, his own son with his face in his hands.

<br/>

Then Lord Aldric Thorne set down his noodles, rose from his crate, smoothed the front of his plain dark coat, stepped into the middle of the back room of the Fish & Hammer, and, with tremendous dignity and a perfectly straight back, raised his right knee to the height of his belt.

<br/>

He held it there, for a count of three.

<br/>

His standing leg was locked absolutely rigid. He was leaning very slightly backward. His face was a mask of composed, aristocratic concentration.

<br/>

Garek watched with the critical eye of a master craftsman.

<br/>

"Standing leg's locked," he said. "Bend it."

<br/>

Aldric bent it.

<br/>

"You're leaning back. Lean forward."

<br/>

Aldric leaned forward. One. Two. Three. He lowered his knee.

<br/>

Garek considered him for a very long time.

<br/>

"Better," he said at last. He raised one big hand, slowly, about a third of the way. "*Yes.* On probation. Do them every morning."

<br/>

Aldric inclined his head, gravely, exactly as he might have acknowledged a decree from the Concord. Then he sat back down on his crate, picked up his noodles, and resumed eating them with perfect composure.

<br/>

Julian had not taken his face out of his hands. But his shoulders were shaking.

<br/>

Vespera had turned to look out the window, and was holding very still.

<br/>

Corwin, on the bench, without opening his eyes, said: "*Mm*."

<br/>

"Is that a yes?" asked Aldric.

<br/>

"That's a *very significant* 'Mm,'" said Julian, into his hands. "Father. He doesn't give them to just anybody."

<br/>

Lord Aldric Thorne considered this.

<br/>

"Thank you, Corwin," he said, with great dignity.

<br/>

"Mm," said Corwin again.

---

"*Action items*," said Mark, when the room had recovered.

<br/>

He wrote them down one at a time, and the room grew quiet again as he did.

<br/>

*1. Concord acknowledgment. Thirty-one arrivals, pending release. Seven elders. One house with no name. One door with no handle. (Aldric knows the stair.)*

<br/>

*2. Rolling deployment. The Auditor is in Sector 05. The spec goes everywhere eventually. Some sectors will not want it. (Monitor.)*

<br/>

*3. The new silver moon. The Concord's own deadline. Days, not weeks.*

<br/>

*4. External attention: LOW. Not zero.*

<br/>

He looked at the last line for a moment. Then, beside it, he wrote one word.

<br/>

*Monitor.*

<br/>

"That's a lot," said Torren, looking at the list.

<br/>

"It is," said Mark. "It always is. That's what action items are." He put down the charcoal and looked around the room, at all of them. "But we've got a team. We've got a charter. We've got a message bus, and an infirmary, and a reference instrument, and an Auditor that's following *our* spec now. And we've got a few days." He paused. "In my old job, I'd have killed for a few days."

<br/>

"What will we do with them?" asked Julian.

<br/>

Mark thought about it.

<br/>

"First," he said, "I'm going to look at Garek's knee properly."

<br/>

From the doorway, Garek straightened, very slightly, and looked deeply gratified.

---

The days that followed were, Mark thought later, the closest thing to peace he'd had in either world.

<br/>

They weren't quiet. Nothing at the Fish & Hammer was ever quiet. But they were full of the kind of noise he'd almost forgotten existed: the noise of things *working*.

---

The Infirmary reopened the next morning.

<br/>

Lyra painted a new sign. It said *OPEN. GRIP TRAINERS AVAILABLE. DO YOUR LEG LIFTS.* and, underneath, smaller, in a different hand, *Heat therapy by appointment. Two practitioners.*

<br/>

The second practitioner arrived at dawn on the first day in a plain grey cloak, with the hood down, and stood just inside the yard gate looking at the line of patients with an expression of such nervous, transparent delight that Vespera, setting out the intake forms, had to turn away for a moment to compose herself.

<br/>

"Lady Seraphine," said Vespera.

<br/>

"Just Seraphine," said Lady Seraphine. "Please. In here." She looked down at her small pale hands. "I've never done it in front of anyone. Not once. Not in forty years." She swallowed. "I don't know if I'll be any good."

<br/>

"You kept a man's tea warm for thirty years without him ever noticing," said Vespera. "You'll be very good."

<br/>

Her first patient was a midwife from the river quarter.

<br/>

She was a small, brisk woman of about fifty, with strong forearms and tired eyes, and her name had been on the reconciliation list: *unsanctioned clean instrument*. She sat down on the crate in front of Seraphine and held out her hands, which were stiff and swollen at the knuckles from thirty years of difficult work, and Seraphine took them gently in her own.

<br/>

The warmth came. Low and steady and soft.

<br/>

The midwife closed her eyes.

<br/>

"Oh," she said, after a while. "Oh, that's what I was trying to do. With the crystal. For the mothers. Before the grey thing came." She opened her eyes and looked at Seraphine. "I never knew how to make it *gentle*. It always came out too hot, or not at all."

<br/>

"Small," said Seraphine. "That's the whole secret. Much smaller than you think. Smaller than anybody ever tells you." She smiled. "I'll show you."

<br/>

By the end of the week, the midwife was helping in the line. Before long, she had three students of her own.

<br/>

Seraphine came every morning. She never wore the hood again.

---

Archmage Ignis gave his first lecture under the new curriculum two days later.

<br/>

Mark was there. He'd been asked to attend, in writing, in a letter delivered by a nervous junior faculty member, signed with a flourish that took up half the page: *Your presence is requested, Apprentice Vance, so that you may observe the consequences of your actions.* He'd taken it as an invitation.

<br/>

The Grand Amphitheater was packed. Word had gone round. Every student in the Academy had heard some version of what had happened in the vaults, and none of the versions agreed, and all of them involved Ignis.

<br/>

At ten o'clock exactly, the Archmage swept onto the stage in his crimson robes, his beard freshly oiled, his great sapphire staff in his hand. He stopped at the lectern. He surveyed the hall with his old, supreme, condescending disdain.

<br/>

"*Silence*," he commanded.

<br/>

The hall went silent.

<br/>

"I am Archmage Ignis," he said. "Head of the Department of Advanced Aetheric Channeling. For twenty years, I have stood on this stage and taught you a fundamental truth of the Art." He paused. His jaw worked. "That truth," he said, with visible effort, "has been… *revised*."

<br/>

A murmur ran through the hall.

<br/>

Ignis raised a hand. It went silent again.

<br/>

"Aether," said Archmage Ignis, slowly and distinctly, as if each word were being extracted from him with tongs, "does not demand *suffering*."

<br/>

He let that hang in the air.

<br/>

"Aether," he said, "demands *payment*."

<br/>

He turned and planted the heel of his staff on the granite stage. The fine silver tail at the heel touched the stone. He drew himself up to his full height, and raised the staff, and began, at the top of his lungs, a forty-line incantation of kinetic elevation.

<br/>

The sapphire glowed. The fifty-pound iron anvil on its oak pedestal rose smoothly, silently, three feet into the air, and hung there.

<br/>

No sparks. No smoke. No screaming.

<br/>

Ignis held it there for ten seconds. Then he lowered it, gently, back onto the pedestal.

<br/>

"*Ow*," said Archmage Ignis, out of twenty years of habit.

<br/>

Then he stopped, and looked down at his hands, which did not hurt in the slightest, and his face went through a complicated series of expressions.

<br/>

In the front row, a first-year giggled.

<br/>

Ignis's head snapped round. The first-year went white.

<br/>

And then, from somewhere in the middle of the hall, very quietly, someone else said "*Ow*." Then someone else. Then a dozen students, all at once, softly, affectionately, the way a congregation says *amen*: "*Ow*."

<br/>

Ignis stood at the lectern and stared out at them.

<br/>

Mark, in the top corner of the highest row, held his breath.

<br/>

Then Archmage Ignis's beard twitched. And something happened at the corner of his mouth that nobody in the Academy had ever seen before, and that the first-years would talk about for the rest of the term.

<br/>

"*Sacred*," said Ignis gruffly, to the hall. "But painless. Page one. Take notes."

<br/>

And the lecture went on.

---

Tessa Fenn arrived at the Fish & Hammer the morning after that with a small bag over her shoulder and her chipped wooden rod in her belt, and asked to see the head engineer.

<br/>

Torren pointed at Lyra. Lyra pointed at Mark. Mark pointed back at Lyra.

<br/>

"Her," said Mark. "She designed the spec."

<br/>

Tessa looked at Lyra, who was fifteen and covered in silver polish, for a long, measuring moment.

<br/>

"I want to learn," said Tessa. "Properly. Not just the cooldown. All of it. Why it works." She took the rod out of her belt and looked at it. "I've been doing it by feel my whole life. I want to know the *why*."

<br/>

"You'll have to sweep," said Lyra. "And sort scrap. And carry crates. And learn trace ratios. And charge crystals until your arms ache."

<br/>

"I've done worse," said Tessa. "I've done all of that, actually. Just not here."

<br/>

Lyra considered her. Then she held out her hand.

<br/>

"Apprentice," she said. "The Fish & Hammer's first."

<br/>

Tessa shook it.

<br/>

Julian was in the yard that morning, as he was most mornings now, carrying things for Torren with his healing hands, and when he saw who'd arrived, he stopped in the middle of the yard with a crate of copper offcuts in his arms and went very still.

<br/>

Tessa saw him. Her face didn't change.

<br/>

Julian set the crate down. He walked over to her, slowly, and stopped in front of her, and Mark, watching from the workshop door, saw him take a breath.

<br/>

"I called your rod firewood," Julian said. "In front of the whole class. Last year."

<br/>

"I remember," said Tessa.

<br/>

"It was a terrible thing to say. You were doing it *right*. Better than any of us. Better than me." He swallowed. "I'm sorry."

<br/>

Tessa looked at him for a long moment.

<br/>

"There are forty crates of scrap in the back of the yard," she said. "They need carrying to the sorting bench."

<br/>

Julian blinked. "All right."

<br/>

"Every day," said Tessa. "For a week."

<br/>

Julian looked at her. Then, very slowly, he smiled.

<br/>

"All right," he said.

<br/>

He carried crates every morning. On the fourth, Tessa let him help her sort them. On the fifth, she called him by his name.

---

Nan Brask and Lord Aldric Thorne met that same week, in Nan's parlor above the warehouse, and Mark, who had arranged it, sat in the corner and was very careful not to breathe too loudly.

<br/>

They didn't say anything for a long time.

<br/>

Nan sat in her armchair with her knitting. Aldric sat across from her, very straight, in a plain wooden chair that Brann had brought up from the warehouse, with his hands folded on his knees. Behind Nan's chair, on the wall, hung the great knitted organizational chart of the Ironhead Syndicate, with its rows of little knitted boxes and its knitted lines and, in the box at the very top connected to everything else, a small knitted goat. On the table beside her sat a framed saucer.

<br/>

Aldric looked at the chart. He looked at it for a long time, carefully, box by box, line by line, the way he'd once looked at a column of carved names beside a Keeper door.

<br/>

Nan watched him look.

<br/>

*Click*, went her needles. *Click, click*.

<br/>

Then Aldric looked at the goat.

<br/>

"Most people," said Nan Brask pleasantly, "ask why there's a goat on it."

<br/>

Aldric didn't take his eyes off the chart.

<br/>

"I don't need to ask," he said. "It's the thing everything else depends on. That nobody would ever think to audit."

<br/>

Nan's needles stopped.

<br/>

She looked at him over her spectacles for a long, long moment. Mark, in the corner, held his breath.

<br/>

Then Nan Brask smiled. It was the warm, genuine, absolutely terrifying smile, and Mark had only ever seen her give it once before.

<br/>

"*Lord* Thorne," she said. "I believe we're going to get along."

<br/>

She picked up her knitting again. And Mark, watching, saw that she had started on something new: a small, careful square of grey wool, on which, very slowly, she was knitting a tiny silver ring.

<br/>

Two days later, a new box appeared on the chart, connected by a single knitted line to the goat.

---

Corwin's report on Thursday came in through the message bus at a quarter to ten that evening, relayed by Wren from a bench across the street from *The Silver Ladle*, where Corwin had been lying, with his arms folded and his eyes closed, since seven.

<br/>

Wren delivered it to the Fish & Hammer personally, standing in the center of the workshop, with the crisp gravity of a field officer.

<br/>

"Subjects arrived separately," she said. "Mage Vespera at seven. Inspector Valerius at five past. He'd been waiting round the corner since half past six. He was *nervous*." She consulted her memory. "They sat at his table. The one by the window. He did *not* read his book. He *brought* his book. It stayed in his pocket the whole time."

<br/>

"Go on," said Lyra, who was not spying, and who was sitting on the edge of her stool with her hands clasped.

<br/>

"They had the soup," said Wren. "It came cold." She paused for effect, the way Pip did. "The Mage warmed it. Both bowls. Right there at the table. Without even looking." Another pause. "The Inspector *laughed*."

<br/>

Lyra pressed both hands over her mouth.

<br/>

"They talked for two hours," Wren went on. "Corwin couldn't hear what about. But at nine o'clock, the Inspector reached across the table, and—" She stopped.

<br/>

"*And?*" said Lyra.

<br/>

Wren frowned, trying to get it exactly right.

<br/>

"Corwin says," she said carefully, "that's private. And he's not reporting it. And he went to sleep." She shrugged. "That's the report. Message bus, out."

<br/>

Lyra stared at her in outrage.

<br/>

Torren saluted. Mark found that he was smiling, and didn't try to stop.

---

The rest of it came in pieces, over those last days, the way good news usually does: not all at once, in a single triumphant moment, but in small bright fragments, one after another, like lamps coming on along a road.

<br/>

Jax came to the Infirmary a few mornings later.

<br/>

He didn't come for treatment. He came with his brother. Garek had been doing his knee exercises in the yard, slowly, carefully, with the brace on, three times a day, and one morning Jax walked through the gate behind him and stood in the corner by the water barrel with his arms folded, watching.

<br/>

He watched Garek do the leg lifts. He watched him do the stretches Mark had shown him for his back, the one that did a thing in the mornings. He watched him squeeze one of Lyra's grip trainers, ten times, with the wrist that a skinny boy from downriver had once locked behind his back in an alley.

<br/>

When Garek was finished, Jax unfolded his arms and walked slowly across the yard to where Mark sat at the intake table.

<br/>

He stood in front of it for a long moment.

<br/>

"My wrist," he said at last, gruffly, not quite meeting Mark's eyes. "The one you… in the alley. It still aches. When it's cold." He paused. "Does he—" He jerked his chin at Garek. "Does that thing he does with the leather. Would that…"

<br/>

Mark looked up at him. At the sixteen-year-old who had sneered at him in an alley and promised to break both his legs, and who was standing in front of him now, with his arms at his sides, asking.

<br/>

He reached under the table and took out a grip trainer.

<br/>

"Ten times," he said. "Every morning. Before you do anything else."

<br/>

Jax took it. He turned it over in his hands.

<br/>

"That's it?" he said.

<br/>

"That's it," said Mark. "And come back next week. I'll look at it properly."

<br/>

Jax nodded slowly. Then he turned and walked back across the yard, past his brother, who looked at him, and then at the grip trainer in his hand, and didn't say anything at all, but put one big hand on the boy's shoulder as he passed, and left it there.

---

Tobin Pell turned out to be Old Pell's grandson.

<br/>

Nobody had thought to ask. It came out at a noodle stall on the river, when Tobin, who had been made Academy node number two and took his duties with enormous seriousness, sat down at the counter beside an old fisherman who had been coming to the stall every night for a month, and the old man looked at him and said, "Tobin! Have you been eating properly?"

<br/>

"Grandad," said Tobin, mortified.

<br/>

Old Pell turned out to be very proud of him, and to see perfectly well in the dark, and to attribute both facts entirely to the glowing fish.

<br/>

Tobin ate a bowl of them that night, on his grandfather's insistence. The next day, he reported to the bus that he could see the outlines of furniture in a dark room, faintly, and that it was the most terrifying thing that had ever happened to him, and that he would like another bowl.

---

In the corner of the ceiling of Mark's narrow dorm room in the West Wing, the lentil spider, which had been on probation since his first week at the Academy and had grown, by Mark's estimate, to roughly the size of a large bean, was formally taken off probation that week.

<br/>

Mark informed her of the decision in writing, by holding up his ledger to the corner of the ceiling with the relevant line visible.

<br/>

She did not respond. Mark took this, as he did with all spiders, as acceptance of the terms.

---

Hollis Grey's family mill ran every day, without a single crystal burning out, and before the week was out his father walked all the way to the Fish & Hammer on foot, in his best coat, to shake Mark's hand, and Torren's, and Lyra's, and then, after some thought, Bessie's housing.

<br/>

Duchess got a hat.

<br/>

Nobody was entirely sure who gave it to her. It was a small, slightly lopsided knitted hat, grey wool, with two holes for the horns, and she wore it with great dignity around the Syndicate stables for the rest of the season. Torren maintained, to anyone who would listen, that it had been a gift from Nan Brask. Nan Brask, when asked, said only, *click, click*, and did not look up.

---

Lyra received her mastery near the end of the week.

<br/>

It came in a letter, sealed in the plain red wax of the provincial Chapterhouse, delivered to the Fish & Hammer by High Inspector Valerius in person. He handed it to her in the middle of the yard without ceremony, and then stood back with his hands clasped behind him, and Mark noticed that he did not look at Vespera at all, very carefully, the entire time, and that Vespera did not look at him, with equal care, and that both of them had gone faintly pink.

<br/>

Lyra broke the seal.

<br/>

She read it standing in the middle of the yard, with Tilly on one wrist and L-2 on the other, and her lips moved slightly as she read, and her face did several things at once.

<br/>

"*Master Engraver*," she said at last. Her voice was very small. "Of the province. Five years early." She looked up. "It says… it says the Chapterhouse workshop is adopting the new standard for all foci. Effective immediately." She swallowed. "It says the reference instrument is to be known in all records as… *the Lyra Pattern*."

<br/>

There was a short silence in the yard.

<br/>

"It was in the contract," said Torren slowly. "Aldric's. 'Lyra receives her mastery five years early.'" He frowned. "We didn't sign it."

<br/>

"No," said Valerius. "We didn't." He looked at Lyra. "The Chapterhouse examined the reference instrument independently. Three senior masters. For two days." The corner of his mouth moved. "They were quite upset by it. Then they were quite upset that they hadn't thought of it. Then they gave you your mastery." He paused. "You earned it. Nobody gave it to you."

<br/>

Lyra stood there with the letter in her hands.

<br/>

Then she turned and walked into the workshop without a word, and Mark, after a moment, followed her.

<br/>

She was standing at the workbench, in front of the empty space where, a long time ago, she had sat with a graver and a strip of silver and wept over a ruined bracelet in the dark. She was looking down at Tilly on her wrist.

<br/>

"Three weeks of evenings," she said quietly. "Then two nights on a step. And now it's got my *name* on it."

<br/>

"It's always had your name on it," said Mark. "Now it's just in writing."

---

Nan Brask's offer came the same afternoon. It always did, Mark reflected, at exactly the right moment. Nan had a gift for it.

<br/>

She came to the Fish & Hammer herself, with Brann carrying her chair, and sat in the yard by the intake table with her knitting, and when Lyra came out of the workshop, she looked up over her spectacles and said, pleasantly, as if continuing a conversation from a month ago:

<br/>

"My offer still stands, Master Engraver."

<br/>

Lyra stopped.

<br/>

"The Syndicate could use the head of the Lyra Pattern," said Nan. *Click, click.* "Production. Distribution. The whole province, to start. Your own workshop. Your own people. Forty-five percent." She paused. "Of *you*, this time. Not just the business."

<br/>

Lyra looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked across the yard: at Torren, at Bessie and Mabel and Dot and Gertrude humming in their row, at Tessa sorting scrap with Julian, at the line of patients waiting outside the Infirmary door, at Garek in the doorway with his brace and his stick, at Mark.

<br/>

"No," said Lyra. "Thank you."

<br/>

Nan's needles didn't stop.

<br/>

"I already have a job," said Lyra. She straightened. "I'm the head engineer of Project Bessie."

<br/>

There was a short pause.

<br/>

Mark, at the intake table, opened his mouth to point out that there was, technically, no such title in the charter. That he had not, technically, ever appointed anyone head engineer of anything. That the charter's organizational structure, such as it was, did not, technically—

<br/>

He closed it again.

<br/>

"She is," he said. "Head engineer."

<br/>

Lyra turned and looked at him. Something lit up in her face that he would remember for a very long time.

<br/>

Nan Brask looked from one of them to the other, over her spectacles.

<br/>

"*Noted*," she said, with satisfaction, and went back to her knitting.

---

Mark brought Mott a cup of tea that night.

<br/>

He hadn't been to the archive since the morning they'd come up out of the stairs. His disciplinary shifts had been quietly cancelled: Ignis had written a short, extremely stiff letter informing him that his "penance in the lower vaults" was "deemed complete," and that he should "consider his practical lab privileges restored," and then, at the very bottom, in a smaller, slightly shakier hand, *Page one. Sacred, but painless.* Mark had kept the letter.

<br/>

But at eight o'clock that night, out of habit, or something more than habit, he found himself walking down the archive stairs with a cup of tea in his hands.

<br/>

He'd made it himself, at the Fish & Hammer, in the small forge. Torren's tea, strong and dark and slightly smoky. He'd carried it all the way across the city with his hand cupped over the top. It wasn't very warm anymore.

<br/>

The archive was quiet. Immaculate. The leaning tower of plates in its corner under its broom. The long tables. And at the small desk by the door, wrapped in grey wool, with a new scarf, writing slowly in a fat ledger with a quill wedged into the crook of his palm, sat Archivist Mott.

<br/>

He looked up as Mark came in.

<br/>

"Ah," he said warmly. "Are you one of the new ones?"

<br/>

Mark stopped in front of the desk.

<br/>

He thought about all the nights he'd answered. *Yes. Yes. Yes.* Every night, for months, without knowing what the question was. And the last night, at the top of the stairs, before the carriage. *Yes.* And Mott, waiting. *I know.*

<br/>

He set the cup of tea down on the corner of the desk.

<br/>

"No," said Mark. "I'm a resident."

<br/>

Mott looked at him.

<br/>

He looked at him for a long time, through the thick, bottle-bottom spectacles, with his quill held motionless in his crooked hand. And Mark saw it again: the thing he'd seen in the archive the night Vespera first spoke the words aloud, and on the night before the carriage, and on the stair when Mott held out his candle. Something old and sharp and very clear, deep behind the glass.

<br/>

"Lovely," said Mott softly.

<br/>

And then, for the first time in all the time Mark had known him, he didn't go back to his ledger.

<br/>

He set down his quill. He reached out with both of his twisted, swollen hands, and wrapped them carefully around the cup of tea, and lifted it, and drank.

<br/>

He made a small, surprised sound.

<br/>

"*Warm*," said Mott.

<br/>

Mark looked at the cup. He'd carried it across the whole city. It had been barely lukewarm when he set it down.

<br/>

He looked at Mott's hands around it.

<br/>

Mott took another sip, and closed his eyes, and smiled.

<br/>

Mark didn't ask. He found, standing there in the quiet archive with the old man drinking his tea, that he didn't want to. Some systems, he'd learned, you could take apart and understand. And some you just let run, and were grateful that they did.

<br/>

"Goodnight, Archivist Mott," he said.

<br/>

"Goodnight," said Mott, without opening his eyes. "Mind you lock the old cases."

---

He walked north the next morning, the last day before the new silver moon.

<br/>

He didn't tell anyone where he was going. He left a note on the workbench, in English, which nobody could read, and then, underneath it, in Lyra's careful local script, which she'd written out for him the night before without asking why: *Back tonight. Don't name anything while I'm gone.*

<br/>

He walked out through the North Gate in the early morning, past the first lamp the Auditor had darkened, burning blue and steady now in the grey dawn, and up the long rutted road, and then off it, into the pines, following the sound of the water.

<br/>

It took him most of the morning. He didn't hurry.

<br/>

The forest was exactly as he remembered it. The fragrant pine brush. The overgrown fern beds. The game trail winding between them. He found the outcrop of slate near the water's edge, where he'd picked up a sharp triangular flake and pressed it against his forearm to prove he wasn't dreaming. He found the flat granite boulder where he'd sat down hard afterward, with the cold stone against his thin trousers, and looked up at two moons.

<br/>

He found the patch of moss where he'd woken up.

<br/>

It was just moss. Soft and green and damp, at the foot of a tall pine, a little way back from the riverbank. There was nothing to mark it. No scorch, no crater, no glyph glowing in the earth. Nothing at all to show that one morning, a few months ago, a sixty-year-old software engineer from Raleigh, North Carolina, had come up through the far side of the world and landed here, in a body that wasn't his, speaking a language he'd never learned.

<br/>

`[ARRIVAL: SECTOR 04 — RIVER, NORTH OF OAKHAVEN.]`

<br/>

He sat down on the granite boulder, and looked at the river.

<br/>

It was exactly as he remembered it, too. The churning Class IV rapid, smashing white foam against the giant boulders. The pillow waves on the left. The ugly undercut rock in the center slot. The wide, easy eddy line on the far bank. He'd mapped it in three seconds, that first morning, out of forty years of whitewater instinct, and told a fir tree that if this was his brain's final exit sequence, he'd give it a solid eight out of ten.

<br/>

It wasn't an exit sequence.

<br/>

He sat on the boulder with his hands on his knees, and for the first time since he'd woken up on that moss, he let himself think about Raleigh. Properly. All of it.

<br/>

He thought about the house. The home office, with its second monitor, burning a hole through his retinas at eleven forty-two on a Thursday night. The rain on the roof. The loblolly pines dripping in the backyard. He thought about the kitchen, and the coffee maker with its drip tray full of sediment, and the chair by the window where he used to read on Sunday mornings, and the stack of books on the floor beside it that he'd always meant to get to.

<br/>

He thought about the morning after.

<br/>

He'd never let himself think about the morning after. Not once. Not in all these months. He'd thought about the *night*: the chest, the neck, the copper, the dark. The stroke. He'd turned it over and over, and filed it, and told himself he understood it.

<br/>

He'd never once thought about the *morning*.

<br/>

Who had found him? Had anyone? Had his phone kept buzzing on the desk, Slack notifications piling up, until someone at the company got worried enough to call someone else? Had someone come to the door? A neighbor? The police? Had they found him slumped over the keyboard with his forehead on the backspace key and a screen full of *b*s?

<br/>

Or had they found an empty chair?

<br/>

Was there a stone somewhere in North Carolina with his name on it? Was there a bed in an ICU, with a machine breathing beside it, and a body in it that had been sixty years old and had bad knees and arthritic hands and a shoulder that ached when it rained? Was someone sitting beside that bed? Was *anyone*?

<br/>

Was there a funeral? Who came? Did Brad come?

<br/>

He found, to his surprise, that he hoped Brad had come.

<br/>

`[ORIGIN STATUS: UNKNOWN.]`

<br/>

He sat on the boulder, and the river ran on in front of him, white and loud and cold, and he didn't take anything apart. He didn't troubleshoot. He didn't reach for a metaphor or a log line or a diagram on a whitewashed wall. He didn't try to fix it.

<br/>

He just sat there, and let it be unknown, and grieved.

<br/>

He grieved for the house, and the chair by the window, and the books he'd never read. For forty years of work that someone else was going to have to explain to Brad. For the knees and the shoulder and the hands that had clicked on the keys like dry twigs. For the life he'd had, and hadn't known he was going to lose, and still didn't know if he'd lost.

<br/>

He didn't know how long he sat there. A long time. The sun moved across the river. The shadows of the pines swung slowly around the boulder.

<br/>

When he was finished, or as finished as he was going to be, he wiped his face with the back of his hand.

<br/>

Then he said it out loud. Quietly. To the river.

<br/>

"I'm not waiting," Mark said. "For a diagnosis. There isn't going to be one."

<br/>

The river didn't answer.

<br/>

"I live here now," he said.

---

On the far bank of the river, something moved in the thicket.

<br/>

He looked up.

<br/>

It burst through the brush exactly the way it had that first morning: a horrific cross between an angry wild boar and a black bear, six feet tall at the shoulder, with four curved obsidian-black horns curling out of its skull and small red eyes. It stopped at the edge of the far bank and stood there, steaming faintly in the afternoon light.

<br/>

Mark didn't move.

<br/>

The beast looked at him across the river for a long moment.

<br/>

Then it lowered its great horned head, and waded a little way into the shallows at the edge of the eddy line, and drank from the river. Slowly. For a long time. When it was finished, it raised its head again and looked across at him once more, with its small red eyes, as if taking note of something.

<br/>

Then it turned, and went back into the trees, and was gone.

<br/>

Mark sat very still on the boulder, and watched the place where it had been.

---

He stayed until the moons came up.

<br/>

The light went gold, and then red, and then grey, and the river went dark, and then, one after another, over the tops of the pines, the two moons rose: the great pale green one, and the small, cratered, brilliant silver one. They hung there over the water, side by side, exactly as they had the first afternoon, when he'd stared at the second one and sighed and said *either I have the most over-engineered dying brain in human history, or I'm actually standing in the dirt*.

<br/>

He was standing in the dirt.

<br/>

He looked up at them for a long time.

<br/>

And out of forty years of habit, without deciding to, his right hand started to rise toward the bridge of his nose. To push up a pair of reading glasses that had been gone since the morning he woke up on the moss.

<br/>

Halfway there, he noticed.

<br/>

He looked at his hand, hanging in the air in front of his face, in the green and silver light. Smooth. Young. Unscarred. Not quite his. Not quite anyone else's, either.

<br/>

He didn't finish the gesture.

<br/>

He let his hand fall, slowly, back onto his knee, and left it there.

<br/>

Then he got up off the boulder, and turned away from the river, and walked home.

---

It was late when he got back to the Fish & Hammer.

<br/>

The lamps were on in every street. Blue and steady and quiet, all the way down from the North Gate. The armory yard was dark except for the one over the gate and a single lantern hanging in the workshop doorway, where someone had left it burning for him.

<br/>

Bessie, Mabel, Dot, and Gertrude were humming in their row beside the cold forge. Edna was humming inside. In the corner of the doorframe, Legacy sat in the center of a freshly repaired web, catching the lantern light.

<br/>

Mark sat down on the crate by the workshop door, under the lantern, and took out his ledger.

<br/>

He opened it to the first page.

<br/>

*System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance.*

<br/>

*Subject: Aether Dynamics & Non-Verbal Execution Protocols.*

<br/>

*Log 01: Grounding reduces physical thermal load.*

<br/>

He'd written it on a bench outside a scribe's shop, with three coppers' worth of charcoal, the morning after he discovered that nobody in this world could read his handwriting. He'd been very pleased about that. *Completely encrypted out of the box.*

<br/>

He looked at it for a long time. Then he turned to the end of the ledger, past twenty-two logs and a few months and two worlds, to the first blank page, and uncapped his charcoal.

---

He read it through once, slowly, the whole way down.

<br/>

Then he sat for a while on the crate in the lantern light, with the ledger open on his knee, listening to the blowers hum.

<br/>

He didn't hear the footsteps until they were right beside him. Slow, heavy, uneven footsteps, with the soft tap of a stick on the cobbles between each one.

<br/>

Garek lowered himself, carefully, with a grunt, onto the edge of the next crate along, and stretched his braced leg out in front of him, and sat there for a moment, looking out at the dark yard.

<br/>

"You were gone all day," he said.

<br/>

"I went to the river."

<br/>

Garek nodded slowly, as if this were a perfectly reasonable thing for a person to do, and didn't ask anything else about it. That was one of the things Mark had come to value about Garek: he never asked about the river.

<br/>

They sat together in the lantern light for a while. Bessie hummed.

<br/>

Then Garek cleared his throat.

<br/>

"Mark," he said.

<br/>

"Mm."

<br/>

"About my knee."

<br/>

Mark looked at him.

<br/>

"Do the lifts," he said.

<br/>

Garek nodded seriously.

<br/>

"Twice?"

<br/>

"Three times."

<br/>

Garek nodded again, satisfied, and settled back against the doorframe. Above his head, Legacy shifted one leg.

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"And the back," said Garek, after a moment, to the yard. "It does a thing. In the mornings."

<br/>

Mark looked at him.

<br/>

Garek looked straight ahead, out at the dark yard and the humming blowers, with the expression of a man who has been saying the same sentence for months and fully expects to go on saying it for years.

<br/>

Mark looked up at the sky over the armory yard, where the two moons hung over the rooftops, one green, one silver.

<br/>

Then he looked down at the ledger on his knee, open to Log 23, to *Status: Home.*

<br/>

He turned the page.

<br/>

There was a fresh, blank page underneath. He smoothed it flat with the side of his hand. He picked up his charcoal stick. And at the top of the page, in neat, small English cursive, he wrote:

<br/>

*Intake Form.*

<br/>

*Patient: Garek.*

<br/>

*Complaint: Back. Does a thing in the mornings.*

<br/>

"Right," said Mark. "Tell me about the thing."

---

### *Stinger: Deep Vault 3 — Oakhaven Chapterhouse*

<br/>

Fifty feet beneath the cobblestones, in the cold and the dark, the Oakhaven Anchor stood exactly as it had for five hundred years.

<br/>

Six feet of seamless, unpolished obsidian, in the center of a room full of tax records. The tip of something much larger. A kiosk in a lobby.

<br/>

Its face was quiet. The red glyph was gone. The gold glyph was gone. In their place, near the top of the pillar, a single glyph glowed a clean, steady, silver-white: the color of the light under a boy's palm on a silver plate, and the wave that had rolled up through the trunks into every lamp in the city.

<br/>

Beneath it, small, in the corner of the stone, where nobody ever looks, sat a single line.

<br/>

`[EXTERNAL ATTENTION: LOW]`

<br/>

It had read *low* for eleven days.

<br/>

It went on reading *low* for a long time that night. Through the hours after midnight, when the lamps in the streets above burned blue and steady, and the blowers hummed in the armory yard, and an old man in a grey scarf sat on the cold steps beneath the Academy gate. Through the slow, quiet hours before dawn, when the city slept.

<br/>

Then, a little before three in the morning, it flickered.

<br/>

Once.

<br/>

The line went dark. And when it lit again, it said something else.

<br/>

`[EXTERNAL CONTACT]`

<br/>

The vault was utterly silent.

<br/>

And beneath it, slowly, a new line began to write itself into the obsidian.

<br/>

It was not First Era script. It was not High Imperial. It was not any glyph or rune or character that the Anchor had ever displayed, in five hundred years of red and gold and silver light. It was a looping, joined-up, unfamiliar hand: small, neat letters, slanting slightly to the right, running together one after another, exactly the way they ran across the pages of a leather-bound ledger that nobody in this world had ever been able to read.

<br/>

Plain English cursive.

<br/>

It wrote itself one letter at a time, the way a cursor writes on an empty screen.

<br/>

*Hello, World.*

<br/>

The light pulsed once, softly, in the dark.

<br/>

And waited.

*End of Book One.*
