## Chapter 22: The Message Bus

<br/>

Mark designed the network on his brass clipboard the next morning, sitting on the workshop's front step beneath the carved fish holding a hammer.

<br/>

It was, he had to admit, a beautiful diagram. Circles for nodes. Arrows for routes. Little boxes for relay points at the market, the docks, the timber yards, the noodle stall, and the Academy gate. In the center, in large letters, he'd written MESSAGE BUS, and beside it, in smaller letters, *Bus Master: Pip.*

<br/>

Pip stood in front of him, freckled face solemn, hands clasped behind his back like a soldier awaiting orders. Behind him stood six other errand boys and two errand girls, ranging in age from about nine to thirteen, all of whom Pip had personally recruited that morning and all of whom were staring at the clipboard with deep suspicion.

<br/>

"Right," Mark said. "Welcome, everyone. This is your onboarding."

<br/>

"What's onboarding?" asked a very small boy with a runny nose.

<br/>

"It's when someone explains your job to you," Mark said, "badly, while you pretend to understand."

<br/>

The small boy nodded. This seemed to match his experience of work so far.

<br/>

Mark walked them through it. Messages would move from node to node along fixed routes. Each runner would cover one route. Pip would sit at the center, collect everything, and pass the important things on. Nobody was to tell anybody outside the bus what they carried. Nobody was to write anything down.

<br/>

"Most of us can't write," Pip pointed out.

<br/>

"Then that part will be easy," Mark said.

<br/>

"What do we get paid?" asked one of the girls, a sharp-eyed eleven-year-old named Wren, who had clearly been sent by the others to ask.

<br/>

Mark had thought about this carefully. Coin was traceable. Coin got stolen. Coin got the attention of older, bigger children.

<br/>

"Noodles," he said. "From Hamm's stall by the river. One bowl per completed route, every day. Plus a fish on rest days."

<br/>

There was a pause while the errand children conferred in whispers.

<br/>

"The *glowing* fish?" Wren asked.

<br/>

"The glowing fish."

<br/>

The errand children looked at each other. Then, as one, they nodded, the way a board approves a budget it had already decided to approve.

<br/>

"Very standard compensation structure for junior staff," Mark said, making a note. "Item two. Code words."

<br/>

He'd thought about this too. Messages needed to be short, quick, and meaningless to anyone listening in. So he'd built a small vocabulary.

<br/>

"'Gerald,'" Mark said, "means *danger*. If you see something bad—Thorne people, Enforcers, anyone asking about me or the workshop—you find Pip and you say 'Gerald.'"

<br/>

"Who's Gerald?" asked the small boy with the runny nose.

<br/>

"Gerald was a ballast block," Mark said. "He died in service."

<br/>

The children absorbed this with grave respect.

<br/>

"'Cheese,'" Mark went on, "means *Lyra needs to know right away*."

<br/>

"Why cheese?"

<br/>

"Trust me," Mark said, "it'll make sense the first time you meet her."

---

It took the message bus less than a day to start using "Gerald" for everything.

<br/>

By noon, Mark had received three Geralds. The first was a genuine report of two Chapterhouse Enforcers buying bread near the market. The second was that it had started to rain. The third, delivered breathlessly by the small boy with the runny nose, turned out after some questioning to mean that Hamm's noodle stall had run out of green onions.

<br/>

"That's not a Gerald," Mark told him.

<br/>

"It *felt* like a Gerald," the boy said.

<br/>

Mark considered this. In forty years, he had sat through countless incident reviews where somebody had escalated a minor issue to the highest severity because it *felt* urgent. He had never once successfully argued anyone out of it.

<br/>

"Fair enough," he said, and wrote *Gerald (Onion)* in his notes as a subcategory.

<br/>

Pip, meanwhile, took his promotion more seriously than anyone had ever taken anything. By the afternoon he had developed a sign-off. He delivered every report, however small, with his hand raised in a crisp salute, and ended each one with the words:

<br/>

"Message bus, *out*."

<br/>

He did this so many times that Torren, passing by the workshop door on his way to the forge, began saluting back without thinking, and then looked deeply confused about why he was doing it.

---

The second recruit was harder to approach, mostly because he was asleep.

<br/>

Mark found Corwin that afternoon in his usual spot in the Academy courtyard, stretched out full-length on a marble bench in a patch of sun, arms folded across his chest, eyes closed.

<br/>

"Corwin," Mark said. "Can I ask you something?"

<br/>

"You're going to anyway," Corwin murmured.

<br/>

"I'm building a network," Mark said. "People who keep their eyes open and pass on what they see. I could use someone inside the Academy."

<br/>

Corwin opened one eye.

<br/>

"You want me," he said slowly, "to keep my eyes *open*."

<br/>

"Figuratively."

<br/>

"Oh, thank the Ancestors." Corwin closed the eye again. "Fine. What do you want to know?"

<br/>

"I was hoping you'd start keeping track of—"

<br/>

"Professor Hale and Professor Doran are feuding about a stolen inkwell. Archmage Ignis has been writing angry letters to Inspector Valerius every day this week and hasn't had a single reply. The head cook is watering down the soup to sell the extra stock on the side. Three of Julian's friends have stopped sitting with him at dinner." Corwin yawned. "And Julian hasn't slept properly since the night of your... *seismic event*. He walks the West Wing corridors at night. Back and forth. Turning that ring."

<br/>

Mark stared at him.

<br/>

"How," he said, "do you know all that?"

<br/>

"Nobody notices someone lying down," Corwin said. "People walk right past me and say *everything*. Professors. Students. Servants. They think I'm asleep." He paused. "Usually I *am* asleep. But I'm a very light sleeper."

<br/>

Mark looked at Corwin with something close to awe.

<br/>

"You've been running surveillance," he said, "for *three years*. Lying on a bench."

<br/>

"I've been lying on a bench for three years," Corwin corrected. "The surveillance just happens around me." He shifted into a more comfortable position. "One condition."

<br/>

"Name it."

<br/>

"Protected nap rights. Nobody wakes me before noon, ever. And when the bus needs me, it sends the small one with the runny nose. He's quiet. I like him."

<br/>

"Done," Mark said.

<br/>

He walked back across the courtyard, took out his clipboard, and wrote, with considerable satisfaction: *Corwin — Horizontal Surveillance Asset. Coverage: entire Academy. Cost: naps.*

<br/>

It was, he thought, the best hire he'd ever made.

---

The meeting with the Ironhead Syndicate took place two nights later, in a warehouse at the edge of the timber yards.

<br/>

The whole team went.

<br/>

Lyra came because, as she put it, "If it's about goats, someone sensible should be there." Torren came because he did not trust Garek and wanted to be standing close enough to hit him if necessary. And Vespera came in disguise.

<br/>

Her disguise was her usual indigo cloak, with the hood pulled up.

<br/>

"Nobody will recognize me," she said firmly, as they walked down the dark lane between the lumber stacks.

<br/>

"Vespera," Lyra said, "you're the only person in Oakhaven who wears that color."

<br/>

"Nobody will recognize me," Vespera repeated, a little less firmly, and pulled the hood lower.

<br/>

The warehouse was huge, dim, and smelled strongly of sawdust and, unmistakably, goat. Two enormous men stood at the door. They looked at Mark, then at Torren, then at Vespera's hood.

<br/>

"Evening, Mage Vespera," one of them said politely.

<br/>

Vespera's shoulders sagged.

---

Garek was waiting just inside.

<br/>

He looked much as Mark remembered him: towering, broad, bearded, with the broken nose and the studded leather jerkin. But he stood differently now. He kept his right arm slightly bent and close to his body, and when Mark stepped forward and offered his hand, Garek hesitated a fraction of a second before shaking it.

<br/>

His grip was strong. But his wrist was stiff, and Mark saw him wince, just briefly, before he let go.

<br/>

"Vance," Garek rumbled.

<br/>

"Garek."

<br/>

Behind his brother stood Jax, sullen and lean. A faint pale scar marked the center of his forehead where a thrown rivet had struck him months ago. When Mark glanced at him, Jax's hand drifted up and touched it.

<br/>

Mark nodded to him politely. Jax glared back. His hand stayed on his forehead.

<br/>

"The boss is waiting," Garek said, and led them deeper into the warehouse.

---

At the back of the building, between towering stacks of timber, sat a desk made from two barrels and a plank. Behind it, in a comfortable armchair that looked like it had been stolen from a much nicer house, sat the head of the Ironhead Syndicate.

<br/>

She was perhaps five feet tall. She was perhaps eighty years old. She had silver hair pinned up in a tight bun, a pair of half-moon spectacles perched on the end of her nose, and a pair of knitting needles clicking steadily in her lap. A ball of grey wool sat in a basket at her feet.

<br/>

She did not look up when they approached. She finished her row.

<br/>

Then she set the needles down, looked at the four of them over the top of her spectacles, and smiled pleasantly.

<br/>

"Sit," she said.

<br/>

They sat, on crates. Nobody had told them to. It simply hadn't occurred to any of them not to.

<br/>

"I'm Nan Brask," the old woman said. "I run things in the lower town. You're the boy who broke my enforcer's wrist."

<br/>

"Sprained," Mark said. "And to be fair, he was trying to break my knees."

<br/>

"That was policy," Nan Brask said, nodding. "Nothing personal." She looked at Torren. "Smith." At Lyra. "Workshop girl." At Vespera's hood. "Mage Vespera."

<br/>

Vespera made a small, defeated noise and pushed back the hood.

<br/>

On the wall behind Nan's armchair, Mark noticed, hung a large rectangle of knitted wool. It took him a moment to realize what it was. Rows of little knitted boxes, connected by knitted lines, each box stitched with a tiny knitted symbol: a fist, a cart, a dagger, a goat. Nan's name sat in a box at the top, in red yarn.

<br/>

It was an org chart.

<br/>

She had *knitted an org chart*.

<br/>

Mark looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at Nan Brask, and he felt something he hadn't felt since he'd left Raleigh: the instant, wordless recognition of a fellow executive.

<br/>

"That's a very clean reporting structure," he said.

<br/>

Nan's eyes sharpened behind her spectacles. "Most people," she said, "ask why there's a goat on it."

<br/>

"I assumed we'd get to the goat," Mark said.

<br/>

Nan Brask smiled. It was a genuinely warm smile, and it was absolutely terrifying.

<br/>

"Oh," she said, "we'll get to the goat."

---

But first, she wanted to talk about crystals.

<br/>

"For eleven years," Nan said, picking up her knitting again, "the Ironhead Syndicate has been the only place in the lower town to buy a mana-crystal for less than the Chapterhouse's two silver bits. We bring them in from outside the province. Cheap. Quiet. Good business."

<br/>

*Click, click, click*, went the needles.

<br/>

"And then, about two months ago," Nan went on pleasantly, "a smith in the south armory yard started selling recharged crystals at half our price. Good ones. Fully glowing. My customers stopped coming." She looked at Torren over her spectacles. "I've lost a great deal of money, Master Torren."

<br/>

Torren shifted on his crate. "Free market," he said gruffly.

<br/>

"Indeed," said Nan Brask. *Click, click.* "I'm a great believer in the free market. That's why I haven't burned your forge down."

<br/>

There was a short silence.

<br/>

"Appreciated," said Torren.

<br/>

"You're welcome."

<br/>

Mark leaned forward. "You said you bring crystals in from outside the province. How do you get them past the North Gate tariff?"

<br/>

Nan smiled again. "Ah," she said. "*Now* we get to the goat."

---

The tariff, she explained, was the key.

<br/>

Mark remembered it from the decree in the town plaza on his first week: a three-percent tariff on ore, textiles, and spirits, and a further levy of *one copper per head on all goods arriving by goat*.

<br/>

"Per head," Nan said. "Per *goat*. Not per item. A goat carrying one crystal pays one copper. A goat carrying three hundred crystals—"

<br/>

"—also pays one copper," Mark finished slowly. "It's a pricing exploit. The tariff charges per container instead of per unit."

<br/>

"I have no idea what that means," Nan said, "but I like how you said it."

<br/>

"So you load your goats," Lyra said, "with as many crystals as they can carry."

<br/>

"In the saddlebags," Nan said. "In the packs. Sewn into the blankets. Braided into their beards, on one occasion, though that was a bit much." *Click, click.* "Thirty goats a month come through the North Gate. Thirty coppers in tariffs. Several thousand crystals."

<br/>

Mark found himself genuinely impressed. It was elegant. It was the kind of loophole he'd seen people exploit in cloud billing models for years.

<br/>

"The Regional Magistrate," Vespera said slowly, "wrote that tariff."

<br/>

"The Regional Magistrate's nephew," Nan corrected, "wrote that tariff. The Regional Magistrate's nephew owes me a great deal of money." *Click, click.* "Every town has a goat problem, Mage Vespera. Ours just happens to be *profitable*."

<br/>

*"The goats,"* Mark thought, dazed. *"The goat tariff. Harlan's forgotten fourth rule. The goat line on the decree. I knew it. I always knew it was the goats."*

<br/>

"So that's the business," Nan Brask said. "Now here's the problem."

<br/>

She set down her knitting and looked at Garek.

<br/>

"Show them," she said.

---

Garek led them to a pen at the far end of the warehouse, behind a wall of timber.

<br/>

There were about twenty goats inside. They looked like ordinary goats. Then Mark looked closer and realized they didn't.

<br/>

Every single one of them had fur standing straight out from its body in a great frizzy halo, as if they'd all been rubbed with a wool blanket and then struck by lightning. Every so often, as two goats bumped flanks, a tiny blue spark snapped between them with a sharp *crack*, and both goats bleated indignantly.

<br/>

In the far corner, one particularly large brown goat stood perfectly still, chewing calmly. Its hooves were roughly an inch off the ground.

<br/>

"Is that goat," Lyra said faintly, "*floating*?"

<br/>

"Only when she's excited," Garek said.

<br/>

"What happens when she's very excited?"

<br/>

Garek, Jax, and the two enormous door guards all exchanged a look of shared trauma.

<br/>

"Don't," Garek said, "get her very excited."

<br/>

"They've been eating the crystals," Nan Brask explained, appearing at Mark's elbow with her knitting. "On the long walk in from the province border. They chew through the saddlebags. The handlers can't stop them. And now..." She gestured at the pen. "They zap people. My handlers can't touch them without getting knocked off their feet. Three men have quit. One man's hair hasn't lain flat in a month."

<br/>

"It's me," said Jax bitterly. "I'm the man."

<br/>

Mark looked at Jax. Jax's hair, he now noticed, was standing up in a stiff frizzy crest, exactly the way Mark's own had in the coal yard in his first week in Oakhaven.

<br/>

"Dry air," Mark said sympathetically.

<br/>

Jax glared at him with pure, distilled hatred.

<br/>

Mark turned back to the pen, his mind working.

<br/>

"The crystals in their stomachs are trickle-charging off the ambient Aether," he said slowly. "Same thing that's been happening to the fish in the river. But these goats aren't grounded. They're on hooves, standing on hay, in a timber building. All that charge builds up with nowhere to go." He nodded toward the floating goat. "She's carrying so much charge she's started repelling the ground."

<br/>

"Can you fix it?" Nan asked.

<br/>

"Probably," Mark said. "You've accidentally invented living capacitors."

<br/>

"Is that bad?"

<br/>

"It's not *great*," Mark admitted.

---

He wanted to examine the goats more closely. He wanted to understand the charge distribution before trying anything. He opened his mouth to say so.

<br/>

Unfortunately, at that exact moment, Torren leaned over the fence for a better look at the floating goat, cupped his hands around his mouth, and said, with enormous warmth:

<br/>

"*Hello*, gorgeous!"

<br/>

The floating goat stopped chewing.

<br/>

Its ears pricked up. Its eyes, which were a strange, unsettling yellow, fixed on Torren.

<br/>

And its fur, already standing on end, began to *crackle*.

<br/>

"Oh no," said Garek.

<br/>

"What?" said Torren. "What did I do?"

<br/>

"You got her *excited*."

<br/>

The goat rose another six inches off the ground.

<br/>

Then she charged.

---

Later, nobody could agree on exactly how long the next four minutes lasted. Garek swore it was an hour. Lyra insisted it was closer to two. Mark, who had a professional interest in incident timelines, eventually reconstructed it to roughly four minutes, though he admitted it was hard to be precise while being pursued by a floating goat.

<br/>

She cleared the fence in a single, weightless bound, sailing over it like a furry, horned soap bubble. She landed on a stack of crates, which exploded in a shower of blue sparks. She bounced off the crates, ricocheted off a support beam, and came down directly on top of one of the enormous door guards, who went rigid, made a noise like a kettle, and fell over backward.

<br/>

"*Duchess!*" Torren shouted. "Her name is *Duchess*!"

<br/>

"*She doesn't have a name!*" Garek roared.

<br/>

"She *does now*!"

<br/>

Duchess caromed across the warehouse in a crackling blue arc, zapping everything she touched. She knocked over a rack of tools. She clipped the second door guard, who also made a kettle noise and fell over. She hit a stack of lumber, and Garek dived behind it with the speed of a man who had learned this lesson before.

<br/>

Jax, who had been standing frozen in the center of the floor, got hit twice, once on the way out and once on the way back. The second time, his hair went from frizzy to vertical.

<br/>

"Mark," Lyra said, very calmly, from behind a crate. "What's the plan?"

<br/>

"Working on it."

<br/>

"Work *faster*."

<br/>

Mark ducked behind the lumber stack as Duchess sailed overhead, trailing sparks—and found himself face to face with Garek, crouched low, his back pressed against the timber.

<br/>

Garek had his right arm extended straight out in front of him, palm up. With his left hand, he was gently pulling the fingers of his right hand back toward his body, stretching the muscles of the forearm.

<br/>

It was the stretch. Mark had described it to him at the door barely an hour earlier, and Garek had flatly refused to try it.

<br/>

Garek froze.

<br/>

He and Mark looked at each other in silence, in the middle of a goat-based disaster, behind a pile of lumber.

<br/>

"I wasn't doing anything," Garek said.

<br/>

"Hold it for thirty seconds," Mark said. "Not ten. Thirty."

<br/>

Garek glared at him.

<br/>

Then, very deliberately, still glaring, he held it for thirty seconds.

---

The stretch had happened like this.

<br/>

When Garek had first shaken his hand at the door, Mark had seen the wince. He had noticed the way the bigger man kept his arm tucked, the way he favored it, the way he flexed his fingers stiffly every few minutes without seeming to notice. He knew what it was the moment he saw it. Thirty years ago, he'd had nearly the exact same tendon injury from hanging off a climbing wall for too long, and he'd spent three miserable months working it back to full strength.

<br/>

And, if he was being honest, he'd felt a small, uncomfortable twinge of responsibility. The thermal zap and the foot-sweep in the timber-yard lane had been entirely justified. They had also been his.

<br/>

So, on the walk to Nan's desk, Mark had fallen into step beside Garek and said, quietly: "Your wrist's still stiff."

<br/>

Garek had given him a look that could have curdled milk. "What's it to you?"

<br/>

"I had one just like it," Mark said. "Years ago. A healer taught me some exercises. They fixed it completely."

<br/>

"Exercises," Garek repeated, as if Mark had suggested he take up embroidery.

<br/>

"Stretches, mostly. Arm straight out, palm up, pull the fingers back gently for thirty seconds. Then palm down, same thing. Slow circles. And if you can find a bucket of dry grain, you push your hand into it and squeeze, over and over. It builds the grip back."

<br/>

Garek had stopped walking and turned to face him.

<br/>

"Why," he said slowly, "would *you* help *me*?"

<br/>

"Because it's a good stretch."

<br/>

"Is it a trick?"

<br/>

"No."

<br/>

"Is it a curse?"

<br/>

"It's not a curse."

<br/>

Garek's eyes narrowed. "If I put my hand in that grain bucket," he said, "does something *bite* it?"

<br/>

"Nothing bites it," Mark said patiently. "It's grain. It's the least threatening substance in the entire warehouse."

<br/>

"Then why would a healer charge for it?"

<br/>

Mark had paused. In his head, he'd reached for a number. Six hundred dollars in co-pays, three months of appointments. He started trying to convert it. Two coppers for four hours of coal hauling. Five coppers a day for scrap sorting. Silver to copper at... he didn't actually know the exchange rate. Somewhere between ten and a hundred? Possibly it varied by district? Possibly there was a goat-based component?

<br/>

He gave up halfway through.

<br/>

"Those exercises," Mark said instead, "cost me more silver than you'd make in half a year of breaking knees."

<br/>

Garek's eyebrows had risen. For the first time, he looked at Mark with something other than suspicion. Something that expensive couldn't possibly be a trick.

<br/>

Then he'd shaken his head firmly.

<br/>

"I'm not doing stretches," Garek said. "I'm an enforcer."

<br/>

And he'd walked off toward Nan's desk without another word.

<br/>

That had been an hour ago.

<br/>

Now, behind the lumber, Garek finished his thirty seconds, flipped his palm down, and started the second stretch without being told.

<br/>

"Good form," Mark said.

<br/>

"*Shut up*," said Garek.

---

Above them, Duchess sailed past with a crackle, trailing a comet-tail of blue sparks.

<br/>

Mark peered over the lumber. In the chaos, a plan had started to assemble itself in his head, the way a fix always did in the middle of a bad outage, piece by piece.

<br/>

"*Team!*" he called. "Gather on me!"

<br/>

Lyra appeared at his side from behind a crate, holding her graver. Vespera crept over from behind a support beam, her hood back, her silver bracelet glowing faintly at her wrist. Torren arrived last, at a crouching run, his beard visibly frizzed.

<br/>

"She's a capacitor," Mark said quickly. "Fully charged, ungrounded, and every time she touches something, she discharges a little and then picks up more from the air. We need to get her onto a ground long enough to drain her completely."

<br/>

"How?" Vespera asked.

<br/>

"Three steps. One: we corner her. Two: we hold her still. Three: we ground her."

<br/>

"Corner her how?" Lyra asked. "She zaps everything."

<br/>

"She zaps everything *clean*," Mark said. "But she's full of Aether, so she probably senses it the way the construct did. Guild plates are lossy. Dirty. They might not spook her." He looked at Lyra's apron. "Do you have any—"

<br/>

Lyra was already pulling plates out of her pockets. Ordinary, scratched Guild templates, the kind she always carried for practice. "Four," she said. "No, five. Six if you count the one in the secret pocket."

<br/>

"Make a wall."

<br/>

"Hold her still how?" Torren asked.

<br/>

Mark looked at Vespera.

<br/>

"You can levitate a hay bale," he said. "Silently. Without your staff."

<br/>

Vespera's eyes went wide. "I've only done it *once*. In the workshop. With soup-level concentration."

<br/>

"Then think about soup," Mark said.

<br/>

"And grounding her?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

Mark looked around the warehouse. His eye fell on a coil of copper wire hanging from a hook on the wall, used for binding timber, and an iron stake driven into the dirt floor for tethering carts.

<br/>

"I'll handle that part," he said. "Torren. When I say *now*, I need you to tackle the goat."

<br/>

Torren looked at him. "*Tackle* the goat?"

<br/>

"Gently."

<br/>

"She'll *zap* me."

<br/>

"Yes," Mark said. "Probably several times. But you're the only one of us big enough to hold her down."

<br/>

Torren thought about this. Then he looked at Duchess, still sailing joyfully around the rafters, shedding sparks.

<br/>

"For Duchess," he said solemnly.

<br/>

"For Duchess," Mark agreed.

---

It went almost exactly to plan, which in Mark's experience happened roughly once every forty years.

<br/>

Lyra laid her Guild plates in a curved line across the far corner of the warehouse, a low metal fence of lossy, sanctioned, deeply unappealing magic. When Duchess bounded toward it, she skidded to a confused halt in midair, sniffing at the plates, and backed slowly into the corner.

<br/>

Vespera stepped out from behind the beam.

<br/>

Mark watched her close her eyes for one long breath. Her lips didn't move. Her right hand didn't reach for a staff. She simply raised her left wrist, the silver bracelet flaring softly, and a hay bale lifted silently off the floor and drifted through the air toward Duchess.

<br/>

The goat's yellow eyes locked onto the floating hay with sudden, total fascination.

<br/>

Duchess stopped crackling. She forgot about everything else. She stretched her neck toward the hay bale and took an enormous, joyful bite.

<br/>

"*Now!*" Mark shouted.

<br/>

Torren hit Duchess at full speed.

<br/>

There was a tremendous *CRACK* and a flash of blue light. Torren made a noise like a whole set of kettles. Every hair in his beard stood out at once, so that for a moment his head appeared to be wearing a large, startled hedgehog. But he held on, both arms wrapped tight around the goat, dragging her down out of the air and pinning her firmly onto the dirt floor.

<br/>

"*Got her!*" he yelled, his voice vibrating. "*Got—her—Mark—hurry—*"

<br/>

Mark was already there. He looped the copper wire around Duchess's neck in a loose, gentle collar, twisted the end tight, and ran the other end to the iron tethering stake in the floor. He wrapped it around the stake three times.

<br/>

The effect was instant.

<br/>

A long, satisfying *hissss* rose from Duchess's fur, like air escaping a tire. A faint shimmer of blue ran down the copper wire, into the stake, and disappeared into the earth. Duchess's fur, inch by inch, began to settle back down into ordinary, fluffy brown goat fur.

<br/>

Her hooves touched the ground. Then her belly settled onto the dirt.

<br/>

Duchess let out a long, contented sigh, chewed her mouthful of hay, and lay down.

<br/>

Torren lay down too, more slowly, still clutching her. His beard remained vertical.

<br/>

For a long moment, the warehouse was silent except for the sound of a goat chewing.

<br/>

Then, from behind the lumber stack, Garek began to clap. Slowly. Then the two door guards, who had recovered, joined in. Then, after a moment's hesitation, so did Jax, whose hair was still pointing straight up.

<br/>

Nan Brask had not moved from her armchair at the far end of the warehouse during the entire four minutes. She looked over her spectacles at the scene. Then she picked up her knitting.

<br/>

"Well," she said. "That was efficient."

---

"What," Nan asked, a short while later, "do I call that thing you put on her neck?"

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth to explain. But Torren, sitting on the floor beside Duchess with his arm still draped protectively over her, beat him to it.

<br/>

"It's a *static-discharge safety cable*," Torren said.

<br/>

He said it perfectly. Every syllable. Not a single *dish*. Not a single *noodle*.

<br/>

There was a brief silence.

<br/>

Nobody reacted. Nan simply nodded and made a note in her knitting pattern. Lyra was busy collecting her plates. Vespera was staring at her own wrist in quiet wonder. Garek was doing wrist circles behind the lumber stack and pretending he wasn't.

<br/>

Only Mark had noticed. He looked at Torren.

<br/>

Torren looked back at him. His eyes were wide. His mouth was slightly open. His beard was still standing straight out from his face.

<br/>

"I said it," Torren whispered. "Mark. I said it right."

<br/>

"You did," Mark said softly.

<br/>

"Nobody *noticed*."

<br/>

"I noticed."

<br/>

Torren nodded slowly. Then he looked down at Duchess, who was now asleep with her head on his knee, and patted her gently. He looked, Mark thought, like a man who had climbed a mountain and found that the summit had no view.

<br/>

"Static-discharge," Torren murmured to the goat. "Safety cable."

<br/>

Duchess snored.

---

The negotiation took place at Nan's barrel desk, with Duchess grounded and dozing nearby.

<br/>

Mark laid out his proposal on the brass clipboard.

<br/>

"You're losing money because we're undercutting your crystal prices," he said. "And you have twenty goats that need grounding collars before they put any more of your staff through the rafters. I can make the collars. Torren can make the collars. Lyra can make them better than either of us." He tapped the clipboard. "So here's my offer. We stop competing. You become our distributor. Torren recharges the crystals; your network sells them through every route you've already got. Same customers, better product, no goats eating the inventory."

<br/>

"And what do *you* get?" Nan asked.

<br/>

"Your network," Mark said. "Your drivers. Your lookouts. Every road into Oakhaven. Including the capital road."

<br/>

Nan's knitting needles paused, briefly.

<br/>

"You want to know who's coming," she said.

<br/>

"I want to know who's coming," Mark agreed.

<br/>

Nan looked at him for a long moment over her spectacles. Then she resumed knitting.

<br/>

"Seventy-thirty," she said. "My favor."

<br/>

"Fifty-fifty," said Mark.

<br/>

"Sixty-forty," said Nan.

<br/>

"Fifty-fifty," said Mark, "and we include grounding collars for every goat, free, for a year."

<br/>

"Sixty-forty," said Nan, "and you also fix Garek's knee."

<br/>

From behind the lumber stack came an outraged noise. "*How* do you know about my knee?"

<br/>

"I know about everyone's knee," said Nan serenely.

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth to counter. But before he could speak, Lyra set down her graver, leaned forward on her crate, and said:

<br/>

"Fifty-five, forty-five, *our* favor."

<br/>

Everyone turned to look at her.

<br/>

"Grounding collars for every goat, free, for *six months*," Lyra went on, ticking points off on her fingers. "After that, cost price. We'll handle collars for any new goats you bring through the North Gate, so your losses to *zapping* go to zero. Your handlers stop quitting. Your inventory stops getting eaten. Our crystals are better than anything you've ever smuggled in, and you'll make more on half of ours than you ever made on all of yours." She sat back. "And we'll *look* at Garek's knee. No promises."

<br/>

There was a long silence.

<br/>

Nan Brask set down her knitting.

<br/>

She looked at Lyra over her half-moon spectacles for a very long time. It was the same look, Mark realized, that she had given the floating goat: careful, assessing, and faintly alarmed.

<br/>

"Workshop girl," Nan said slowly. "How old are you?"

<br/>

"Fifteen."

<br/>

"How would you like," Nan Brask said, "to come work for me?"

<br/>

"No, thank you," Lyra said. "I already have a job. Two, actually."

<br/>

Nan nodded, unsurprised. "If you ever change your mind." She picked up her needles. "Fifty-five, forty-five. Your favor. Six months. Done."

<br/>

Mark looked at Lyra.

<br/>

Lyra looked straight ahead with tremendous composure, as if nothing remarkable had happened at all. Her ears, however, were very slightly pink.

<br/>

"Stakeholder management," Mark murmured. "You'll go far."

<br/>

"I *know*," said Lyra.

---

There was one more thing.

<br/>

When the deal was done, Vespera, who had been very quiet through the entire negotiation, finally spoke.

<br/>

"I want to be clear," she said, her voice stiff, "that I find all of this deeply irregular. I am a sworn mage of the Chapterhouse. I have just made a business arrangement with a *criminal syndicate*. To *smuggle goods*. Using *goats*."

<br/>

Nan Brask looked at her mildly over her knitting.

<br/>

"Tell me, Mage Vespera," she said. "How much does the Chapterhouse charge a smith to re-attune a fitting that fails every six months?"

<br/>

"Twelve silver," Vespera said, "per fitting."

<br/>

"And how much does the fitting cost the Chapterhouse to make?"

<br/>

Vespera hesitated. "About... one."

<br/>

"And the fitting fails every six months," Nan said, "because...?"

<br/>

Vespera opened her mouth. Then she closed it. Mark watched her think back through everything they had learned. Torren's blower. The pinched return channels. The Sacred Restriction.

<br/>

"Because it was designed to," Vespera said quietly.

<br/>

"Mm," said Nan Brask. *Click, click.* "I smuggle crystals past a tariff. Your Guild sells broken fittings and charges people to fix them, forever. I'm just a little old lady with some goats, dear." She smiled pleasantly. "Which one of us, would you say, is running the bigger racket?"

<br/>

Vespera had no answer.

<br/>

She sat in silence on her crate for a long moment. Then, quietly, she pulled her hood back up over her head.

<br/>

"I need a minute," she said.

<br/>

"Take two," said Nan. "Then have some stew."

---

Nan insisted on stew.

<br/>

"We always eat after a deal," she said. "It's how the Syndicate does business. You don't stab a man who's had your stew." She paused. "Well. Not right away."

<br/>

The stew was goat. Mark was a little worried about this until Nan assured him, with great firmness, that it was *not* one of the smuggling goats. "We don't eat staff," she said. And it was, everyone agreed, astonishingly good. Thick and dark and rich with herbs, served in wooden bowls, with flatbread baked on a hot stone by one of the formerly unconscious door guards.

<br/>

Torren ate three bowls. Lyra ate two and pocketed a flatbread. Vespera ate one in silence and then, very quietly, asked for the recipe. Duchess slept through all of it, grounded and content, snoring with her head in Torren's lap.

<br/>

Mark found himself sitting next to Garek on an overturned crate by the fire.

<br/>

For a long time, neither of them said anything. Garek ate his stew slowly. Mark noticed that he was using his right hand to hold the spoon.

<br/>

At last Garek put his bowl down. He flexed his right wrist. Then he rotated it, slowly, in a full circle. Then the other direction.

<br/>

He looked down at his own hand as if it belonged to somebody else.

<br/>

"It's better," Garek said gruffly, without looking at Mark. He kept his voice low, so the others wouldn't hear. "Hasn't felt this good in weeks. I tried the stretches. Last night. And this morning." He paused. "And behind the lumber."

<br/>

"I noticed," Mark said.

<br/>

"Keep your voice down."

<br/>

"Sorry."

<br/>

Garek rolled the wrist again. "I can hold the truncheon again. Without it aching."

<br/>

"That's great," Mark said, and found he meant it. "Keep going. Twice a day. Morning and night. It'll keep getting better. Lyra's going to carve you something to squeeze."

<br/>

"She already did," Garek muttered, and reached into his jerkin.

<br/>

He pulled out a small, smooth block of carved wood, shaped to fit the palm of a large hand, with shallow grooves for fingers. Mark recognized Lyra's work immediately: those long, perfect, continuous strokes. At some point during the negotiation, while Garek had been absentmindedly squeezing a lump of wool from Nan's knitting basket, Lyra had noticed, pulled a scrap of oak from her apron, and carved it without a word.

<br/>

Garek turned it over in his hand. Then he tucked it carefully back inside his jerkin.

<br/>

They sat in silence for a while longer. The fire crackled. Torren snored, gently, against Duchess.

<br/>

Then Garek cleared his throat.

<br/>

"Vance," he said, very quietly. "What about knees?"

<br/>

Mark looked at him.

<br/>

"Left one," Garek said, staring into the fire. "Fifteen years of chasing people down alleys. Goes stiff in the cold. Clicks when I take stairs." He paused. "And the shoulder. The right one. Clicks when I lift it over my head."

<br/>

"Anything else?"

<br/>

A longer pause.

<br/>

"My back," Garek admitted. "Lower back. It does a thing in the mornings."

<br/>

"What kind of thing?"

<br/>

Garek considered how best to describe it. He opened his mouth. He closed it. He made a small, vague gesture with his hands that conveyed nothing whatsoever.

<br/>

"A *thing*," he said finally.

<br/>

Mark looked at the enforcer for a long moment. This was the man who had once promised to break both his legs. Who had swung an iron-shod truncheon at his head in a dark lane. Who now sat beside him at a stew fire with a carved grip trainer in his jerkin, asking about his knee.

<br/>

*"Well,"* Mark thought, *"there it is. My first patient."*

<br/>

"Physical therapy," Mark told him, "is a subscription service."

<br/>

"A what?"

<br/>

"It means," Mark said, "we're going to be seeing a lot of each other. We'll work on it. One joint at a time."

<br/>

Garek nodded slowly. Then he picked up his bowl again and went back to his stew.

<br/>

"Don't tell Jax," he said.

<br/>

Across the fire, Jax was staring at the two of them with an expression of total, mounting disbelief. His hair was still pointing straight up.

<br/>

"Too late," Mark said.

---

They were leaving the warehouse a little before midnight when one of Nan's goat drivers caught up with them at the door.

<br/>

He was a weathered, wiry man with a face like an old saddle, and he'd been brought in from the stables at Nan's request.

<br/>

"Boss says you want to know who's on the roads," he said. "I drive the capital road. In and out, twice a month." He glanced around, then lowered his voice. "Couple of things you should hear."

<br/>

The team stopped.

<br/>

"Go on," said Mark.

<br/>

"First thing," said the driver. "About two weeks back. Night I was bringing a string of goats in late. Coming past the Academy's rear gate around three in the morning." He hesitated. "A wagon came out."

<br/>

"What kind of wagon?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

"Big one. Heavy. Covered. Riding low on the axles, like it was carrying something very heavy." He paused again. "No driver."

<br/>

The team went quiet.

<br/>

"No driver?" Vespera repeated.

<br/>

"No driver," the goat driver said. "Nobody on the bench. Nobody walking beside it. Nothing pulling it that I could see. Just rolled right out the gate, turned down the lane, and went off into the dark." He shook his head. "My goats wouldn't go near it. Every one of them backed up and wouldn't move till it was gone."

<br/>

Mark and Lyra looked at each other.

<br/>

Two weeks ago. Three in the morning. The night after the construct.

<br/>

The night the archive had been cleaned.

<br/>

"Second thing," the driver went on. "Last few days, there's been carriages on the capital road. Fancy ones. Coming this way." He paused. "Thorne crest on the doors."

<br/>

Vespera's hand tightened on her staff.

<br/>

"Third thing," the driver said. "Men in the lower town, asking questions. Well dressed. Quiet. Asking about a boy who fixes blowers." He looked at Mark. "That'd be you."

<br/>

"That'd be me," Mark agreed.

<br/>

"They all had rings," the driver said. "Silver. Each with a little stone in it." He hesitated. "Funny thing. The stones were gold."

---

It was nearly two in the morning when Mark finally sat down at his desk in the narrow dorm room in the West Wing, his back to the wall, his clipboard on the desk, his ledger open in front of him.

<br/>

In the corner of the ceiling, the lentil spider was still on its web. Mark nodded to it.

<br/>

"Busy night," he told it.

<br/>

The lentil spider did not respond.

<br/>

He picked up his charcoal stick and wrote:

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 14: Message Bus Deployed & Strategic Partnership Secured*
> *Network Architecture:*
> *- Bus Master: Pip. Ends every report with 'Message bus, out.' Torren now salutes back reflexively.*
> *- Field Staff: 9 errand runners. Compensation: noodles (1 bowl/route) + glowing fish on rest days.*
> *- Academy Node: Corwin. Horizontal Surveillance Asset. Has known everything for three years. Cost: naps.*
> *- Protocol: 'Gerald' = danger. 'Cheese' = notify Lyra. Field staff currently escalating everything to Gerald. Subcategory added: Gerald (Onion).*
> *Partnership — Ironhead Syndicate:*
> *- Leader: Nan Brask. Knits org charts. Fellow executive. Terrifying.*
> *- Goat tariff confirmed as a per-container pricing exploit. The goats were always the joke. The goats were also always the business.*
> *- Charged goats (living capacitors) resolved via copper grounding collars. Lead incident: Duchess (named by Torren; floats when excited). Resolved with Guild-plate containment (Lyra), silent levitation decoy (Vespera, flawless), goat tackle (Torren, heroic, beard still vertical).*
> *- Deal: Syndicate becomes crystal distributor. Terms negotiated by Lyra (55/45, our favor). Nan has offered Lyra a job. Lyra declined. Lyra knows.*
> *- In exchange: full access to Syndicate road network, including the capital road.*
> *Occupational Health Program (unplanned):*
> *- Patient #1: Garek. Wrist: significant improvement. Compliance: initially hostile, now secretly diligent (observed stretching behind lumber mid-incident).*
> *- Pending: left knee, right shoulder, lower back ('does a thing in the mornings'; nature of thing unknown).*
> *- Note: if this works, every enforcer in the Syndicate will want one.*
> *Intelligence (first delivery):*
> *1. Night of the archive cleanup: driverless wagon left the Academy rear gate ~03:00, riding heavy. Goats refused to approach. Gerald.*
> *2. Thorne carriages on the capital road, heading for Oakhaven. Gerald.*
> *3. Well-dressed men asking about 'a boy who fixes blowers.' Silver rings with gold stones. Gerald, Gerald, Gerald.*
> *Vendor Review:*
> *- Ironhead Syndicate: Strong logistics, excellent field staff, questionable compliance. Four stars. Goat stew: five stars.*
> *Personal Note:*
> *Torren said 'static-discharge safety cable' correctly tonight. Nobody noticed but me. Record it here so it is remembered.*

<br/>

Mark set down the charcoal and read the entry over. His eyes lingered on the last item under *Intelligence*. Gold stones. Not red. Gold.

<br/>

*"They're not just watching anymore,"* he thought. *"They're looking."*

<br/>

He was still turning that over in his mind when there was a soft knock at the window.

<br/>

Mark froze. Then he got up, crossed the narrow room, and pulled the shutter open.

<br/>

On the ledge outside, two floors above the courtyard, crouched a small, breathless, freckled figure.

<br/>

"*Pip?*" Mark whispered. "How did you get *up* here?"

<br/>

"Drainpipe," Pip whispered back. His face was solemn and urgent. He raised his hand in a crisp salute. "Message bus, *delivering*."

<br/>

Mark's heart began to pound. "What is it?"

<br/>

Pip drew a deep breath.

<br/>

"*Gerald*," he said.

<br/>

Mark's mind raced: the Thorne carriages, the gold rings, the driverless wagon. "What happened? Who's coming? Where?"

<br/>

Pip looked at him, puzzled.

<br/>

"Hamm ran out of noodles," he said. "Whole pot's gone. Nobody's getting paid tomorrow till he gets more flour." He paused. "The runners are *very* upset."

<br/>

Mark stared at him.

<br/>

Then he went back to his desk, picked up the charcoal, and under *Protocol*, very carefully, added a line:

<br/>

> *Revision: 'Gerald' may also refer to noodle supply chain disruption. Severity: (to field staff) critical.*

<br/>

He returned to the window.

<br/>

"Thank you, Pip," he said gravely. "Very important. I'll speak to Hamm in the morning."

<br/>

Pip nodded, satisfied. He saluted once more.

<br/>

"Message bus," he whispered, "*out*."

<br/>

And he vanished down the drainpipe into the dark.
