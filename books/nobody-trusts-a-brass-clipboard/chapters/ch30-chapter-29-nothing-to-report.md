## Chapter 29: Nothing to Report

<br/>

The only way to get Prototype 2.0 out of Cheese the Fourth was to eat Cheese the Fourth.

<br/>

"I sealed it *perfectly*," Lyra said, standing over the great pale wheel on the workbench with a knife in her hand and an expression of grim professional pride. "With a warm blade. There's no seam. If I cut into it from outside, I'll go straight through the linen and scratch the traces." She set the knife down. "We have to go in from the top. Slowly. By hand." She paused. "By mouth."

<br/>

Everyone looked at the cheese.

<br/>

It was the size of a cartwheel hub. It weighed as much as a small dog.

<br/>

"It's eleven o'clock at night," said Vespera.

<br/>

"It's power budget," said Mark. "We'll need it down there. Calories in the system." He picked up a knife. "Call it pre-deployment fueling."

<br/>

"*I'll* have some," said Torren, who had been standing beside the bench for the last ten minutes with the bright, patient expression of a dog beside a dinner table.

<br/>

They ate the cheese.

<br/>

It took the five of them nearly forty minutes, standing around the workbench in the lamplight, carving careful wedges off the top and passing them around. It was very good cheese: hard and nutty, with crystals of salt in it that crunched between the teeth. By the twentieth minute, nobody was enjoying it anymore. By the thirtieth, Lyra had stopped speaking entirely and was chewing with the fixed, distant gaze of a soldier on a long march.

<br/>

Julian had never eaten cheese standing up.

<br/>

"At home," he said, holding his wedge in his bandaged fingers as if unsure of the protocol, "it came on a board. After dinner. With grapes. And one sat down."

<br/>

"Here," said Torren, through a mouthful, "it comes off a wheel. Before a dungeon. With a knife. And one *chews*."

<br/>

Julian considered this. Then he took a large, deliberate bite, standing up, like a man crossing a personal Rubicon.

<br/>

"It's better," he said, surprised.

<br/>

"Everything's better standing up," said Torren. "Except sleeping. Ask Corwin."

<br/>

At forty-one minutes, Lyra's knife touched waxed linen. She set the knife down, slid two fingers into the hollow, and drew the bundle out of the heart of the cheese like a surgeon delivering something precious. She unwrapped it on the bench.

<br/>

Prototype 2.0 lay in the lamplight exactly as Mark had last seen it: two crystal sockets on a band of pigskin leather, dual silver traces, six filed copper fins, the tiny silver sliding pin by the palm. The crystals glowed a steady, cool blue.

<br/>

Untouched. Unaudited. Unrestored.

<br/>

Mark picked it up and turned it over in his hands, and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched for nine days.

<br/>

"Thank you, Cheese the Fourth," said Torren solemnly, to the hollow wreck of the wheel. "You did your duty."

<br/>

"Never again," said Lyra faintly, to nobody. "I'm never eating cheese again."

<br/>

"You say that every time," said Vespera.

---

They divided up the team in the yard, under the dark lamp by the gate.

<br/>

Garek would hold the workshop. He didn't argue. He'd been standing in that doorway for the better part of two weeks, and he took up his position in it again without a word, settling his shoulders against both sides of the frame with the quiet satisfaction of a man returning to his own chair. Above him, Legacy settled into the center of her web.

<br/>

"Leg lifts," said Mark.

<br/>

"Three times," said Garek. "I know."

<br/>

Hollis and Pip would run the relay. Hollis would wait at the top of the archive stairs inside the Academy; Pip would wait at the rear gate outside; and between them, at every corner from the Academy to the Fish & Hammer, a runner would be posted with one word to pass on if anything went wrong.

<br/>

"Not *Gerald*," Pip said firmly. "Everybody says Gerald. If something goes wrong down there, it'll be a *really* big Gerald, and the runners have to know it's *this* one. So I made a new word."

<br/>

"What's the word?" said Mark.

<br/>

Pip drew himself up.

<br/>

"*Margaret*," he said.

<br/>

From the doorway of the workshop, Torren made a small, wounded sound.

<br/>

"That's what I call *Legacy*," he said.

<br/>

"I know," said Pip. "That's why it's perfect. Nobody else uses it." He saluted. "If it's Margaret, everyone comes. Message bus, out."

---

Valerius met them at the Academy's rear gate at a quarter to midnight.

<br/>

He was standing in the shadow of the wall in a plain dark cloak with the hood back, and he was alone. No Enforcers. No silver-and-white robes. He looked smaller without them, and older, and very tired, and Mark noticed that he held his left arm slightly away from his body, the way you hold an arm when there's something on the inside of the wrist you'd rather not think about.

<br/>

"Inspector," said Mark.

<br/>

"Apprentice Vance." Valerius inclined his head. Then his eyes went past Mark, to Vespera, and stayed there a moment longer than they needed to. "Mage Vespera."

<br/>

"Inspector," said Vespera.

<br/>

There was a short, complicated pause, in which Mark, Lyra, Torren, and Julian all found something extremely interesting to look at in four different directions.

<br/>

Valerius held out a long brass detection rod, the same kind his Enforcers had carried into Torren's yard on the morning of the audit, a very long time ago.

<br/>

"You'll want this," he said. "It won't find the Auditor. Nothing finds the Auditor. But it will tell you if anything below you wakes up." He handed Mark a folded sheet of paper. "And this is the Chapterhouse Enforcers' night rotation. Two men in the main hall until four. One more at the stair to Deep Vault 3, but he never goes past the vault itself. Nobody does. I've stood in that vault and looked at the Anchor more times than I can count, and I never once wondered what was underneath it." He paused. "I'll be in the main hall myself, tonight. On an unscheduled inspection. If anyone comes down from the Chapterhouse side, I'll find a reason to keep them talking."

<br/>

"Thank you," said Mark.

<br/>

"Don't." Valerius's mouth twitched, very faintly. "I'm only doing it because they've scheduled me for correction. It's remarkable how quickly that clarifies one's loyalties." He looked at Vespera again. "Thursday," he said, "still stands. If there is a Thursday."

<br/>

Vespera looked back at him for a long moment.

<br/>

"I'll have to check," she said.

<br/>

And then, for the first time Mark had ever seen in all their months together, Mage Vespera smiled at someone who wasn't a patient. It was small and brief and slightly crooked, and it changed her whole face.

<br/>

Valerius looked at it as if he had been handed something fragile.

<br/>

Then he pulled his hood up, turned, and walked off along the wall toward the Chapterhouse without another word.

<br/>

Lyra leaned over to Mark. "Did you see—"

<br/>

"I saw."

<br/>

"She *smiled*."

<br/>

"I saw, Lyra."

<br/>

"I'm writing it down," said Lyra, and did.

---

They went down at midnight.

<br/>

The archive stairs were exactly as they'd always been: worn stone, cold walls, the faint smell of old parchment drifting up from below. Hollis stood at the top with a lantern and a face set hard, and watched them go.

<br/>

They met Mott on the first landing.

<br/>

He was coming up as they were going down, as he always did at midnight, one slow step at a time, wrapped in his grey wool, with Lyra's grip trainer tucked into the pocket of his cardigan and his candle held up in front of him in his crooked, swollen hand.

<br/>

He stopped on the landing and looked at them all.

<br/>

He looked at Torren, with his good apron and his reading spectacles pushed up on his forehead. At Lyra, with her tool roll and her twelve pockets. At Vespera, with the old ironwood staff she hadn't carried in weeks. At Julian, in Torren's borrowed shirt, with his clean white bandages.

<br/>

Then he looked at Mark, for a long time.

<br/>

He didn't ask if Mark was one of the new ones.

<br/>

He held out his candle.

<br/>

Mark looked at it. It was a short, cheap tallow stub in a small tin holder, burning with a small, steady yellow flame.

<br/>

"It's dark below the third," said Mott.

<br/>

Mark took it.

<br/>

Mott nodded, once, as if something had been settled. Then he turned and went on up the stairs without his candle, very slowly, one step at a time, his hand on the wall, into the dark, toward the gate and the inscription and the cold stone step where he would sit until three o'clock in the morning.

<br/>

Nobody said anything until his footsteps had faded.

<br/>

"He's never given anyone his candle," said Lyra quietly.

<br/>

"He's never needed to," said Mark, and found that his voice wasn't entirely steady.

<br/>

He held the candle a little higher, and they went on down.

---

The archive was dark and silent, the long tables empty, the leaning tower of plates standing in its corner under its broom. Mott's ledger lay closed on his desk. Mark walked past it without looking at it, which took some effort.

<br/>

Below the archive, the stairs went on.

<br/>

Mark had been down them once before, on the worst night of his life in this world, running *up* them with a seven-foot construct behind him and Lyra's hand in his. He didn't remember much about them from that night. He remembered them now. They were narrower than the stairs above, and steeper, and the treads were worn into deep, smooth hollows in the center, as if by centuries of feet. Somebody had come down here a great many times, long ago. Then they'd stopped.

<br/>

The second landing was a small, square stone room with a single iron door, rusted shut. Julian stopped beside it and laid his bandaged hand on the metal.

<br/>

"This is as far as my father ever took me," he said quietly. "Twice. He stood here and told me about the Keepers' oath. And then we went back up." He looked at the stairs going down into the dark. "I always wondered what was below."

<br/>

"Now you'll know," said Torren cheerfully, peering past him down the stairwell with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead and a look of pure, uncomplicated delight on his broad face. "Look at that stonework. *Look* at it."

<br/>

Everyone looked at it. It was a stairwell.

<br/>

"That's quarter-sawn granite," Torren said reverently. "Dry-laid. No mortar. Look at the joints. You couldn't get a *hair* in there." He ran a thick finger lovingly along a seam. "My mother would have *wept*. My mother wept at a good wall. Dwarf side of the family. We all do."

<br/>

"You're happy," said Lyra, accusingly.

<br/>

"I'm *underground*," said Torren, as if that explained everything, and for him, apparently, it did. He went on down the stairs ahead of them, humming, patting the walls.

---

Corwin was waiting at the third landing.

<br/>

He was lying on his back on the top step of the flight below, with his arms folded over his chest and his eyes closed, exactly as he lay on every other step in the Academy. He'd left the workshop an hour before them. Nobody had seen him go.

<br/>

"You're here," said Mark.

<br/>

"I'm here," Corwin agreed. "Relay point. Somebody has to sit at the halfway mark and pass messages up. I'm very good at sitting." He opened one eye. "And this is as far as I go."

<br/>

Mark crouched down beside him, and set Mott's candle on the step.

<br/>

"You told me," he said. "My first night. 'Don't go below the third landing.' You never said why."

<br/>

Corwin was quiet for a long time. The candle flame stood perfectly still in the dead air of the stairwell.

<br/>

"My first year," he said at last. "First month. I was a different person then. I used to *stand up*. Walk around. Do things." He said it the way another person might confess to a youthful career in piracy. "Some of the older boys dared me. Go down past the third landing, alone, with a candle, and bring something back. Anything. A pebble. Just to prove you'd been."

<br/>

"Did you?"

<br/>

"I went down to the fourth landing," said Corwin. "That's as far as I got."

<br/>

"What was there?"

<br/>

Corwin didn't answer for a long moment.

<br/>

"I'm not going to tell you," he said. "You'll see it. It's not really a thing you tell." He closed the open eye. "I stood there for a while. And then I came back up. Very quietly. And I went to my room, and I lay down on my bed." He paused. "And I found that I didn't really want to get up again."

<br/>

Nobody said anything.

<br/>

"It seemed safest," said Corwin. "Lying down. Doing the minimum. Not being noticed. If you're very still, and very quiet, and you never do anything that rings…" He shrugged against the stone. "Nothing ever comes looking."

<br/>

Mark looked down at him, at the long, lazy figure stretched out on the cold step, who had stood up in the arena for exactly four minutes and been better than anyone else in the Academy, and then lain down again. He thought he understood something about Corwin now that he hadn't before.

<br/>

"Three years," Mark said.

<br/>

"Three years," Corwin agreed. "Best decision I ever made." He opened both eyes and looked up at Mark. "I'll be right here. If you need to send something up, send it to me, and I'll send it to Hollis. And if you need me to come down…" He considered. "Send it twice."

<br/>

"Thank you, Corwin."

<br/>

"Mm," said Corwin, which was, everyone now understood, a very significant thing for him to say.

<br/>

Mark picked up Mott's candle and stepped past him, down onto the first stair below the third landing.

<br/>

Nothing happened. It was just a stair.

<br/>

He went on down. The others followed.

---

The fourth landing was where the stairwell stopped being a stairwell.

<br/>

Mark had been expecting another small stone room, another rusted door. Instead, the stairs simply ended, and opened out, and he found himself standing at the head of a long, straight, perfectly level corridor that ran away into the dark further than the light of Mott's candle could reach.

<br/>

He held the candle up.

<br/>

And then he stood very still, and said nothing at all for quite a long time.

<br/>

"Mark?" said Lyra, at his shoulder.

<br/>

He didn't answer. He was looking at the walls.

<br/>

Along both sides of the corridor, at waist height and again at shoulder height and again just below the ceiling, ran thick bundles of silver. Not wire. Not ribbon. *Conduit*: rods of solid silver as thick as a man's wrist, a dozen to a bundle, each bundle laid into a long, shallow trough cut into the stone, and the troughs ran straight and level down the whole length of the corridor into the dark. Every few yards, a bundle branched: three or four rods splitting off, curving down neatly through a rounded channel in the wall, and vanishing into the floor. Every branch was clean. Every curve had the same radius. Every rod was held in place by a small bronze clip, and every clip was spaced at precisely the same interval, and every one of them, as far as Mark could see, was perfectly aligned.

<br/>

There were labels. Small bronze plates, every twenty feet, with a few First Era glyphs stamped into each one, and Mark's translation layer reached for them and caught them:

<br/>

`[TRUNK 4 — NORTH QUARTER]`

<br/>

`[TRUNK 4 — BRANCH 17]`

<br/>

`[TRUNK 5 — RETURN]`

<br/>

Mark took one step forward, holding up the candle, and then another, and laid his free hand very gently on the nearest bundle. It was cold under his fingers, and it hummed. Very faintly. A deep, low, steady hum, below the edge of hearing, that he felt in the bones of his hand more than he heard.

<br/>

"Mark," said Lyra again, "what *is* it?"

<br/>

Mark realized, to his own considerable surprise, that his eyes were stinging.

<br/>

"Someone," he said, and his voice cracked slightly, "did cable management."

<br/>

Lyra stared at him.

<br/>

"Five hundred years ago," Mark said. "Properly. Labeled trunks. Consistent bend radius. Clips on a fixed pitch. *Separate return lines.*" He gestured helplessly down the corridor. "Do you know how many server rooms I walked into in forty years? *Hundreds*. Do you know how many of them looked like *this*?"

<br/>

"How many?" said Torren, interested.

<br/>

"*None*," said Mark. "*Not one*. They looked like someone had dropped a plate of spaghetti and then poured concrete over it. There was a cable in a building I worked in, in 2016, that *nobody could find the other end of*. We had to *leave it in*." He laid his hand on the silver bundle again, almost tenderly. "And somebody here, in a world without electricity, without CAD software, without a single *zip tie*, sat down and did *this*."

<br/>

There was a respectful pause.

<br/>

"Is he all right?" Julian murmured to Lyra.

<br/>

"He gets like this," said Lyra. "About tidy things."

<br/>

"He cried a bit at the Concord decree," said Vespera, "because the clauses were numbered consistently."

<br/>

"I did *not*," said Mark, wiping his eyes. "I had dust in my eye. It's very dusty down here."

<br/>

"It's not dusty at all," said Torren, patting the wall. "It's immaculate. That's the *point*."

---

It got colder as they went down.

<br/>

There were more stairs at the far end of the long corridor, wide and shallow this time, leading down to another level, and another long corridor, and more silver trunks along the walls. The cold came up the stairs to meet them, the way cold air spills out of an open cellar door in summer. At the fourth landing it had been chilly. By the time they reached the bottom of the next flight, Mark could see his breath.

<br/>

By the level below that, there was frost.

<br/>

It wasn't snow, and it wasn't ice. It was a fine, glittering fur of white crystal, growing on the walls in neat bands just above and below each silver trunk, as if the cold were coming *out* of the conduit and freezing whatever moisture it touched. The flagstones underfoot were dry and gritty with it. When Mark breathed out, the cloud of his breath hung in the candlelight for a long moment before it faded.

<br/>

"They're cooling something," he said quietly.

<br/>

"*What* are they cooling?" said Lyra. She had her arms wrapped around herself. Her teeth were chattering faintly.

<br/>

"I don't know yet." Mark looked down the corridor, where the frost got thicker. "But in my world, when you walk into a room and it's suddenly very, very cold, and there are big bundles of cable on every wall, and they're all labeled, and they all run in the same direction…" He held the candle a little higher. "It means there's something at the end of them that runs very, very hot. And somebody spent a lot of money making sure it never overheats."

<br/>

"Like Bessie," said Torren softly.

<br/>

"Like Bessie," Mark agreed. "If Bessie were the size of a cathedral."

<br/>

Julian had gone quiet. He was walking a little behind the others, looking at the frost on the walls, and Mark, glancing back, saw his face in the candlelight: pale and set and very young.

<br/>

*The paintings,* Mark thought. *Whole cities frozen solid.*

<br/>

He didn't say anything. He turned back around and kept walking.

---

The Keeper door was on the fifth landing.

<br/>

It stood at the end of the frost-lined corridor: a single tall, narrow door of black iron set into a frame of dark grey stone, with a keyhole of polished obsidian at its center and the circle-and-line seal carved deep into the lintel above it. On either side of the door, the stone frame was flat and smooth, and it was covered, from floor to ceiling, in writing.

<br/>

Not First Era script. Ordinary script. The local hand, the kind Mark saw every day on market signs and Chapterhouse notices and intake forms, carved into the stone in small, careful letters, line after line after line, in dozens of different hands.

<br/>

Julian stopped in front of it.

<br/>

"Oh," he said, very softly.

<br/>

Mark held the candle up to the bottom-left corner of the frame, where the oldest lines were, and read.

<br/>

*Year 1 of the Measure. Inspection complete. Seals intact. Nothing to report. — Edric Thorne.*

<br/>

*Year 2. Seals intact. Nothing to report. — E.T.*

<br/>

*Year 3. Nothing to report. — E.T.*

<br/>

He moved the candle up the column.

<br/>

*Year 41. Nothing to report. — Halden Thorne.*

<br/>

*Year 42. Nothing to report. — H.T.*

<br/>

*Year 43. Nothing to report. — H.T.*

<br/>

The handwriting changed every few dozen lines. The names changed. *Halden. Merrow. Cassius. Aldwin.* Twenty generations of Thornes, one line a year, every year, for five hundred years, each one standing in this exact spot in the cold, holding a candle, looking at the door, and carving the same three words into the stone before they turned and went back up.

<br/>

*Nothing to report.*

<br/>

*Nothing to report.*

<br/>

*Nothing to report.*

<br/>

"It's an inspection log," said Mark slowly. "An annual maintenance check. One line a year. For five hundred years."

<br/>

"It's the Keepers' oath," said Julian. His voice was strange. "Father told me about it. On the second landing. Every Thorne heir comes down here once a year, after they take the ring, and checks that the seals are holding, and writes it on the wall." He looked up at the endless column of lines. "And never opens the door. That's the oath. The key is only for the day the seals fail." He swallowed. "I didn't know it looked like this. I didn't know there were so *many*."

<br/>

Lyra had come up close beside the frame with her lens and was reading the middle columns, her lips moving slightly.

<br/>

"*Year 212*," she read aloud. "*Nothing to report.* *Year 213. Nothing to report.* *Year 214. Nothing to report.*" She moved her lens. "*Year 340*—" She stopped. "Oh. *Year 340. A rat. Nothing to report.*"

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"A rat," said Torren.

<br/>

"*A rat*," Lyra confirmed. "In Year 340, Cassius Thorne came down here and saw a rat. And he felt it was important enough to record." She moved her lens. "*Year 341. Nothing to report. No rat.*"

<br/>

Torren laughed, loudly, before he could stop himself, and the sound boomed down the frozen corridor and came echoing back, and he clapped his hand over his mouth, eyes wide.

<br/>

Nothing happened.

<br/>

"Sorry," he whispered through his fingers. "Sorry. *No rat.*"

<br/>

Mark found himself smiling too, despite the cold, despite everything. Twenty generations. Five hundred years of a family standing in the cold in front of the same door, checking the same seals, finding nothing, writing it down anyway. He'd known men like that. He'd *been* a man like that. Every Monday morning for thirty years: *All systems nominal.* *No incidents.* *Nothing to report.*

<br/>

There was something terribly human about it.

<br/>

He moved the candle up to the top of the right-hand column, where the newest lines were.

<br/>

*Year 497. Nothing to report. — Aldric Thorne.*

<br/>

*Year 498. Nothing to report. — A.T.*

<br/>

*Year 499. Nothing to report. — A.T.*

<br/>

And beneath it, the last line on the wall. The letters were cut deeper than any of the others, and less evenly, as if the hand that cut them had been less steady than it had been for all the years before.

<br/>

*Year 500. Red glyph. Something to report. — A.T.*

<br/>

Julian stood looking at it for a long time.

<br/>

Nobody said anything. Mark watched him read his father's handwriting, and saw his face change. He saw the young man take in the whole of it: the cold, and the dark, and the endless column of *nothing to report*, and his father, alone, standing in this exact spot a few months ago, holding a candle, looking at a door, writing the first different words that anyone in his family had written here in five hundred years.

<br/>

"He's never been frightened," Julian said quietly. "Not once. Not in my whole life. I've never seen him frightened of anything."

<br/>

He reached out with his bandaged hand and touched the deep, uneven letters, very lightly.

<br/>

"He was frightened when he wrote this," he said.

<br/>

Then he took his hand away, and reached into the pocket of Torren's borrowed shirt, and drew out Gerald's Cousin in its scrap of blue velvet.

<br/>

"I'll open it," Julian said.

<br/>

"Julian," said Vespera quietly. "The oath—"

<br/>

"Five hundred years of nothing to report," said Julian. "And then my father came down here, frightened, and wrote *something*. And then he went back upstairs and kept the door shut anyway, and let a thing in a grey robe come to clean up after him." His bandaged fingers closed around the key. "I think it should be a Thorne who breaks it."

<br/>

Nobody argued.

<br/>

He fitted the black key into the obsidian keyhole. It went in smoothly, perfectly, as if it had been waiting. He turned it, with both bandaged hands, slowly, and there was a long, deep, heavy *clunk* from somewhere inside the iron, like a great bolt sliding back in a well-oiled track after five hundred years of waiting to be used.

<br/>

The Keeper door swung open.

<br/>

Beyond it, the dark went on.

---

Beyond the Keeper door was a short passage, a flight of narrow steps, and a second, smaller arch.

<br/>

And beyond the arch was the rack room.

<br/>

Mark didn't call it that at first. At first, he didn't call it anything. He stopped in the archway with Mott's candle held up in front of him, and the others came up behind him and stopped too, and for a long, long moment, none of them moved or breathed.

<br/>

It was a hall. Long and high and narrow, vaulted in grey stone, running away into the dark further than the candle could reach. The silver trunks ran along the walls here too, thicker than ever, bundled in dozens, rising up into the vault of the ceiling and branching down into the floor.

<br/>

And on both sides of the hall, set into the walls at regular intervals, were alcoves.

<br/>

And in every alcove stood a construct.

<br/>

They were exactly like Gerald. Seven feet of smooth silver-white metal, long-limbed and narrow, with blank oval faces and dark grey hearts set into their chests. Each one stood in its alcove with its arms at its sides and its head bowed forward, very slightly, like a monk at prayer, or a man asleep on his feet. Each one was dusted with a fine, glittering fur of frost. Each one was perfectly still.

<br/>

Mark held up the candle and counted the ones he could see. Ten on the left. Ten on the right. And the alcoves went on, into the dark, past the reach of the light, as far as he could tell, forever.

<br/>

*"Server racks,"* he thought, numbly. *"Rows and rows of server racks. Powered down. Waiting."*

<br/>

*"Hot spares."*

<br/>

Behind him, very quietly, Lyra made a sound that was almost a whimper. Julian had gone so white that his face looked like one of the constructs'. Torren had stopped humming.

<br/>

And Vespera, Mark saw, had shifted her grip on the old ironwood staff, and was holding it now not like a walking stick, but like a weapon.

<br/>

Nobody said anything for a long time.

<br/>

"This," Mark said finally, in a whisper so quiet it barely moved the candle flame, "is what Corwin saw."

<br/>

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

---

"Rules," Mark breathed. He turned to face them, very slowly, keeping his voice so low that they had to lean in to hear him. "They only wake for clean circuits. Remember Gerald. Remember the archive. They came up the stairs because I ran an old plate. Dirty magic doesn't touch them. *Noise* doesn't touch them. Only clean."

<br/>

"So we just… walk past," whispered Julian.

<br/>

"We just walk past," Mark agreed. "No clean casting. Nothing that rings. Prototype 2.0 stays in Alpha, switched off, in my satchel. Vespera, your bracelet—"

<br/>

"I took it off," Vespera murmured. "It's in my pocket. It's off."

<br/>

"Lyra?"

<br/>

Lyra hesitated, then patted her apron. "L-2's in the twelfth pocket. It's not finished. It's not *on*. It's just a strip of silver."

<br/>

"Then we walk," said Mark. "Single file. Slowly. Down the middle. And nobody touches *anything*."

---

They walked.

<br/>

It was, Mark thought afterward, the longest walk of his life, and he had once walked from the parking garage to the boardroom to tell a CEO that the company's entire customer database had been publicly accessible for nine months.

<br/>

The hall was very long. The alcoves went on and on, on both sides, and in every one stood a construct with its head bowed, frosted and silver and absolutely motionless. The candle threw their shadows huge and wavering across the vault of the ceiling. The silver trunks hummed in the walls. The only other sounds were five people breathing as quietly as they possibly could, and the faint, gritty crunch of frost under their boots.

<br/>

Mark kept his eyes forward. He didn't look into the alcoves. He looked at the dark ahead, and the candle flame, and the floor.

<br/>

Twenty constructs. Thirty. Forty. He stopped counting.

<br/>

They were about halfway down the hall when Torren sneezed.

<br/>

It was not a small sneeze. Torren was not a small man, and nothing about him had ever been small, and the sneeze came out of him like a cannon going off in a cathedral: a vast, explosive, helpless *HAAAH-CHOO* that boomed down the stone hall and rolled back off the vaulted ceiling and kept echoing, on and on, into the dark.

<br/>

Everyone froze.

<br/>

Mark stood absolutely still in the center of the hall, with the candle held up in his hand, and didn't breathe. On either side of him, frosted and silent, forty constructs stood in their alcoves with their heads bowed.

<br/>

Not one of them moved.

<br/>

The echo faded. The silence came back.

<br/>

Seconds passed. Five. Ten. Twenty.

<br/>

Nothing.

<br/>

Behind Mark, very, very softly, in a voice of profound and heartfelt relief, Torren whispered:

<br/>

"*Bless me.*"

<br/>

Lyra made a sound that might have been a sob or a laugh, and pressed both hands over her mouth. Julian closed his eyes. Vespera let out a long, slow breath through her nose.

<br/>

"Dust," Torren whispered, apologetically. "It's the frost. Gets up your nose."

<br/>

"You said," Lyra breathed through her fingers, "it was *immaculate*."

<br/>

"It *is* immaculate," Torren whispered back. "That's *worse*. Nothing for the frost to stick to but *me*."

<br/>

Mark found that he was smiling, very slightly, despite everything, despite the forty silver figures on either side of him and the hum in the walls and the cold in his bones. *Noise doesn't touch them,* he thought. *Only clean.* He'd known that. He'd *said* that. But it was one thing to know it, and another to stand in the middle of the hall while a quarter-dwarf blacksmith tested it at full volume.

<br/>

"Keep walking," he whispered.

<br/>

They kept walking.

---

They were nearly at the end of the hall, perhaps ten alcoves from the far arch, when Mark heard the hum.

<br/>

It wasn't the deep, steady hum of the silver trunks in the walls. It was higher. Thinner. Very faint, so faint that at first he thought he'd imagined it: a tiny, crystalline whine, like a fingertip run around the rim of a glass.

<br/>

It was coming from behind him.

<br/>

He turned his head, very slowly.

<br/>

Lyra had stopped walking. She was standing in the center of the hall with her eyes wide and her hand pressed flat over the twelfth pocket of her apron, and the thin, high whine was coming from under her palm.

<br/>

"It's *not on*," she breathed, horrified. "It's *not*. It's just a strip. It's not *finished*. There's no crystal in the seat. It shouldn't—"

<br/>

But Mark understood instantly, the way he'd have understood a ground loop on a circuit board. *She's cut it right. The traces are clean. And the whole hall is full of ambient Aether pouring out of those trunks, and a clean trace with nothing pinching it will pick up a current from anywhere, like an antenna. It doesn't need to be on. It just needs to be right.*

<br/>

*She made it too well.*

<br/>

In the alcove to their left, very slowly, a construct's head turned.

<br/>

It was only an inch. Perhaps less. A tiny, smooth, silent movement of the blank oval face, up and to the side, toward the center of the hall. Toward Lyra. Frost cracked faintly and fell from its shoulders in a small, glittering shower.

<br/>

Then it stopped.

<br/>

Nobody breathed.

<br/>

Very slowly, very carefully, Lyra slid her hand into her pocket, closed her fingers tightly around the strip of silver inside, and pressed it flat against the lead-lined bottom of the pocket, smothering it.

<br/>

The thin whine died.

<br/>

The construct didn't move again. Its face stayed turned that one inch toward the center of the hall, frozen, frosted, waiting.

<br/>

"*Walk*," Mark breathed.

<br/>

They walked. Faster now. Not running. Mark would not let them run. They walked the last ten alcoves with their hearts hammering and their eyes fixed on the far arch, and every alcove they passed felt like a held breath, and then they were through the arch and down three steps and around a corner, and out of sight of the hall, and Lyra sat down hard on the bottom step and put her face in her hands.

<br/>

"It's not ready," she said into her palms. Her voice was shaking. "It's not *ready*, Mark. It hums. It hums by *itself*. If the Auditor heard that—if one of those *things*—"

<br/>

"I know," said Mark. He sat down on the step beside her. "But Lyra. Listen to me." He waited until she looked up. "It hummed because it's *right*. It's so clean that it picks up current out of thin air. That's not a failure. That's the most important test result you've ever had." He paused. "It just means you need a way to make it quiet when you want it quiet. A switch. A shield. A *mute*."

<br/>

Lyra stared at him. Then she looked down at her pocket.

<br/>

"A mute," she said slowly.

<br/>

He could see her start to think about it, right there on the step, with her face still wet. He decided that was probably the best thing that could have happened.

<br/>

"Later," he said gently, getting up. "Not here."

---

Below the rack room, it got hot.

<br/>

It happened suddenly. One moment they were walking down a short flight of steps with their breath steaming in the candlelight and frost crunching under their boots. The next, they turned a corner and the frost was gone, and the air was warm, and then it was more than warm, and then it was like walking into the open mouth of Torren's forge on a summer afternoon.

<br/>

Mark felt sweat break out across his forehead and down his back. Mott's candle flame leaned sideways in a hot, steady draft coming up the passage toward them.

<br/>

The passage opened out.

<br/>

And they were standing on a narrow stone ledge at the edge of the largest room Mark had ever seen.

<br/>

It wasn't a room. It was a cavern, a vast natural vault in the living rock, so big that the candle couldn't touch the walls or the ceiling or the floor. But they didn't need the candle. The cavern was full of light.

<br/>

It was full of crystals.

<br/>

They rose up out of the floor of the cavern in a great, glittering forest, hundreds of them, maybe thousands: tall, faceted pillars of the same pale crystal as every mana-shard Mark had ever recharged on his workbench, except that the smallest of these was taller than Torren and the largest rose up into the dark above them like the trunks of ancient trees. And every single one of them glowed. Not the cool, steady blue of a charged crystal on a bench. A deep, warm, pulsing red-gold, like the coals at the bottom of a forge, like the light behind closed eyelids on a summer day.

<br/>

The heat came off them in waves.

<br/>

And running into the base of every crystal, from every direction, out of the walls and the floor and the ceiling of the cavern, came the silver trunks. Thousands of them. The same neat, labeled, perfectly bundled conduit that had lined every corridor and every hall on the way down, all of it converging here, branching and dividing and running into the crystals like roots into a forest floor.

<br/>

At the very center of the cavern, rising up out of the heart of the crystal forest and vanishing into the dark of the ceiling far above, stood a single column of black obsidian, as wide as a house.

<br/>

Nobody said anything for a very long time.

<br/>

"It's a battery," said Mark at last.

<br/>

His voice sounded very small in the enormous warm space.

<br/>

"It's a battery bank," he said. "It's the biggest battery bank I've ever—that anyone has ever—" He stopped. He tried again. "Every one of those trunks. In every corridor. Every branch. *TRUNK 4, NORTH QUARTER.* They all come here. Every one of them runs down from somewhere up there, in the city, and comes here, and feeds *these*." He swept the candle out across the glowing forest. "They're charging. All of them. Right now."

<br/>

"Charging with *what*?" whispered Lyra.

<br/>

Mark didn't answer straight away.

<br/>

He was looking at the color of the light. Red-gold. Warm. Pulsing. He'd seen that color before. He'd seen it creeping up the silver core of Julian's staff in the arena, dull red, cherry, orange. He'd seen it in the forge yard on the very first day, in Torren's melted regulator discs. He'd seen it in the blisters on Julian's palms.

<br/>

*Heat.*

<br/>

"The forty percent," he said slowly.

<br/>

Everyone turned to look at him.

<br/>

"The Sacred Restriction," said Mark. His mind was racing now, the pieces sliding into place one after another with the cold, inevitable click of a design review going terribly wrong. "The pinch in the return channel. Forty percent loss. Every staff, every bracelet, every lamp regulator in the Empire. We've always said it was *wasted*. Lost to heat. Bleeding into the caster's hands, into the focus, into the casing, into the ground." He turned to Vespera. "*Into the ground.* Where does it go, Vespera? When your staff gets hot and you plant the heel on the floor and the heat bleeds out of it? Where does the heat go?"

<br/>

Vespera stared at him. "Into the stone. Into the earth. It… disperses."

<br/>

"It doesn't disperse," said Mark. "It *comes here*."

<br/>

He pointed out over the crystal forest with the candle.

<br/>

"The silver trunks run under the whole city. Under every street. Under every Chapterhouse and every forge and every house with a hearth-lighter. Every bit of heat that gets trapped in a Guild focus and bled out through the ground runs down into the trunks, and down here, into *these*." His voice was very quiet now. "It isn't waste. It never was. It's a *tax*. Every caster in the Empire has been paying forty percent of every spell, in heat, in blisters, in burned hands, for five hundred years. And it's been collected. Down here. Banked."

<br/>

"Banked for *what*?" said Torren.

<br/>

Mark looked at the great black column rising up through the center of the cavern, into the dark, toward the vault above.

<br/>

"For the dam," he said.

<br/>

The cavern was silent except for the deep, endless hum of the trunks, and the faint crackle of the heat.

<br/>

"That's what powers it," Mark said. "The Measure. That's how it's run for five hundred years without anyone maintaining it. It doesn't need maintaining. Everybody in the Empire maintains it, every day, every time they light a lamp or mend a cart or warm their hands. They pay into it with their pain." He looked out at the glowing forest. "Five hundred years of everyone's pain. Banked in a cellar."

<br/>

Nobody said anything.

<br/>

Mark stood on the ledge and looked out at the crystals, and something tugged faintly at the edge of his mind. Something about the ground. About heat, and the ground, and where it went. He reached for it, and couldn't quite catch it, and then Julian made a small, strangled sound beside him and it was gone.

<br/>

Julian was staring out at the crystals, his bandaged hands pressed against his chest.

<br/>

"Every time," he said. "Every time I cast. Every blister. Every time my hands burned and I told myself it was *sacred*." His voice cracked. "It came *here*."

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark quietly.

<br/>

Vespera hadn't moved. She stood on the edge of the ledge with the old ironwood staff in her hand, the one she'd carried for twenty years, and looked out at the crystal forest. Her face, in the red-gold light, was perfectly still. Mark saw her look down at her own right hand, the one that had held the staff, and turn it over, and look at the old, faint, silvery scars across the palm. Twenty years of them. He'd never noticed them before.

<br/>

"Every burn I've ever had," she said, very softly, so softly that he almost didn't hear. "Every one. Since I was twelve." She looked back up at the crystals. "It came down through the ground. Into here."

<br/>

She stood looking out at it for a long moment, and her brow drew down, very slightly, as if something about that sentence had snagged on something else in her mind, and she was trying to see what.

<br/>

*Through the ground.*

<br/>

Then she shook her head, as if to clear it, and gripped her staff, and turned away.

---

Torren was the last to stop staring.

<br/>

He stood at the edge of the ledge with his reading spectacles pushed up on his forehead and his mouth slightly open, and his face, in the red-gold glow, had an expression on it that Mark had only ever seen him wear once before: on the morning he'd first stood in front of the repaired Bessie and felt the clean, cool air come out of her for the first time.

<br/>

"Look at her," Torren breathed.

<br/>

Mark followed his gaze. He was looking at the largest of the crystals, the great pillar nearest the black column, rising up out of the forest like an ancient tree, glowing a deep, steady, patient red.

<br/>

"Five hundred years," Torren murmured. "Holding it all. Every burn in the Empire. Never put down. Never thanked." He shook his head slowly. "Nobody's ever even *looked* at her."

<br/>

"Torren," said Mark.

<br/>

"*Grandmother*," said Torren softly.

<br/>

"*Torren.*"

<br/>

"That's her name," said Torren, with great tenderness and absolutely no intention of being argued with. "She's earned it." He raised one big hand toward the great glowing crystal, a little awkwardly, like a man waving to an old relative across a crowded room. "Hello, Grandmother. Sorry it took so long."

<br/>

Nobody, Mark thought, was going to argue with that either.

---

A narrow stair cut into the rock ran down from the ledge, switching back and forth across the cavern wall, through the crystal forest, toward the floor. They took it single file, sweating, squinting into the red-gold glare, Torren in front because he insisted on testing every step with his heel before he trusted it. The heat grew worse with every turn. By the time they reached the bottom, Mark's shirt was soaked through, and Mott's candle had begun to bend in its tin holder like a wilting flower.

<br/>

He blew it out and put it carefully in his satchel. They didn't need it anymore. The crystals lit everything.

<br/>

The black column stood at the center of the cavern floor, rising out of a ring of the largest crystals like the trunk of a tree out of a ring of roots. Up close, it was enormous: smooth, seamless, unpolished obsidian, wider than Torren's whole workshop, running straight up into the dark of the cavern ceiling, toward the vault and the Chapterhouse and the city above.

<br/>

"That's the Anchor," Julian said quietly. "Isn't it?"

<br/>

"I think so," said Mark. "Or the bottom of it." He tipped his head back and looked up, along the length of the column, into the dark. "Valerius has stood in Deep Vault 3 and looked at it. So has your father. Six feet of obsidian, in the middle of a room full of tax records." He looked back down at the vast black wall in front of him. "That was the tip. *This* is the rest of it."

<br/>

"Like an iceberg," said Lyra.

<br/>

"Like a kiosk," said Mark. "In the lobby. With a nice little screen, so the public can read the notices." He laid his palm flat on the obsidian. It was cool. In this heat, impossibly cool. "And then, three floors down, behind six locked doors, with its own power supply and its own cooling, the actual server."

<br/>

He walked slowly around the base of the column, with his hand trailing along the stone, and the others followed him.

<br/>

On the far side, facing away from the stair, set into the base of the obsidian, he found it.

<br/>

It was an alcove. Shallow and arched, about the height of a man, cut into the face of the column with the same impossible precision as everything else down here. The floor of the alcove was raised slightly, a single smooth step. And on its back wall, at about the height of Mark's chest, were two things, side by side.

<br/>

On the left: a keyhole of polished obsidian, carved around with a circle cut by a single line.

<br/>

On the right: a flat square plate of silver, about the size of Mark's palm, inlaid flush into the black stone. Its surface was covered in fine First Era trace: long, open, sweeping channels, with no pinch anywhere. And it was dark. Not dull, the way Tilly was dull. *Dark*, the way a lamp is dark before it's lit.

<br/>

Mark stood looking at it.

<br/>

"Julian," he said. "The key."

---

Julian fitted Gerald's Cousin into the keyhole, and turned it.

<br/>

There was no heavy clunk this time. Just a soft, smooth click, deep inside the column, and a line of First Era glyphs lit up across the obsidian above the keyhole, pale and faint, like frost forming on a window.

<br/>

Mark's translation layer reached for them and caught them.

<br/>

`[FACTOR 1 OF 2: KEEPER CREDENTIAL ACCEPTED]`

<br/>

He stared at it.

<br/>

Then the line faded, and a second line wrote itself into the stone, just above the dark silver plate:

<br/>

`[FACTOR 2 OF 2: AWAITING]`

<br/>

Mark started to laugh.

<br/>

He didn't mean to. It came up out of him all at once, in the middle of the red-gold heat, at the bottom of a cavern full of five hundred years of the Empire's pain, and he couldn't stop it. He leaned his forehead against the cool black stone and laughed until his eyes watered.

<br/>

"*Mark*," said Lyra, alarmed.

<br/>

"It's two-factor," Mark gasped. "It's *two-factor authentication*."

<br/>

Everyone stared at him.

<br/>

"Something you *have*," said Mark, pointing at the key, "and something you *do*." He pointed at the dark silver plate. "Two locks. Two proofs. You need both. The key proves you're a Keeper." He tapped the plate, very lightly, with one fingertip. "And *this* wants a spell. Run through these traces. And look at the traces, Lyra."

<br/>

Lyra leaned in with her lens. "Open," she breathed. "All of them. No pinch. Every return grounded." She looked up. "It's First Era. It's *clean*. It wants a clean cast."

<br/>

"It wants a clean cast," Mark agreed. He straightened up, wiping his eyes. "And who gave the Keepers their key?"

<br/>

"The Concord," said Julian slowly.

<br/>

"And who forbade clean casting? Everywhere? For everyone? On pain of correction? For five hundred years?"

<br/>

Julian's face changed.

<br/>

"The Concord," he said.

<br/>

"Twenty generations," said Mark. "Twenty generations of Thornes, every one of them carrying a key, every one of them coming down here once a year to check the seals and write *nothing to report*. Your father, standing in the vault upstairs, reading the little glyphs on the kiosk, trying to make it *answer*." He shook his head. "And they could never, ever have opened it. Not one of them. Not in five hundred years. Because they only ever had *one factor*." He looked at the plate. "The Concord gave the Keepers a key to a door they made sure the Keepers could never open."

<br/>

There was a long silence.

<br/>

"Why?" said Torren. "Why would anyone *do* that?"

<br/>

"I don't know yet," said Mark. He looked at the dark silver plate. "But I'll bet it's in there."

---

Then Vespera said, very quietly, "The hall."

<br/>

Everyone turned.

<br/>

She was standing at the edge of the alcove, with the old ironwood staff in both hands, looking back across the cavern toward the narrow stair that switched up the wall to the ledge, and the passage beyond it, and the three steps, and the arch, and the long, long hall of frosted silver figures with their heads bowed.

<br/>

"A clean cast," Vespera said. "Down here. At the bottom of a cavern full of crystals." She looked at Mark. "It'll ring, won't it? Like the shed. Fourteen thousand cycles."

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark.

<br/>

"And the rack room is directly above us."

<br/>

"Yes."

<br/>

"And Lyra's strip of silver turned a head, just by being *right*."

<br/>

Mark didn't answer that. He didn't need to.

<br/>

The heat crackled around them. In the crystal forest, Grandmother glowed her patient red-gold.

<br/>

"Then I'll hold the stair," said Vespera.

<br/>

"Vespera—"

<br/>

"I'm *marked*, Mark." She held up her left wrist, and the pale grey glyphs caught the red light. "If I cast clean, they come for me *and* the Auditor comes for me. I can't help you at that plate. I can't help you with anything clean. Not anymore." She turned the ironwood staff in her hands, slowly, and looked at it. "But I can still do it the old way."

<br/>

Lyra stared at her. "With the *staff*? With the—the *pinch*? Vespera, it'll *burn* you—"

<br/>

"I know exactly what it will do," said Vespera calmly. "I did it every day for twenty years." She looked at the crystal forest, at the trunks running into it, at the vast patient glow of five hundred years of everyone's pain. "And every burn I ever had came down here and fed the wall that's going to keep the world quiet while you open that door." The corner of her mouth moved, very slightly. "This one will, too. I can live with that."

<br/>

"I'll go with her," said Torren at once. "In case anything needs tackling."

<br/>

"Torren—"

<br/>

"I'm quarter-dwarf," said Torren. "And I sneezed in that hall and nothing happened. I'm *very lucky*." He was already moving, walking beside Vespera toward the foot of the stair, rolling up his sleeves. "And somebody has to stand next to her," he added, without looking back, "and make sure she doesn't do it alone."

<br/>

Vespera didn't say anything to that. But Mark saw her glance sideways at the big, broad, sweating smith walking beside her, and saw something in her face soften.

<br/>

They took up their positions at the foot of the stair: Vespera on the bottom step, staff planted, Torren beside her with his feet set wide and his fists loosely at his sides, both of them looking up toward the ledge and the dark passage beyond.

<br/>

Mark watched them for a moment.

<br/>

Then he turned back to the alcove, and opened his satchel, and took out Prototype 2.0.

---

Julian stood on his left. Lyra stood on his right, with her hand pressed flat over the twelfth pocket of her apron.

<br/>

Mark buckled the cuff around his right wrist. The leather was warm from the satchel, and the two crystals in their sockets glowed a steady, cool, impossible blue against the red-gold light of the cavern, like two drops of clear water in a forge.

<br/>

He hadn't cast clean in nine days. Not since the night before the Auditor arrived. He'd forgotten what it felt like to have the cuff on, live, in the dark, with his thumb resting against the tiny silver pin.

<br/>

He hadn't forgotten anything else.

<br/>

"When I do this," he said quietly, "the room's going to go cold. Fast. And there'll be a chime. And then I think a great many things are going to happen at once."

<br/>

"I know," said Lyra.

<br/>

"Stay behind me."

<br/>

"I know, Mark."

<br/>

He looked at Julian. Julian looked back at him, very pale in the red light, his bandaged hands clenched at his sides.

<br/>

"For the girl," Julian said quietly. "From the coast."

<br/>

Mark held his gaze for a moment. Then he nodded, once.

<br/>

He turned to the dark silver plate. He took a slow, deep breath through his nose, filling his diaphragm. *Inhale: four counts.* He dropped his weight into his heels, the way he had a thousand times, in the shed and the arena and the archive. *Exhale: four counts.* Root.

<br/>

He laid his right palm flat against the silver.

<br/>

He nudged the pin to Beta.

<br/>

*Click.*

---

It was exactly like the shed, and it was nothing like the shed.

<br/>

The crystals on his wrist flared. The current came up through his feet and his legs and his spine and his arm, clean and smooth and frictionless, no pinch anywhere, no heat in his hand at all, and poured out of his palm into the open First Era traces on the plate, and the traces *drank it*. He felt them take it. He felt every long, sweeping channel fill, one after another, like water filling the grooves of a carving, until the whole plate under his hand was lit with a pale, clean, silver-white light.

<br/>

And the cavern rang.

<br/>

The sound came up out of the plate, and out of the column, and out of the stone under his feet, and out of every crystal in the forest at once: a single, pure, crystalline note at fourteen thousand cycles, so loud and so clean that it went through Mark's skull like a struck bell and set his teeth humming. It rolled out across the cavern. It went up the walls. It went up the stair, and through the passage, and up the three steps, and through the arch.

<br/>

And the cold came.

<br/>

All the heat in the cavern went out of it at once, the way the heat had gone out of the shed. The red-gold glow of the crystals flickered and dimmed. The air dropped forty degrees in a heartbeat. Mark's breath burst out of him in a white cloud. Frost raced across the floor of the alcove, across the face of the column, across the backs of his hands, across the silver plate, glittering.

<br/>

Somewhere far above, in the dark, there was a sound.

<br/>

It was very small. A faint, crisp, brittle *crack*, like the first crack of ice on a pond.

<br/>

Then another.

<br/>

Then dozens of them, all at once, a long, rolling, crackling cascade, like a whole frozen lake breaking up in the spring.

<br/>

*Frost,* Mark thought. *Falling off their shoulders.*

<br/>

"*Mark!*" Lyra shouted.

<br/>

He didn't take his hand off the plate.

<br/>

At the foot of the stair, Vespera raised the ironwood staff over her head with both hands.

<br/>

And then, up on the ledge, in the mouth of the dark passage, something moved: a tall, narrow, silver-white shape, seven feet high, ducking under the arch. Its blank oval face turned and looked down across the cavern, at the column, at the alcove, at the boy with his palm flat against the silver plate. Then it stepped out onto the stair, and started down. Behind it, another shape ducked under the arch. And behind that one, another.

<br/>

Vespera began to chant.

<br/>

Mark had never heard her chant before. In all their months together, she had only ever cast silently, or not at all. Now she raised her voice, and it filled the frozen cavern: High Imperial, ancient and rolling and precise, every syllable cut clean, the full forty-line incantation of a kinetic repulsion, the way she had been taught at twelve years old by masters who believed that magic must hurt.

<br/>

The old ironwood staff blazed. Sparks poured off the silver tip in a crackling cascade. The heat came up through the staff into her hands, Mark saw it come, saw her flinch, saw her grip tighten instead of loosen, saw her plant her feet and keep chanting through it, her voice never once wavering.

<br/>

And on the last syllable, she swept the staff forward, and a great invisible wall of force tore up the stair and struck the first construct full in the chest.

<br/>

It flew backward up the steps, into the second, and the third, and all three of them went down in a tangle of silver limbs, sliding and crashing back up the stair toward the ledge.

<br/>

Vespera staggered. Torren caught her by the shoulders before she fell.

<br/>

She didn't drop the staff. Her palms, where they gripped the ironwood, were already red and blistering. She looked down at them, breathing hard. Then she looked up the stair at the silver figures picking themselves up, and set her feet again, and began the chant a second time.

<br/>

"*How long?*" Torren roared over the chime, looking back toward the alcove.

<br/>

Mark didn't know.

<br/>

He kept his hand on the plate. The silver-white light under his palm was spreading. Out of the plate, across the obsidian, up the face of the column in long, branching lines, like frost, like roots, like the silver trunks running through every wall of the city above. The lines raced up the black stone, higher and higher, until they vanished into the dark of the ceiling, toward the vault, toward the kiosk in the lobby, toward the red glyph and the gold glyph that had burned there for months.

<br/>

And then, at the top of the column, very far above, in the dark, a third light came on.

<br/>

Mark saw it. A single glyph, shining down through the dark like a star: not red, not gold, but a pale, clean, brilliant silver-white, the exact color of the light under his hand.

<br/>

The chime stopped.

<br/>

In the sudden silence, the frost on the column began to melt, and drip.

<br/>

At the foot of the stair, a long, faint, shimmering curtain of silver-white light dropped down out of the dark across the bottom of the steps, between Vespera and the constructs, as smooth and silent as a sheet of glass. The first construct, three steps above it, reached for Vespera with one long silver arm, and its hand touched the curtain, and stopped.

<br/>

It didn't break through. It didn't push. It simply stopped, with its fingers flat against the light, and stood there, and waited.

<br/>

Behind it, the others stopped too, one after another, on the stair, in a long silver line, and stood very still.

<br/>

Vespera lowered her staff, very slowly. Her hands were shaking.

<br/>

"What," she said hoarsely, "is *that*?"

<br/>

Mark knew. He'd seen it a thousand times, in a thousand server rooms, the moment an administrator logged into a critical system and the system, quite sensibly, locked everyone else out while he worked.

<br/>

"Session lock," he said. "It's protecting the console. While it's in use."

<br/>

He looked down at his hand, still pressed flat against the silver plate.

<br/>

And under his palm, across the whole face of the column in front of him, the obsidian *rippled*.

<br/>

It moved like water. Like the surface of a dark pool when a stone drops in. The ripples spread out from the plate, across the black stone, wider and wider, and then they slowed, and smoothed, and stilled. And where they had been, the face of the column was no longer rough, unpolished obsidian. It was a surface. Flat. Dark. Faintly luminous. Waiting.

<br/>

Julian and Lyra stood frozen on either side of him.

<br/>

Slowly, very slowly, Mark took his hand off the plate.

<br/>

The surface stayed lit.

<br/>

`[SESSION OPEN]`

### *Stinger: Oakhaven*

<br/>

At the third landing, Corwin sat up.

<br/>

He did it all at once, without any of his usual slow, reluctant stages. One moment he was lying flat on his back on the cold stone step with his arms folded over his chest. The next, he was sitting bolt upright, with both eyes open, staring down the dark stairwell below him, where the last echoes of a single pure crystalline note were still fading away into the stone.

<br/>

He listened for a long moment.

<br/>

Then he turned, cupped his hands around his mouth, and called up the stairwell, toward the archive, and Hollis, and the world above. He didn't call it once.

<br/>

He called it twice.

<br/>

"*Margaret!*" Corwin shouted. "*MARGARET!*"

<br/>

At the top of the archive stairs, Hollis Grey dropped his lantern, turned, and ran.

<br/>

At the Academy's rear gate, Pip heard it, and went white, and put two fingers in his mouth and whistled, and down the lane a runner heard the whistle and shouted *Margaret*, and down the next lane another runner shouted it, and the word went out across the sleeping city in every direction at once, from corner to corner, from child to child, at the speed of small bare feet.

<br/>

In the doorway of the Fish & Hammer workshop, Garek heard it.

<br/>

He'd been standing in that doorway for two weeks. He looked down at the threshold under his boots for a moment, as if saying goodbye to it.

<br/>

Then he stepped out of the doorway for the first time in fourteen days, and started to run, and above his head, in the empty frame, Legacy drew all eight legs in tight and watched him go.

On the north side of the city, three streets from the Chapterhouse, the Auditor was halfway between two houses.

<br/>

It had come out of the chandler's shop on Wick Lane at exactly the speed it always walked, and turned left, as it always turned left, toward the next door in its grid: a narrow green door belonging to a widow who mended nets. It was perhaps four steps from that door when it stopped.

<br/>

It stood in the middle of the dark street, perfectly still, for a long moment.

<br/>

Then, slowly, it turned its blank, slate-grey face away from the widow's green door, toward the south, toward the dark tower of the Oakhaven Chapterhouse, and the vaults beneath it, and the column beneath those.

<br/>

On the front of its grey robe, over the place where a heart would have been, a single faint glyph flickered and lit. Silver-white.

<br/>

The Auditor turned away from the green door.

<br/>

And for the first time since it had walked through the North Gate of Oakhaven, it left its grid. It skipped the widow's house, and the house after that, and the whole of the rest of Wick Lane, and walked straight down the middle of the street toward the Chapterhouse, at exactly the same speed as always.

<br/>

Behind it, the lamps did not go out.

Forty miles to the north, on the dark road from the capital, a black carriage with no horses and no driver was already moving south.

<br/>

It had left an hour before.

<br/>

Inside, in the dark, Lord Aldric Thorne sat alone on the grey velvet seat with his hands folded in his lap, looking out of the window at the night.

<br/>

On the third finger of his right hand, the stone in his ring had changed. It wasn't gold anymore. It was a clean, pale, brilliant silver-white.

<br/>

He looked down at it for a long time.

<br/>

"There you are," he said softly.

<br/>

And the carriage rolled on toward Oakhaven, without a sound.
