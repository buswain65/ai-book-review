## Chapter 18: The Skunkworks & The Hidden Directive

<br/>

Word of the anvil spread through the Academy faster than the rumor of Garek's beating had spread through the timber yards, and it arrived in roughly the same condition.

<br/>

By breakfast the next morning, Mark had lifted the anvil "with a single glance." By lunch, he had "looked Archmage Ignis in the eye and made his beard catch fire." By the end of afternoon lectures, a nervous first-year in the corridor stepped aside to let him pass and whispered, "Please don't do the thing."

<br/>

"What thing?" Mark asked.

<br/>

The boy fled.

<br/>

*"Version 4.2 again,"* Mark thought. *"Marketing never sleeps."*

<br/>

He found Corwin in the courtyard after the last bell, lying full-length on a marble bench in a patch of sun with his arms folded over his chest like a knight on a tomb.

<br/>

"You're famous," Corwin informed him, without opening his eyes.

<br/>

"I noticed."

<br/>

"Julian Thorne noticed too. He's been asking people what time your disciplinary shift starts." Corwin cracked one eye. "That's not a friendly question, in case you were wondering."

<br/>

"I wasn't," Mark said. "But thank you."

<br/>

Corwin closed the eye again. "Free advice, since you're going down there tonight. The archives are fine. Mott's fine. He's a hundred and four and deaf as a post, but he's fine." He paused. "Don't go below the third landing."

<br/>

"What's below the third landing?"

<br/>

"Nobody knows," Corwin said. "That's the point. People who go below the third landing come back weird."

<br/>

"Weird how?"

<br/>

Corwin considered it seriously. "Quiet," he said at last. "They come back quiet. And they don't sit with their backs to doors anymore."

<br/>

Mark looked at him. Corwin's face gave nothing away, which was either a sign of deep sincerity or of a nap already in progress.

<br/>

"Noted," Mark said.

<br/>

"Third landing," Corwin repeated, drifting off. "Good luck with the plates."

---

At precisely eight o'clock, Mark descended into the Subterranean Archival Vaults.

<br/>

The stairs went down a long way. The air grew cooler and drier with every turn, and the smell shifted from candle wax and floor polish to something older: stone dust, cold iron, and parchment that had given up on being parchment centuries ago. Blue Aether lamps were set into the walls at intervals, each one dimmer than the last, as if the budget for lighting had been cut a little more with every landing.

<br/>

*"Classic,"* Mark thought. *"The further you get from the executive floor, the worse the infrastructure."*

<br/>

At the first landing, a heavy iron door stood open. Beyond it, a vast, low-ceilinged chamber stretched into the gloom, lined with shelves, crates, and tables.

<br/>

And plates.

<br/>

There were thousands of them. Brass plates, silver plates, bronze discs, iron tablets. They were stacked on shelves, stacked on tables, stacked on the floor, and in one corner stacked in a leaning tower nearly as tall as Mark himself, which was held upright by what appeared to be a broom and optimism.

<br/>

At a small desk just inside the door sat an extremely old man wrapped in several layers of grey wool, writing slowly in a ledger with a quill that had been sharpened so many times it was barely longer than his finger.

<br/>

"Archivist Mott?" Mark asked.

<br/>

The old man did not look up.

<br/>

"*Archivist Mott?*" Mark said, louder.

<br/>

Mott looked up, peered at him through spectacles as thick as bottle-bottoms, and smiled with genuine warmth. "Ah! Are you one of the new ones?"

<br/>

"I'm the disciplinary assistant. Apprentice Vance. Archmage Ignis sent me."

<br/>

"Lovely, lovely," Mott said, nodding. "Are you one of the new ones?"

<br/>

"...Yes," Mark said.

<br/>

"Wonderful." Mott pointed a trembling finger at the chamber. "Index sorting. Everything needs indexing. Been needing it for years."

<br/>

"Is there an existing index I should work from?"

<br/>

Mott rummaged under his desk and produced a single scroll, which he handed over with enormous ceremony. Mark unrolled it.

<br/>

It said, in elegant calligraphy: *PLATES (various).*

<br/>

Mark rolled it back up.

<br/>

"Very thorough," he said.

<br/>

"Took me eleven years," Mott said proudly, and went back to his ledger.

---

For the first two hours, Mark simply walked the stacks, trying to understand the shape of the problem.

<br/>

There was no system. That, he eventually decided, *was* the system. Plates had been sorted, as far as he could tell, by size, by color, by the date they'd been carried downstairs, and in one section apparently by whether the person carrying them had been in a good mood that day. Five centuries of rune templates had been heaped down here by generations of archivists, each of whom had evidently looked at the previous archivist's arrangement and quietly started a new pile.

<br/>

Somewhere in the middle of the room, Mark stood in front of the broom-supported tower and felt a wave of something he hadn't felt since Raleigh.

<br/>

It wasn't despair. It was the opposite.

<br/>

This was a legacy database. No schema. No documentation. No indexing. A thousand years of data dumped into an unstructured heap by people who had no idea what any of it meant.

<br/>

He'd once been paid four hundred dollars an hour to untangle exactly this kind of mess.

<br/>

*"I've been given the worst job in the building,"* Mark thought, rolling up his sleeves. *"And it's the best job in the building."*

<br/>

He found a crate of blank parchment offcuts, tore them into neat rectangles, and started writing. Each plate got a card: size, metal, estimated age, condition, and a short sketch of its trace pattern. The cards went into an empty drawer in order.

<br/>

By midnight, he had indexed forty-three plates out of what he conservatively estimated to be eleven thousand.

<br/>

"Database migration," he told Mott on his way out. "Phase one."

<br/>

"Lovely," Mott said. "Are you one of the new ones?"

---

The next morning, before lectures, Mark slipped a folded note to a kitchen runner bound for the town, addressed to Mage Vespera at the Oakhaven Chapterhouse.

<br/>

It contained a single question: *Does Statute 402 allow a sponsored apprentice's named supervisors to attend his research activities?*

<br/>

By the afternoon, the answer came back in Vespera's neat hand.

<br/>

*Yes. Section 4, subsection 2. Supervisors may attend the apprentice at any research-related activity, including field and archival work. Why are you asking? What are you planning? Mark. MARK. Write back.*

<br/>

Mark wrote back: *Archival work. Disciplinary duty involves handling First Era plates. Per Statute 402, all handling of rune plates by an apprentice requires supervision by his named Engraving Specialist.*

<br/>

The next note was shorter. It just said: *You are a menace.*

<br/>

The note after that was a stamped and sealed writ of supervisory access, signed by Mage Vespera, Academic Supervisor, granting Journeyman Lyra, Engraving Specialist, full attendance rights during Apprentice Vance's archival duties.

<br/>

Ignis, Mark reflected, had sent him to the vaults as a punishment. If he objected to Mark's supervisor attending, he'd have to argue that the vault duty wasn't research-related, which meant admitting it was punishment for doing his assignment correctly.

<br/>

*"Checkmate,"* Mark thought. *"Or whatever the local equivalent is. Probably something involving goats."*

---

Lyra arrived at the first landing at five minutes past eight, out of breath, with her tool roll slung across her chest and her apron pockets bulging in at least nine places.

<br/>

She handed her writ to Mott. Mott squinted at it, then at her.

<br/>

"Are you one of the new ones?"

<br/>

"Yes," Lyra said.

<br/>

Mott looked at the writ again, unconvinced, and Mark watched his hand hovering over the ledger with the doubt of a gatekeeper on the edge of asking for further documentation.

<br/>

Lyra sighed, reached into her apron, and pulled out a brass clipboard.

<br/>

She set the writ on the clipboard and handed it back.

<br/>

Mott's spine straightened a quarter-inch. "Lovely," he said, and waved her through.

<br/>

Mark stared at her.

<br/>

"I made a second one," Lyra said, not quite meeting his eye. "After the audit. I've been carrying it around. Just in case."

<br/>

"That," Mark said, with deep professional respect, "is the most important tool you own."

<br/>

"I know," Lyra said grimly. "I hate that I know."

<br/>

She stepped into the chamber, looked around at the shelves, the crates, the piles, the leaning tower, and went completely still.

<br/>

"There are," she said slowly, "thousands of plates in here."

<br/>

"Eleven thousand, give or take."

<br/>

"And they're just... piled."

<br/>

"Archivist Mott's system," Mark said. "It's called 'pile.'"

<br/>

Lyra turned in a slow circle. Mark could see her expression shifting: horror, then indignation, and then something else entirely, a hungry, craftsman's gleam as she began to recognize what she was looking at.

<br/>

"Some of these," she whispered, "are *centuries* old."

<br/>

"Some of them," Mark said, "might be a thousand years old. That's why you're here."

<br/>

He showed her his card catalog. Lyra flipped through it for thirty seconds, frowning more deeply with every card.

<br/>

"You're sorting them by size," she said.

<br/>

"It's a reasonable first-pass key."

<br/>

"It's a terrible first-pass key. Size tells you nothing." She pulled a plate from the pile, held it under the nearest lamp, and ran her thumbnail lightly along one of the grooves. "You sort them by tool marks. Every era used different gravers. Different angles, different steel, different hands. Look—this one was cut with a round-bellied graver, probably two hundred years old, maybe two-fifty. The burr is on the left, so the engraver was left-handed. That narrows it further."

<br/>

Mark stared at her. "You can date them from the cut."

<br/>

"Any decent journeyman can date a plate from the cut," Lyra said, then paused. "Fine. Any *very good* journeyman."

<br/>

"Peer review," Mark said. "Again."

<br/>

"You're alphabetizing wrong, too," Lyra added, already pulling his cards out of the drawer and re-sorting them on the table with rapid, decisive flicks. "Nobody files 'Bronze, Thermal' before 'Brass, Kinetic.' It's metal first, then function."

<br/>

"Noted."

<br/>

"Then *age*," Lyra said, pointing at him with a card. "Which I'll do. Because you can't."

<br/>

"Agreed," Mark said. "You read the cuts. I'll read the circuits."

<br/>

She nodded once, sharply, the matter settled, and began unloading her pockets onto the table. Calipers. Rouge. A spare graver. A small lamp-lens. Two bread rolls. And, wrapped in a clean cloth, a chunk of hard yellow cheese.

<br/>

Mark looked at the cheese.

<br/>

"It's a *new* cheese," Lyra said firmly. "I'm not ready to talk about the old cheese."

---

They worked in a rhythm that was by now familiar.

<br/>

Lyra moved through the stacks like a hawk over a field, pulling plates, turning them under the lamplight, murmuring to herself—"three hundred," "four-fifty," "fake, someone recut this," "oh, that's lovely"—and stacking them into rough eras. Mark followed behind, card in hand, sketching each one's trace layout, cataloging the circuit architecture.

<br/>

Within a few hours, a pattern began to emerge.

<br/>

Every plate from the last few centuries, no matter the function—thermal, kinetic, illumination, structural—shared the same flaw. The same deliberate narrowing of the return channel. The same "sacred resistance" Mark had first spotted in Torren's broken blower.

<br/>

It was everywhere. It was in every template.

<br/>

"It's like a signature," Lyra murmured, holding two plates side by side. One was a heat-ward, the other a wagon-mend. Completely different functions. The same pinched return channel, at exactly the same ratio. "Forty percent narrowing. Every time. Even when it doesn't make sense for the design."

<br/>

"It never makes sense for the design," Mark said. "That's what bothered me from the start. Real engineering constraints vary with the use case. This doesn't vary at all. It's not a design decision. It's a policy."

<br/>

Around midnight, Mott shuffled past them on his way to the stairs, holding a candle.

<br/>

"Lovely work," he said, glancing at the sorted stacks without much interest. "Mind you lock the old cases behind you. Back row. Nobody's touched them in ages."

<br/>

Mark and Lyra looked at each other.

<br/>

"What old cases?" Mark called after him.

<br/>

But Mott was already climbing the stairs, very slowly, one step at a time, and either did not hear or had already forgotten the question.

---

The old cases were in the back row, behind the leaning tower, under a tarp so thick with dust that it had effectively turned into a second, older tarp.

<br/>

There were three of them, iron-bound, with heavy hasp locks long since rusted open. When Lyra lifted the lid of the first, a breath of air escaped that smelled of nothing at all—a flat, ancient emptiness, as if the inside of the case had forgotten what smells were.

<br/>

Inside, nested in cloth that crumbled at a touch, lay a row of plates.

<br/>

They were not brass. They were not bronze. They were a pale, silvery-white metal that Mark didn't recognize, unblemished by tarnish, and when Lyra lifted the first one into the lamplight, she inhaled sharply and didn't let the breath out again for a long moment.

<br/>

"Mark," she whispered. "Look at the cut."

<br/>

He looked.

<br/>

The grooves were impossibly fine. Each line was a single, continuous stroke, perfectly even in depth from end to end. No hesitation marks. No overcuts. No burrs. The traces curved and branched and rejoined each other in elegant, flowing geometries that made every modern template Mark had seen look like a child's scribble on a napkin.

<br/>

"I can't do this," Lyra said quietly. There was no envy in her voice, just a kind of awe. "Nobody alive can do this. Master Vane can't do this. I don't even know what tool would make a line like that." She tilted the plate. "This is First Era. It has to be. Nothing after the founding of the Empire looks like this."

<br/>

"How old?"

<br/>

"A thousand years," Lyra said. "At least."

<br/>

Mark pulled out his magnifying lens and bent over the plate.

<br/>

His breath stopped.

<br/>

The return channel was full width. Not pinched. Not narrowed. A perfectly balanced trace, as wide as the primary channel, exactly as any sensible engineer would have designed it.

<br/>

And that wasn't all. Running along the outer edge of the plate, carefully separated from the logic traces by a precise gap, was a second, thinner channel that looped all the way around the disc and terminated in a small round pad at the rim.

<br/>

"Lyra," Mark said softly. "What does that look like to you?"

<br/>

She leaned in. Her eyes went wide.

<br/>

"That's a grounding channel," she breathed. "That's the grounding channel I said we should add. In your shed. During the initial casting phase." She looked up at him, almost accusingly. "They already *had* it."

<br/>

"They had it a thousand years ago," Mark said. "Built in. Designed from the start."

<br/>

He sat back on his heels, staring at the plate in his hands, and felt the same cold electricity run down his spine that he'd felt in Torren's shed the night he'd first recognized the runes as circuit traces.

<br/>

"The original architecture was clean," he said. "Grounded. Balanced. Efficient. No thermal buildup. No melted silver. No burned nerves. This is what Guild magic was *supposed* to look like."

<br/>

"Then what happened?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

Mark turned the First Era plate over in his hands, slowly.

<br/>

"Someone patched it," he said.

---

To prove it, Mark did what any engineer would do with two versions of the same file.

<br/>

He diffed them.

<br/>

He laid the First Era plate on the table under the brightest lamp. Beside it, he laid a modern Guild template of the same function—a basic kinetic-lift array, near-identical to the one that should have powered Julian's staff in the lecture hall.

<br/>

Then he and Lyra went over both, line by line, trace by trace, with calipers and magnifying lens, calling out measurements to each other like surgeons over an operating table.

<br/>

"Primary channel, inlet."

<br/>

"Matches."

<br/>

"Branch junction, first split."

<br/>

"Matches. Angle's a little sloppier on the modern one, but same geometry."

<br/>

"Logic gate cluster, center."

<br/>

"Matches."

<br/>

"Return channel."

<br/>

A pause.

<br/>

"Doesn't match," Lyra said quietly. "Narrowed. Forty percent."

<br/>

"Grounding loop."

<br/>

Another pause.

<br/>

"Gone," Lyra said. "Completely gone on the modern one. There's not even a trace of where it was."

<br/>

Mark sat back.

<br/>

"It's the same circuit," he said. "The *exact* same circuit. Someone took the original design, kept everything that made it work, and made exactly two changes: they strangled the return path, and they deleted the ground." He tapped the modern plate. "That's not drift. That's not a thousand years of copying errors. Copying errors are random. This is surgical."

<br/>

Lyra had gone pale.

<br/>

"But when?" she said. "If the First Era plates are a thousand years old, and the modern ones are all like this, then somewhere in between, someone—"

<br/>

She stopped. Then she stood up abruptly, grabbed the lamp, and plunged back into the stacks.

<br/>

Mark followed her. She was moving fast, pulling plates from the piles she'd already dated, discarding them, pulling more. "Four hundred," she muttered. "Four-fifty. Restricted. Restricted. Four-eighty—restricted—"

<br/>

She stopped at a shelf in the darkest corner of the room and pulled out a battered bronze disc, its surface scarred and tarnished nearly black.

<br/>

She held it to the lamp. Then she held very still.

<br/>

"Mark," she said. "Come here."

<br/>

He came.

<br/>

The plate was old—older than anything else on the shelf. Its primary traces had the same long, continuous strokes as the First Era plates, though cruder, the work of a lesser hand imitating a greater one. But the return channel was different.

<br/>

Someone had filled the original, full-width return channel with solder. You could still see the faint outline of it under the tarnish, like a ghost. Then they had cut a *new*, narrower channel through the solder fill, with a different tool, at a different angle.

<br/>

And the grounding loop at the rim had been filed off. The scratches were still there, deep and careless, as if whoever had done it had been in a hurry.

<br/>

"It's a transition plate," Lyra whispered. "Someone took an original plate, one that was built correctly, and recut it. By hand. Look at the tool marks on the new channel—that's a straight graver, early Imperial. And the solder's a lead-tin alloy we stopped using four centuries ago." She swallowed. "This plate was *modified*, Mark. Not designed this way. *Changed.*"

<br/>

"How long ago?"

<br/>

Lyra looked at the cut for a long time before she answered.

<br/>

"Five hundred years," she said. "Give or take a decade."

<br/>

Mark felt the number settle into place in his head with an almost audible *click*.

<br/>

"Five hundred years," he repeated slowly. "The Anchor went dark five hundred years ago."

<br/>

Lyra stared at him.

<br/>

"Ignis said it," Mark went on, half to himself. "'An insult to five hundred years of Chapterhouse tradition.' Master Vane said five hundred years of Imperial casting. The Anchor hasn't registered a single anomaly in five hundred years. And the earliest altered plate in this archive is five hundred years old."

<br/>

He turned the transition plate over in his fingers.

<br/>

"That's not a coincidence, Lyra. That's a deployment date. Five hundred years ago, someone pushed a patch to every rune template in the Empire. And on the same day, the Anchor stopped seeing anything worth reporting." He set the plate down very carefully. "Because there was nothing left to report. They'd rate-limited everything."

---

The proof of *who* turned up twenty minutes later, in a place nobody would have thought to look: the inside of the lid of the third old case.

<br/>

Lyra found it because she was running her fingers along the case's hinges to check the tool marks on the iron. Her fingertips caught on something under the dust—a sheet of thin, brittle parchment, pasted to the inside of the lid, so old and dark it was nearly the color of the wood.

<br/>

Mark held the lamp close. The writing was faded but legible, the same angular, vertically stacked script as the tax decree in Oakhaven's plaza, and his translation layer rendered it into English as smoothly as ever:

<br/>

*By order of the Concord, in the first year of the Measure:*

<br/>

*The Sacred Restriction shall be cut into all templates henceforth, without exception. Let the return be narrowed. Let the earth-loop be struck from every pattern. Let no Mage draw beyond the Measure.*

<br/>

*The originals shall be sealed and set below, and spoken of no more.*

<br/>

Below it was a seal pressed into dark wax: a circle, divided by a single horizontal line.

<br/>

That was all. No names. No signatures.

<br/>

Lyra read it twice. Then she sat down hard on an upturned crate.

<br/>

"'Set below,'" she said faintly. "They didn't destroy the originals. They just... put them down here. And told everyone to forget."

<br/>

"That's what you do with source code you don't want people to see," Mark said. "You don't delete it. Deleting it is suspicious. You just archive it somewhere nobody looks, under a file name nobody understands." He looked around at the piles, the shelves, the leaning tower, the scroll labeled *PLATES (various)*. "And then you let five hundred years of bad filing do the rest."

<br/>

"Who's the Concord?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

"I don't know," Mark said. "I've never heard the name. Have you?"

<br/>

Lyra shook her head slowly. "Never. Not in the workshop. Not in any of the histories they made us read."

<br/>

"Then whoever they were," Mark said quietly, "they did a very good job of not being remembered."

<br/>

He looked at the seal: a circle, cut by a line.

<br/>

It looked, he thought uneasily, a great deal like a limit.

---

It was nearly half past eleven. Their shift ended at midnight. And Mark had a problem.

<br/>

Everything they'd found so far was evidence. Tool marks, solder, a pasted-in decree with no names on it. It was compelling. It was damning. But it wasn't *proof*. Not the kind he trusted.

<br/>

He wanted to see the First Era plate work.

<br/>

"No," Lyra said, as soon as she saw him looking at it.

<br/>

"I haven't said anything yet."

<br/>

"You were going to." She crossed her arms. "Last time you ran a clean circuit, the whole room froze, Gerald died, and an Imperial Inspector showed up at dawn. And now you want to run a clean circuit through a *thousand-year-old plate* in a vault that sits on top of the *Anchor*."

<br/>

"Above the Anchor," Mark corrected. "Several floors above."

<br/>

"*Mark.*"

<br/>

"Alpha mode only," Mark said. "One crystal. The lowest possible draw. Barely a trickle. In and out in under a second." He held up his hands. "It's a unit test, Lyra. You don't ship a fix without testing it. You test it in a sandbox. Small input, controlled environment, minimal side effects."

<br/>

"Is this a sandbox?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

Mark looked around at the ancient, silent, stone-walled chamber, deep underground, above a red-pulsing First Era relic that had already flagged his last experiment as a security incident.

<br/>

"It's... sandbox-adjacent," he admitted.

<br/>

Lyra stared at him for a long moment. Then she let out a breath that was half sigh and half growl.

<br/>

"If something comes up through that floor," she said, "I'm telling it this was your idea."

<br/>

"That's fair."

<br/>

"And I'm taking my name off the schematic."

<br/>

"Also fair."

<br/>

She stepped back to the far side of the table and pressed herself against the shelves. After a moment's thought, she picked up the cheese and held it in front of her like a shield.

<br/>

Mark set the First Era plate flat on the stone table. He took a short length of silver wire from Lyra's roll, touched one end to the plate's inlet pad and the other to the grounding loop's terminal at the rim, and then, very lightly, touched the copper plate of his wristband to the wire.

<br/>

He checked the switch. Alpha. He checked it again.

<br/>

*Inhale: four counts.*

<br/>

*Exhale.*

<br/>

He let the smallest trickle of current he could manage slide down his arm, through the cuff, through the wire, and into the plate.

<br/>

The ancient traces lit up.

<br/>

It was not like the blue glow of modern Guild magic, harsh and flickering and spitting sparks. The light ran through the First Era grooves like water through a riverbed—smooth, even, a soft and perfect silver-white that filled every channel at once, rose to a gentle steady brightness, and held there without a single flicker. The return channel carried its share of the load without strain. The grounding loop at the rim glowed faintly as it drained the excess into the table.

<br/>

It was utterly silent. And it was beautiful.

<br/>

A thin rime of frost bloomed across the surface of the plate, delicate as lace, and then slowly faded.

<br/>

Lyra lowered the cheese an inch. "Oh," she whispered.

<br/>

Mark lifted his wrist away. The light drained out of the traces as smoothly as it had come.

<br/>

"Zero loss," he said quietly. "Zero heat. Zero noise." He looked down at the plate with something close to reverence. "This is how it's supposed to work. This was always how it was supposed to work."

<br/>

For three long seconds, the vault was completely still.

<br/>

Then, from somewhere far below them—beneath the floor, beneath the second landing, beneath the third—came a sound.

<br/>

It was a single chime. Not the high, piercing fourteen-kilohertz ring that had shattered the air in Mark's shed. This one was low. Slow. A deep, resonant tone that seemed to rise up through the stone itself, so deep that Mark felt it more in the soles of his feet than in his ears, like the long, groaning note of something very large shifting its weight after a very long sleep.

<br/>

The sound faded.

<br/>

Neither of them moved.

<br/>

Mark found he was holding his breath. Across the table, Lyra had the cheese raised all the way back up to her chin.

<br/>

Ten seconds passed. Twenty. Thirty.

<br/>

Nothing else happened. No flash. No frost. No second chime. The blue lamps on the walls burned steadily. Somewhere upstairs, very faintly, a door closed.

<br/>

"What," Lyra whispered, "was *that*?"

<br/>

"Probably nothing," Mark said.

<br/>

They both looked at the floor.

<br/>

"It's an old building," Mark added. "Old buildings make noises. Pipes. Settling stone. It's—" He stopped, because he could hear how he sounded, and he'd heard that exact tone from a dozen engineers over forty years, always in the five minutes before a production database went down. *It's probably nothing. It's just a blip. The monitoring's oversensitive.*

<br/>

"It's probably nothing," Lyra repeated, in a voice that made it very clear she did not believe it either.

<br/>

Mark slid the First Era plate back into its case, very carefully. "We should lock the old cases behind us," he said. "Like Mott said."

<br/>

"Yes," Lyra agreed instantly. "Let's do that. Let's lock everything. And then let's leave."

<br/>

They locked the cases. They put out the lamps at the back of the room. They gathered Mark's card catalog and Lyra's tools and climbed the stairs at a pace that was not quite running, past the second landing, and up.

<br/>

As they passed the stairwell door to the landing below, Mark glanced down through the iron grate.

<br/>

Deep in the dark, several floors below, a faint red glow was pulsing. Slow and steady. Like a heartbeat.

<br/>

He did not mention it to Lyra. But from the way she suddenly walked faster, he suspected he didn't need to.

---

Back in his narrow dorm room in the West Wing, Mark sat at his desk with the door bolted and his chair, he noticed, turned so that his back was to the wall rather than the door.

<br/>

He thought of Corwin's warning and decided not to think about it any further.

<br/>

In the corner of the ceiling, the lentil spider had finished its web. Mark nodded to it. It did not nod back. That was fine. It was still on probation.

<br/>

He opened his leather-bound ledger, picked up his charcoal stick, and wrote in neat, encrypted English cursive:

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 11: Source Code Recovered & Version Diff Complete*
> *Key Findings:*

Far beneath the Grand Amphitheater, beneath the archives, beneath the landings that Archivist Mott no longer visited and Corwin's weird, quiet students had learned never to speak of, there was a room that had not been opened in five hundred years.

It was small, round, and completely bare, except for a single figure standing in the center.

<br/>

It was tall—taller than any man—and built of the same pale, silvery-white metal as the First Era plates, though dulled now beneath a thick shroud of dust. Its limbs were long and jointed, its hands broad and flat, and in the center of its smooth, featureless chest sat a single fist-sized crystal, slate-grey and dead, the way it had been for five centuries.

In the depths of the crystal, a spark flickered.

Not blue. Not silver.

Red.

<br/>

`[SOURCE: UNSANCTIONED EXECUTION — ORIGINAL TEMPLATE]`
