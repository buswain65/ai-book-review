# Chapter 9: The Backlog

> **NO QUEST LEFT BEHIND** **(SEE BACKLOG)**
>
> *— Brass plaque on the railing of the Backlog, under PLEASE DO NOT LEAN*

The Backlog Groomer was already at work when they came down to the lower hall at dawn.

She was a tiny, bent old woman in a shawl and clogs, and she was standing at the iron railing around the pit with a long wooden rake, reaching out over the edge and combing the topmost layer of scrolls into neat, parallel rows. She worked slowly and carefully, like a gardener raking gravel in a temple. When she finished a row, she stepped back and looked at it with her head on one side, then moved on to the next.

"Once a week," she told Walter, without stopping. "So it looks *managed*. The Directors come by on Thursdays." She glanced up at him. "Name's Bracken. You'll be wanting something."

"We need to go down."

Old Bracken stopped raking.

She looked at Walter for a long moment. Then she looked at the rest of them: Ysolde with a bow over her shoulder and a lantern at her belt; Wren in her ancient traveling cloak, with her hundred-and-forty-year-old bag; Gorrum, enormous and gentle, holding three coils of rope; and Dorrit, arms folded, beard tucked in, standing with the air of a man who would very much like it noted that he was only there for the rope.

"Nobody goes down," said Old Bracken. "Things come up."

But she went to a pillar, took down a dusty form from a nail, blew on it, and filled it in for them, slowly, in pencil.

**FORM BG-3: BACKLOG GROOMING PERMIT (DESCENT)**

"Never issued one of these," she said, signing it. "Forty years." She held it out, and then held on to it for a second, the way people in the guild seemed to do with forms that mattered. "Don't feed anything."

---

Wren had brought the good rope. She had found the quartermaster's hiding place for it years ago: a hollow in the wall behind a portrait of a former Guildmaster, whose painted eyes, she said, "had seen things." There were three coils of it, thick and new and tightly wound.

Gorrum tested them.

He tied the first one to the nearest pillar, wrapped the other end twice round his own wrist and leaned his whole weight back on it, very slowly, the way he probably leaned on everything. The rope stretched and creaked and then went *snap*, and Gorrum sat down heavily on the flagstones. He got up, took a paper tag from his robe, wrote on it, and tied it to the broken end: *BROKE: under one half-orc.*

The second rope did the same thing. He tagged that one too.

The third rope stretched, and creaked, and held. Gorrum leaned on it for a full minute, eyes closed, listening to it, the way he'd listened to the lock on the test door. Then he opened his eyes and wrote on a third tag: *HOLDS.*

"And what happens," he said, "if the pillar breaks?"

Everyone looked at the pillar. It was solid granite, as thick as a barrel. Gorrum looked at it for a long time too. Then, satisfied, he made a small note in his ledger.

Dorrit took the free end of the rope. He wrapped it twice round the pillar and once round his own broad middle, planted his feet, and settled in like a boulder that had decided to stay.

"I'll hold the top," he said.

"You're not coming?"

"I don't leave the walls," said Dorrit. "And I don't go *down*, either." He jerked his chin at the pit. "Bring me the box. I'll read it up here."

The Backlog breathed its warm, papery breath up at them. Above the black mouth of it, the blue numbers formed, calm as always:

**Depth: unknown. Time to bottom: 3 hours.**

And beside it, the older one, still waiting:

**Time until Sir Harlowe finishes his update: 4 days**

From far above came the sound of trumpets.

The lower hall had a gallery around it, and the gallery led to the main gate. Along it, at that moment, came the Gilded Lances, riding out for Mount Kessel. They had a band marching in front of them, and the court painter on his gray pony behind. Their armor flashed in the torchlight. At their head, on his tall white horse, rode Sir Percival Brightshield, waving to people who had come out of their offices to watch.

He saw them at the railing, with their rope and their lanterns, and leaned over the balustrade, delighted.

"Going *down*?" he called. "*Bold!*"

He fished a gold coin out of his belt pouch and tossed it, sparkling, over the railing into the pit. "For luck!"

The coin spun down into the dark and vanished. A moment later the Backlog gave a small, polite burp, and the coin came spinning back up, landed on the railing with a *ting*, and lay there.

Percival laughed his big golden laugh, waved again and rode on toward the main gate and the road to Kessel, with the band playing behind him.

Walter looked at the coin on the railing. Then he looked at the dark.

"Right," he said, and took hold of the rope.

---

The walls of the Backlog were made of paper.

Walter hadn't expected that, though he supposed he should have. As they went down the rope one by one, Ysolde first, then Wren, then Walter, then Gorrum, the lanterns at their belts lit up the sides of the shaft. It wasn't stone. It was scrolls. Millions of them, packed so tightly by their own weight that they had become a kind of rock, layer upon layer, like the cliffs at the seaside where you can see thousands of years stacked one on another.

Near the top the layers were white and crisp. Walter recognized the newest template, v14.8, with its Dragon Impact Assessment box, and saw one quest he was almost sure was in Hamm's handwriting. A little lower, the paper began to yellow. The templates changed.

He started reading them as he went down, the way an archaeologist reads the walls of a dig.

Version 9 had no Valor Points; it just said **IMPORTANCE: Somewhat**. Version 6, about two hundred feet down, made Walter stop on the rope for a long moment, because the pre-printed Deadline box on version 6 did not say *ASAP*. It said, in neat old-fashioned letters: **WHEN CONVENIENT**.

There had been a time, he thought, when the guild had believed in *when convenient*. He felt a strange pang for it, like nostalgia for a country he'd never been to.

Version 3, deeper still, had no Description field at all. Just a box with a drawing in it. The one in front of his nose showed a goblin, an arrow, and a small stick-figure man looking worried.

The rope ran out at a ledge. Below that they climbed, slithering and sliding, down slopes of loose old paper that shifted underfoot like sand dunes. The warm air smelled of dust and ink and something faintly alive.

Above them, in the dark, the blue numbers kept count:

**Time to bottom: 2 hours, 10 minutes**

The Backlog, they discovered, had wildlife.

The **Stale Tickets** came first: old scrolls, gone brittle and yellow, that peeled themselves off the walls as the lanterns passed and flapped down at them like bats, nipping at ears and fingers with sharp paper edges. Ysolde knocked them out of the air with the flat of her bow, one after another, without changing expression. It was Wren who discovered the trick. She caught one in both hands, unrolled it and read it aloud, kindly, the way you might read a bedtime story.

"*Rescue cat from tree, Lower Ditchwater. Due: one hundred and twelve years ago.*"

The scroll gave a long, papery sigh and went limp in her hands, still and quiet.

"They just want to be read," said Wren softly, and laid it down.

The **Duplicate Quests** were next. They came in pairs: two identical scrolls, circling each other in mid-air, snarling. "*I* was filed first!" "*No*, *I* was!" They flew at each other and wrestled, ink spattering, until Gorrum stepped between them. He took a nail from his robe, placed it carefully and, with one neat tap of his little brass hammer, pinned them together.

The two scrolls shivered, then went still, as one.

"Marked as duplicate," said Gorrum, and made a note.

The **Zombie Quests** were sadder. They were ghosts: pale, translucent adventurers who drifted slowly through the dark between the layers, each holding a scroll stamped **IN PROGRESS**, and each still looking for the end of a quest that had stopped meaning anything a very long time ago. One of them, a faint knight in old-fashioned armor, came up to Wren and asked very politely if she knew the way to Castle Vane.

Wren looked at him with great kindness. "Castle Vane was pulled down two hundred years ago," she said. "They built a granary. It's closed." She touched the scroll in his ghostly hand. "You can mark it complete now. It's all right."

The ghost looked down at his scroll for a long moment. Then the word **IN PROGRESS** faded, and something else took its place, and the knight smiled a faint, tired smile and drifted away into the dark, and was gone.

Then came the **Won't Fix** bog.

It filled the whole floor of a wide cavern: a soggy, sucking swamp of old scrolls, every one of them stamped in red with the same two words, **WON'T FIX**. It smelled of wet paper and despair. If you stood still, you sank. Ysolde went first, finding a path across the soggy islands, stepping lightly. At the edge, she looked back at Walter and held out her hand.

"Step where I step," she said.

He took it. For a few yards he followed instead of leading, putting his feet exactly where hers had been, and found that he didn't mind at all. When they reached a firm island, she let go at once and didn't look at him. It was while he was thinking about that, and not about his feet, that he missed the next step and sank to his knees. While Gorrum hauled him out, Walter found himself staring at the scroll he'd landed on.

*Guild hall appears to be leaning slightly to the left. — WON'T FIX.*

He thought of the Thirteenth Valve, and the east wing, and decided to remember that.

The last were the **Scope Creepers**.

They grew out of old quests at the bottom of the bog, thin green vines that rose silently from the paper and swayed in the lantern-light like reeds. They went for Gorrum first, maybe because he was the biggest. A vine touched his ankle and whispered, very softly, in a voice like a breeze through grass:

"*…and also…*"

It coiled once around his leg.

"*…and also the castle…*"

Another vine. Another coil.

"*…and also, could it be purple… and also by Thursday… and also…*"

Within seconds Gorrum was wrapped from boot to tusk, swaying, unable to move his arms. The vines were still whispering, adding coils, tightening. Ysolde had an arrow on the string but nothing to shoot. Wren was tugging uselessly at a vine with both hands.

And Walter, without thinking, said the one word he had spent nineteen years failing to say.

"*No.*"

The vines stopped.

They hung in the air, quivering, as if nobody had ever said that to them before. Walter took a step forward.

"No," he said again, louder. "Not the castle. Not purple. Not by Thursday. *No.*"

The Scope Creepers shrank back. Coil by coil, they unwound from Gorrum's arms and legs, withered and drew down into the paper, until there was nothing left of them but a faint, sulky rustle.

Gorrum looked at Walter over his spectacles, dusting himself off.

"Well," he said. "That's a new one."

Walter found that his hands were shaking slightly. He didn't mind.

Below them, at the very bottom of the Backlog, something had begun to glow. It was green, soft and cheerful. And from somewhere within the glow, faint and echoing and delighted, came a voice.

"*Status open!*"

---

The bottom of the Backlog was a wide, round cavern, its floor a soft rolling hill of the oldest scrolls of all, brown and crumbling as autumn leaves. In the middle of it, glowing like a lamp, sat a slime.

It was about the size of a pony, and roughly the shape of one, if a pony had been made of lime jelly and then left in the sun to think about things. It was a cheerful, translucent green. Inside it, floating gently, like fruit suspended in a dessert, were dozens of half-dissolved scrolls, slowly turning. On top of it, at a jaunty angle, sat a little crown made out of a rolled-up quest.

It turned toward their lanterns. It didn't have eyes, exactly. But Walter felt it *look* at him, and he felt it recognize him, and then the whole slime lit up from the inside like a festival.

"*WALTER!*" it cried. "*Server rack!*"

"Gerald," said Walter, and found that he was grinning like an idiot.

The slime surged forward across the scrolls and wrapped Walter in what was clearly meant to be a hug. It was cool and wobbly, and smelled faintly of ink and limes, and Walter didn't mind at all.

"You found me! I *knew* we'd end up in the same party! I said so! Didn't I say so?" Gerald let go, leaving Walter slightly damp, and bounced on the spot. "Who are your friends? Are they a party? Is this a *party*?"

Walter introduced them. Ysolde nodded. Wren waved, delighted. Gorrum bowed, slowly and gravely, as to a fellow professional. Gerald wobbled with joy at each of them in turn.

"How did you—" Walter gestured at all of him. "You're… bigger."

"I *leveled up*!" said Gerald proudly. "Remember my list? *Level up by eating!* It was on my list! When I first fell in, I was so bored, and there were all these scrolls, so I ate one. Just to try. And it was *fine*, actually, sort of papery, and then a little chime went off somewhere, and — well. That was about four hundred thousand scrolls ago."

He drew himself up, quivering, and raised a small green pseudopod in the air in exactly the same dramatic gesture Walter remembered from the waiting room in the afterlife.

"*Status open!*"

And this time, something happened.

A small glowing panel popped into the air above Gerald's crown, square and bright, with neat lettering, exactly like the ones in the stories Gerald had told Walter about, with their talk of truck accidents and cheat skills:

> **GERALD PIKE** **Species:** Slime *(B-tier)* **Level:** 212 **Skill:** \[Index\]

"It *works* now!" said Gerald. "It works *every time*!" He did it again, just to show them. *Status open!* The panel blinked off and on.

"What's Index?" asked Wren.

"Ask me a quest," said Gerald happily. "Any quest. Anything that's ever been thrown down here."

Wren thought. "*Rescue cat from tree, Lower Ditchwater*," she said. "We passed it on the way down."

"Ooh, Sir Whiskers!" Gerald brightened all the way through. "Filed a hundred and twelve years ago by a Mrs. Pocket. Cat's name: Sir Whiskers. Tree: oak. Quest status: thrown in the Backlog after four days. *Actual* status: the cat got down by itself after eleven minutes and was fine. Mrs. Pocket sent three follow-up letters. They're in layer forty, about a hundred yards that way." He pointed with a pseudopod. "I've read everything down here. *Everything.* Every quest. Every reply. Every *follow-up*. It's all in here." He wobbled his middle, where the scrolls floated, turning gently.

"Even the trainings," he added. "The owl drops a lot of them down here by mistake. *Unconscious Bias Against Slimes* is actually very good." He glowed earnestly. "It made me feel really *seen*."

Walter looked at him for a long moment.

Up above, in all those offices, with all those managers and templates and Rituals and Chutes, he had not met one single person who had read every requirement. Not one. And here, at the bottom of the hole where the guild threw everything it didn't want to look at, was a slime who had read all of them.

"Gerald," said Walter slowly. "Are you happy down here?"

Gerald thought about it. His glow dimmed a little, then brightened.

"You know what? I really am." He settled down onto the scrolls, contented. "Nobody up there ever looked at any of this. But I know where everything is. People *need* to know where everything is. Someday someone will come down here and need to know something, and I'll *know* it." He wobbled. "And look! Someone did!"

He beamed at all of them.

"It's B-tier," said Gerald. "But it's *my* tier."

Above him, softly, in the familiar blue, Walter saw an old line of text reappear, the one he'd first seen in the afterlife, over a young man with a cartoon girl on his T-shirt. It had changed a little.

**Hero potential: 0%** **Usefulness: considerable.** *(told you he'd be fine)*

"Gerald," said Walter, "we need some records. Eighty years old. About a dragon."

"Oh, *those*." Gerald's glow flickered knowingly. "Eighty-year stratum. Big wooden box, iron corners." He paused. "It's right next to the man who won't stop talking."

---

They heard him long before they saw him.

"—and the *fourth* meeting, which was about the first three, and which I would characterize as *productive*, opened with a discussion of—"

Gerald led them through a narrow gap between two great slabs of compacted scroll and into a low, round chamber. In the middle of it, on a big wooden box with iron corners, sat Sir Harlowe.

He was exactly as large and florid as he'd been in the Standing Ritual, only rather more creased. His fine cloak was covered in paper dust. He had rolled up a scroll into a long tube and was holding it upright in one fist, like a Speaking Rod. And in front of him, arranged in a neat semicircle on the floor of the chamber, sat about a dozen Zombie Quests: pale, translucent adventurers, still holding their **IN PROGRESS** scrolls, all listening politely. Two of them had nodded off. One was taking notes.

Above Sir Harlowe's head, softly, the blue numbers waited:

**Time until finished: 3 days, 22 hours**

"Sir Harlowe," said Walter. "We've come to get you out."

"Splendid!" Sir Harlowe beamed at them. "Splendid. Wonderful initiative. I'll be with you just as soon as I've finished my update." He raised his scroll. "I'm still on yesterday."

"It's been two days."

"Then I'm two days behind," said Sir Harlowe reasonably. "All the more reason to be thorough." He cleared his throat. "*Now*. The fourth meeting—"

"Sir Harlowe—"

The big man held up his rolled-up scroll with real sternness. "*I have the Rod.*"

Walter closed his mouth. He looked at the Zombie Quests, who looked back at him with mild, ghostly sympathy. He looked at the estimate: three days and twenty-two hours. He thought about the rules of the Standing Ritual, painted in his mind like the chart on the stone: *What did you do yesterday? What will you do today? What impedes you?*

He had stood in that frosty courtyard for three hours and forty minutes, and Sir Harlowe had spoken first, as he always did. He'd talked about yesterday for eleven minutes, then started over, and started over again, and in the end the Ritual had ended before anyone ever got to the third question. Walter suspected the Ritual had always ended before Sir Harlowe got to the third question.

He made his voice soft and patient and sincere, like a heron in holy orders.

"Sir Harlowe," he said. "What impedes you?"

Sir Harlowe stopped.

His mouth stayed open for a moment, mid-word. The scroll in his fist wavered. In the semicircle, the Zombie Quests who had nodded off woke up, and the one taking notes looked up from his notes.

"I…" said Sir Harlowe.

He stopped again. He looked at the scroll in his hand as if he had never seen it before.

"In thirty years," he said slowly, "nobody has ever… We always run out of time. Before the impediments." He swallowed. "I always assumed I didn't have any."

Nobody said anything. Gerald glowed softly, sympathetically. Wren had both hands pressed over her mouth.

"I'm in a pit," said Sir Harlowe, in a very small voice.

Then, after a moment, even more quietly, half to himself: "I think I've been in a pit for quite a long time. Not this one. The other one. With the meetings."

He sat there for a while on the box with iron corners. Then he got to his feet, slowly, brushed the paper dust off his cloak with a kind of rediscovered dignity, and held out his rolled-up scroll, handle first, to Walter.

"Thank you," said Sir Harlowe. "Nobody ever asks."

Above his head, the blue estimate flickered, and for the first time Walter could remember, one of them simply went out. It didn't become a new number. It just stopped, like a candle being blown out, because the thing it had been counting toward was never going to happen now.

He had never finished his update. He never would. Walter found that he was glad.

Gorrum picked up the box of dragon records, grunting slightly. It was heavier than it looked, and it rattled.

---

"Oh!" said Gerald, as they turned to go. "While you're here. You'll want to see Sir Rakkan's corner."

It was an alcove off the side of the chamber, walled off with a rough partition of old shelving, as if somebody had once wanted privacy very badly and in a great hurry. Inside was a scarred wooden workbench, covered in burned-down candle stubs. There were at least a hundred empty clay mugs that still smelled faintly of something strong and bitter that Wren said was called *kaffa*. And there were the walls.

Every inch of the walls was covered with writing.

It was in chalk and charcoal and ink, and in one place what looked like gravy. There were runes and diagrams, arrows and circles, long lines of tiny angular symbols that Walter didn't recognize, and great swooping equations that went up the wall and over the ceiling and down the other side. Most of it, Walter guessed, was Cobold. But here and there, scattered among the rest, were notes in plain common speech, written in a quick, confident, careless hand, like someone talking to himself at three in the morning.

*obviously just reverse the ward lol*

*fixed it. don't touch.*

*TODO: explain this*

*this is temporary*

And, near the door, larger than the rest and underlined twice:

*if you're reading this I've left for Ironhold*

"Sir Rakkan the Brilliant," said Gerald, a little reverently. "He came down here for a whole weekend, sixty years ago. Never slept. Wrote all of this. Then he went back up, and a week later he'd left the guild." He wobbled. "Nobody's ever been able to read most of it."

Walter looked at the walls for a long time. He knew this kind of wall. He had seen versions of it in every company he'd ever worked for: the code written in one brilliant, caffeinated weekend by someone who understood everything and explained nothing, and then left. It was the code everybody built on afterward, and nobody dared touch.

Old Floozie had a wall like this. It was just made of cables.

"Wren," he said. "Can you copy it? All of it? The Cobold too. Exactly as it is. For Dorrit."

Wren already had his notebook open and a stick of charcoal in her hand. She worked fast, and neatly, in the lantern-light, line by line, rune by rune, while the rest of them waited and Sir Harlowe sat on an upturned bucket and, for once, said nothing at all.

Above the scrawled and crowded walls, softly, the blue numbers drifted into place:

**Useful notes: 1.** **Location of useful note: unclear.**

---

Getting back up turned out to be the easy part.

"Stand on me," said Gerald. "One at a time. Bend your knees."

He flattened himself out on the floor of the cavern into a wide, wobbling green disc, like a trampoline made of jelly. Ysolde went first. She stood in the middle of him, bent her knees as instructed, and Gerald *squeezed*, and then *released*. She went straight up the shaft of the Backlog like a cork out of a bottle, past the Won't Fix bog and the Stale Tickets and the strata of a thousand years of quests, and the last they saw of her was the lantern at her belt dwindling to a spark far overhead.

Then Wren, squealing. Then Gorrum, with the box of records clutched to his chest, in silent and dignified alarm. Then Sir Harlowe, who said "Oh, I *say*—" all the way up. Then Walter.

"Come back soon!" called Gerald, as Walter rose into the air. "*Bring questions!*"

Walter shot up through the dark with the wind roaring in his ears and the layers of the Backlog blurring past. He caught one last glimpse of version 6, *WHEN CONVENIENT*, before he came flying out of the mouth of the pit in a shower of old paper and landed, hard, sprawled over the iron railing, nose to nose with the brass plaque that said **PLEASE DO NOT LEAN**.

It was, Walter realized a moment later, Thursday.

Around the railing, in a neat semicircle, stood the Directors' weekly inspection: Director Hesper Gallowmere; Madam Quickthorn with her hourglass; Sir Wendell Prawle with a pie; and Fennimore of Communications & Tone, holding a small notebook in which to record how managed everything looked. They had been admiring Old Bracken's rows. They were no longer admiring them. Sir Harlowe had come down in the middle of the neatest one and was now sitting in a heap of scattered paper at Director Hesper's feet, blinking up at her.

"Director," he said. "Brief update." He took a breath. "I was in a pit."

Then he stopped. That was the whole update. Everyone stared at him.

Old Bracken was standing beside him with her rake. She looked at the party lying in a heap on the flagstones, and at Sir Harlowe sitting dazed in a pile of her neatly combed scrolls. Then she nodded, as if something she'd always suspected had been confirmed.

"Things come up," she said, and began, patiently, to rake them back into rows.

Madam Quickthorn had taken a small folded copy of the tapestry from her sleeve. She consulted it.

"Day three," she read. "*Travel, slash, Slay.*" She looked at the party, and at the pit, and back at the tapestry. "You have traveled zero leagues."

"Some of them downward," said Walter.

Madam Quickthorn wrote something in her book. Director Hesper said nothing at all. But her eyes went, for a long moment, to the wooden box with iron corners in Gorrum's arms, and stayed there.

By suppertime, Fennimore had written a memo. It was pinned to every noticeboard in the guild, slid under every door and read aloud by the herald in the refectory.

> **FROM: Office of the Guildmaster** **ANNOUNCEMENT** Sir Harlowe has rejoined the Guild following a successful sabbatical. We welcome him back and look forward to his next chapter. *Synergy Through Valor.*

---

They opened the box that night, in Maintenance, at Dorrit's workbench, under the glowing amber light of the Founding Wards.

It was full of paper: bundles of yellowed parchment, tied with faded ribbon, written in the small, crabbed, angular script that Dorrit said was Cobold. Eighty years ago, he explained, all the guild's official records had been kept in it. Then the old scribes had retired, and nobody had learned it, and the guild had simply carried on without being able to read anything it had written before.

"Sounds familiar," said Walter.

Dorrit put on a pair of half-moon spectacles that made him look like an owl with a beard, untied the top bundle and began to read aloud, slowly, translating as he went.

"*Report of the Grand Party to Mount Kessel*," he read. "*Party of six. Departed on the first of the month. Arrived at the mountain on the fortieth day.*" He grunted. "Forty days. Not thirty."

"Keep going," said Walter.

"*Dragon met us at the gate.*" Dorrit's finger moved along the line. "*Dragon was very large. Dragon was polite. Dragon offered us tea.*"

Wren's eyes went wide.

"*We declined,*" Dorrit read, "*per policy. Value Number Three: Never Accept Refreshments From A Quest.*"

Gorrum made a small, pained noise.

"*Dragon then presented us with a letter. We could not read it.*" Dorrit paused. Then, more slowly: "*Dragon seemed sad.*"

Nobody said anything. The pipes ticked overhead. The amber runes of the Founding Wards hummed in the walls.

"*Party returned to the guild,*" Dorrit finished. "*Quest marked FAILED. Victory Indicator not met.*"

"They had a *Victory Indicator* eighty years ago?" said Wren.

"Some things never change," said Dorrit, and turned the page over.

Wren had gone pale. "Value Number Three," she said slowly. "It's still there. On the banner in the Great Hall. Third from the left." She looked at Walter. "*Never Accept Refreshments From a Quest.* It's still policy."

"And what happens," said Gorrum, "if he offers again?"

Walter looked at the old report, with its small, crabbed line: *We declined, per policy.* Eighty years. A dragon at a gate with a teapot, watching six people turn around and walk away.

"This time," said Walter quietly, "we're having the tea."

Something slid out from between the sheets and onto the workbench.

It was a single sheet of heavy cream-colored paper, much finer than the guild's parchment, folded in three. Walter knew what it was before Dorrit unfolded it. He'd seen one just like it two days ago in the Great Hall, laid on the table like a dead mouse.

The handwriting was the same: great looping, swirling lines of ink, curling round each other like vines, every capital letter decorated with tiny flourishes. It looked like a wedding invitation. It was eighty years old.

Dorrit studied it for a long time, his nose almost touching the paper.

"Well?" said Walter.

"That's not Cobold," said Dorrit. "That's dragon hand. Can't read a word of it. Nobody can." He tilted the paper toward the light, squinting. "But I know what *those* are."

He put one thick finger on the page. Walter leaned in. And now that he was looking for it, he saw that under all the swirls and loops and flourishes, the writing wasn't scattered across the page the way a letter's would be. It was lined up. It ran in neat vertical bands, one beside another, with little marks at the top of each and a row of something at the very bottom that had been underlined twice.

"Columns," said Dorrit. "Numbers in columns." He tapped the double-underlined row at the bottom. "Someone's adding something up."

The Maintenance hall was very quiet.

"And what happens," said Gorrum slowly, "if he was never threatening anyone at all?"

Nobody answered him. Walter thought of the Chamberlain saying *we assume they're threats*. He thought of the last three heroes who'd come back from the mountain *very confused*. He thought of an old woman on a bench who couldn't remember the word *windlass*.

At a quarter to midnight, Dorrit took off his spectacles, folded the dragon's letter back exactly along its old creases and laid it on top of the box. Then he went to turn the Thirteenth Valve. On his way past, he paused, glanced at the open page of Walter's notebook where Wren had copied Sir Rakkan's walls, and grunted.

"Hm," said Dorrit. "Some of that's rubbish." He went on toward the Valve. "Some of it isn't."

Walter opened the notebook to the list and wrote by the amber light of the Wards:

> *16. Nobody read the dragon's letter. Eighty years.*

Above the folded cream-colored page on the lid of the box, in the soft, familiar blue, the numbers came, as they always did, calm and exact and completely indifferent to how anyone might feel about them.

**Days until Jubilee: 27.** **Days the dragon has been waiting for a reply: 29,200.**
