# Chapter 5: The Standing Ritual

> **INDIVIDUALS AND INTERACTIONS OVER…** **(REMAINDER OF BANNER PENDING APPROVAL)**
>
> *— Banner over the Ritual Ground*

Walter woke before dawn, and the first thing he saw, hanging in the dark above his bunk like the glowing hands of a bedside clock, was the number.

**1 day, 6 hours (too late)**

It had followed him all night. Whenever he rolled over, it was there in the corner of his vision, patient, getting smaller. Somewhere on the road south, three farm boys, a one-eyed veteran and a bard were walking toward a well with swords. Every hour they walked, the number got a little shorter.

Walter had a plan. It was, for once, the right kind of plan: a proper one, through a proper channel.

"The Standing Ritual," Wren had told him the night before, handing him a second honey cake he hadn't asked for. "Every morning. Everybody's there. It's where you say what's blocking you." She'd said it with the conviction of someone describing the sunrise. "That's what it's *for*."

So Walter had written his speech in the notebook by candlelight. It was short and clear. It said what the quest was supposed to be, what it had become, what the client had said, and how long they had. He had read it over four times. He'd timed it in his head: under a minute. He was going to stand up, in front of everyone who mattered, and say it. Then someone with authority would do something about it.

He had been in software for nineteen years, and part of him knew exactly how this was going to go. He put that part in a box and sat on the lid.

---

The Ritual Ground was a courtyard behind the main hall, open to the sky, paved with sharp gray gravel. In the middle stood a ring of thirteen standing stones — real ones, tall and gray and lichened, much older than the guild. They had been there, Wren said, since before anybody could remember. The guild had built itself around them, and then, seeing a circle of stones nobody was using, had found a use for it.

It was cold. Frost lay on the gravel. About forty people stood inside the circle in their cloaks, breathing out little clouds, stamping their feet. Walter noticed that there were no benches anywhere — not in the courtyard, not along the walls, not anywhere in sight. He also noticed that the older adventurers wore boots with remarkably thick soles, and that several of them had small cushions strapped to their lower backs under their cloaks.

In the center of the ring, on a stone plinth, sat a large hourglass.

Beside it stood Brother Tobiah.

He was very tall and very thin, with a shaved head and a long, mild, serious face, like a heron that had taken holy orders. His robes were the gray of the stones, and they were embroidered all over, very finely, with tiny silver hourglasses. In one hand he held a staff of polished oak, about as tall as he was, with a small brass bell hanging from its top. This, Walter would learn, was the Speaking Rod. Whoever held it could speak. Nobody else could.

Tobiah didn't look like a fool, or a tyrant, or any of the things Walter had braced himself for. He looked like something much more dangerous: a sincere man. He looked at the circle of shivering adventurers with real tenderness, like a shepherd looking at sheep he intends to save.

"Friends," he said. His voice was soft, but it carried. "We gather to align."

"*We are aligned*," said forty people in a flat, practiced murmur.

Tobiah reached out and turned over the hourglass. The sand began to fall.

"The Ritual is sacred," he said gently, "because the Ritual is *short*. Fifteen minutes. No more. We stand, so that no one grows comfortable. We speak only with the Rod, so that no one speaks out of turn. Each of us answers the Three Sacred Questions, and nothing more. What did you do yesterday? What will you do today? What impedes you?" He paused. "And remember. Whosoever sits—"

"*—the Ritual begins anew*," murmured the circle.

Walter looked at the hourglass. The sand was falling steadily. It was, by any measure, a fifteen-minute hourglass.

Above it, soft and blue and utterly calm, the estimate appeared:

**2 hours, 4 minutes**

Walter closed his eyes briefly. Then he opened them, took his speech out of his pocket, and held it ready.

---

The Rod went round the circle clockwise. Walter was standing, he calculated, thirty-one places from the start.

The first person to speak was a Senior Party Lead named Sir Harlowe, a big, florid man with a voice built for addressing cavalry. He took the Rod and answered the first Sacred Question, *What did you do yesterday?*, for eleven minutes. He had attended four meetings. He described each of them in detail, including who had said what, and which points had been "really well made," and a fifth meeting he had attended about the first four. At one point the little brass bell on the Rod rang, as it was designed to do when someone talked too long. Sir Harlowe kept talking. Tobiah glanced at him with an expression of gentle reproach and did nothing at all. Walter would come to understand this later: the bell applied to everyone, and especially to people below the rank of Senior.

The hourglass ran out while Sir Harlowe was still on yesterday.

Tobiah turned it over without comment. "A second fifteen minutes," he said, "which is also sacred."

The Rod moved on, slowly. A ranger reported that she had ranged. A cleric reported that he had cleaned. Somebody's impediment was "the impediment from yesterday," and Tobiah nodded as though this made perfect sense.

Then the Rod came to Wren.

She took it in both hands, beaming, and spoke in a bright, clear voice. "Yesterday, I helped. Today, I'll help. Nothing impedes me." She passed the Rod on.

Walter noticed that the grizzled dwarf standing next to her had been silently moving his lips along with every word. He looked at Walter and shrugged.

"Sixty years," the dwarf murmured. "Same every morning. I could do it in my sleep. Sometimes I do."

The Rod moved on. Six places later, it came to a tall woman with a long dark braid, standing slightly outside the circle of cushions and thick soles, as though she'd come to the Ritual by accident and was waiting for it to end. Walter recognized her at once. It was the ranger from the corridor. Ysolde.

She took the Rod.

"Did it," she said. "Doing it. Nothing."

She passed it on. The whole thing had taken under three seconds.

There was a stunned silence. The sand in the hourglass seemed to hesitate. Brother Tobiah closed his eyes as if in mild physical pain.

"Sister Ysolde," he said softly. "The Sacred Questions are not merely *answered*. They are *honored*." He drew a small slip of paper from his sleeve, wrote on it, and held it out to her. Walter could just read the heading: **CONDUCT NOTE: Insufficient Ceremony.**

Ysolde took the slip without any expression whatsoever. She folded it neatly into a small square, opened a pouch on her belt, and added it to what Walter could see was an extremely thick stack of identical small squares. Then she closed the pouch and went back to looking politely at nothing.

Walter found, to his own surprise, that he liked her enormously.

The Rod had made it about halfway around the circle, and the second hourglass was nearly empty, when Sir Godric fainted.

Sir Godric was a knight in full plate armor. He'd been standing perfectly still on the frosty gravel for seventy minutes in roughly ninety pounds of steel. Walter saw him begin to sway, very slightly, like a tall tree in a breeze. Then, slowly and with great dignity, Sir Godric toppled backward. He hit the gravel with a crash like a cart full of saucepans going over.

He lay on his back, blinking up at the sky. "I'm fine," he called. "I'm fine!"

Brother Tobiah looked down at him for a long moment with infinite sorrow. Then he looked up at the circle.

"Sir Godric," he said, "is no longer standing."

A groan went around the stones. It was the groan of forty people who had been on their feet for seventy minutes and knew exactly what was coming.

"The Ritual," said Tobiah gently, "begins anew."

He turned the hourglass over. The Rod went back to the start. Sir Harlowe took it, cleared his throat, and began, again, to describe yesterday's meetings. Walter watched, in the corner of his vision, as the number above the hourglass quietly corrected itself.

**3 hours, 12 minutes**

It was during the second telling of Sir Harlowe's fourth meeting that a handbell rang in the archway at the edge of the courtyard.

The gray-robed runner of the Template Governance Council strode in among the standing stones, satchel bouncing. "Hear ye!" he cried. "By order of the Council, Quest Template version fourteen point seven is hereby *retired*! Version fourteen point eight is now *mandatory*!"

He handed Tobiah a scroll and left. Tobiah read it carefully, nodding.

"Friends," he said, with the patience of a man who has done this many times, "every quest we have discussed this morning was discussed under version fourteen point seven. Those discussions are no longer valid."

The groan this time was quieter. People were saving their strength.

"The Ritual begins anew."

Walter looked at the hourglass, now on its fourth turn, and at the blue numbers above it:

**3 hours, 40 minutes**

And, in the corner of his eye, the other number, the one that didn't care about any of this:

**1 day, 5 hours (too late)**

---

On the fourth pass, by some miracle, the Rod made it all the way around the circle. Then came the Estimation.

Brother Tobiah produced, from somewhere inside his robes, an enormous deck of tarot cards. It was very old, with gilded edges worn down to brown, and he handled it the way other men handle relics. He walked slowly around the circle, and every person drew one card and held it face-down against their chest.

"The first quest," said Tobiah, unrolling a scroll. "*Clear the goblins from the old mill at Hessock.* Reveal."

Forty people turned their cards over at once.

Walter looked around the circle. The cards had numbers on them, and the numbers, he noticed, went 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, with nothing in between. They were also, according to Wren, who was standing on his left and helpfully whispering, *worth* things. There was a little chart for it painted on the nearest standing stone. An Ace was 1. The Tower was 13. The Fool meant *assign this quest to the intern*. Wren, who was holding The Fool, looked pleased. Death meant *re-scope*. The Lovers meant *pair adventuring*. The Hanged Man meant *blocked, pending Legal*.

For the goblins at the mill, the circle held up a 3, two 5s, an 8, a Lovers, a Hanged Man and The Tower.

"Brother Dunstan," said Tobiah to the dwarf with The Tower. "Share your reasoning."

"Goblins," said the dwarf.

Tobiah nodded thoughtfully. "We will draw again."

They drew again. And again. Walter began to see how it worked. Each time, Tobiah's eyes went, very briefly, to the card held by Sir Harlowe, the most senior person in the circle. Each time Sir Harlowe's card didn't match the majority, they drew again. On the fifth draw, Sir Harlowe held up a 5, and so, by now, did most of the circle, because they had also noticed how it worked.

"Five," said Tobiah, with deep satisfaction. "The cards have spoken."

Above the scroll, the blue estimate had been sitting there quietly the whole time.

**9 days**

Walter looked at it. Then he looked at the card in his hand. It was the Three of Cups. It showed three women dancing in a circle, which seemed more relevant than the card probably intended.

He didn't hold it up. He just said, quite clearly, into the cold air: "That one's nine days."

The circle went very quiet. Forty heads turned to look at him. Brother Tobiah turned last, slowly, as if he weren't sure he had heard correctly.

"Junior Scribe," he said, very gently. "We do not speak of *time*."

"Why not?"

"Time is the Old Way." Tobiah said it the way some people say *the Dark Ages*. "In the Old Way, the guild promised *days*. And the days were missed. And the guild was shamed before the King." He laid a hand on his deck. "Now we promise Valor. And Valor cannot be missed, because it is not a measure of time. It is a measure of…" He searched for the word. "*Valor*."

Walter thought about this. "How much is a Valor Point worth?"

Tobiah blinked. "About a day," he said.

"So the goblin quest is nine Valor Points."

"No," said Tobiah patiently. "It is five. The cards have spoken."

He wrote **5** at the top of the scroll and rolled it up. Above it, as Walter watched, the blue estimate adjusted itself, very slightly, to account for this new information.

**9 days (5 Valor) (still 9 days)**

The Estimation went on. Walter stopped speaking and just watched. Every quest was drawn for, and redrawn for, and redrawn for, until Sir Harlowe's card won. Tobiah wrote each number down with the quiet joy of a man filling in a crossword.

It was on the eleventh quest that Walter saw it: the way Tobiah's fingers drew a card from the deck, glanced at it, and wrote down a number, all in one smooth, practiced motion, without looking at the scroll at all. Walter had seen that motion before. He had seen it yesterday, described by a clerk, in a corridor between the third floor and the Quest Board. A monk in a hurry, drawing a card, writing an 8 in the Valor Points box of a quest about a well.

"Between Rituals," Wren whispered, when he asked, "Brother Tobiah estimates quests in the corridors. For efficiency."

Walter took out his notebook and, in the margin of his speech, wrote: *8 Valor. Drawn in a corridor. Unread.*

In the corner of his eye, the other number had changed again.

**1 day, 4 hours (too late)**

---

"And now," said Brother Tobiah, "we honor the Impediments."

There was a bronze bowl on a tripod beside the hourglass, full of glowing coals. Walter had assumed it was for warmth. Nobody had gone near it. Now Tobiah stood beside it and laid one hand on its rim, almost tenderly.

"The Brazier of Release," Wren whispered.

The Rod went round once more, for anyone with an impediment not yet honored. Most people passed it straight on. A cleric said that his impediment was "the meeting about impediments," and was gently told that this was not how it worked. Then the Rod came to Walter.

It was heavier than it looked. The little bell at the top swayed. Forty cold, tired faces turned toward him, and for a moment he was back in every all-hands meeting of his life, the one person who'd put his hand up during *Any questions?*

He unfolded his speech.

"Yesterday," he said, "I wrote a quest. Today I'm asking for help with it. Here's what impedes me." He took a breath. "The quest *Kill the Well Monster* is wrong. The client is Mother Tansy of Lower Mumbling. She told me directly that the thing in her well is not dangerous. It's shy. My write-up said that. It was changed in review, and then a template migration deleted the rest. A party of three new recruits, a veteran and a bard is on the road right now, armed, and they're about a day and four hours from that well. Somebody with authority needs to send a fast rider to stop them, and somebody needs to talk to the client. That's all."

He lowered the paper. Fifty-four seconds. He'd timed it.

There was a silence. It was a different kind of silence from the one in Quest Intake. In this one, a few people were nodding. Sir Godric, who had been helped back to his feet and was leaning slightly on a standing stone, said, "Huh." The dwarf who knew Wren's update by heart was looking at Walter with something like interest.

And Walter felt it: a small, treacherous flicker of hope. Maybe, he thought. Maybe this time.

Brother Tobiah came toward him across the gravel, smiling gently, and took the Rod from his hands.

"Thank you, Junior Scribe," he said warmly. "That was *beautifully* honored. Clear. Brief. Ceremonial." He meant it. Walter could tell he meant it. He drew a slip of paper from his sleeve, wrote on it with a small silver pencil, and held it up so the whole circle could see. *Well quest — incorrect — client says harmless — party en route.* It was a perfectly accurate summary.

"We acknowledge the impediment," said Tobiah.

And forty voices answered, in a low, practiced murmur:

"*We release it.*"

Tobiah dropped the slip into the Brazier of Release. It curled, blackened, flared yellow for one bright second, and was gone.

Walter stared at the coals.

"Wait," he said. "What happens now?"

"Now," said Tobiah kindly, "it no longer impedes you."

"The party is still walking toward the well."

"Yes." Tobiah nodded. "But you are no longer *impeded* by it."

Walter opened his mouth. He closed it again. He thought of the forty-seven values, all of them painted on banners throughout the hall. He reached for the one that had been designed for exactly this moment.

"Value #8," he said. "Escalate Everything. I want to escalate."

"Of course!" Tobiah beamed. If anything, he looked more pleased. "Escalations are taken to the Parking Lot."

"The Parking Lot," murmured several people in the circle, in the tone of churchgoers naming a saint.

"What's the Parking Lot?"

"It's where we put things," said Tobiah, "to discuss later." He turned to the circle, raised his arms, and spoke the closing words. "Friends. We have aligned."

"*We are aligned*," said the circle, with feeling. People were already walking away, limping a little, rubbing their lower backs. Behind Tobiah, the sand ran out of the hourglass for the last time. Walter didn't need to count. He knew the Ritual had lasted exactly three hours and forty minutes.

He stood in the emptying circle with his speech in his hand.

Someone was standing beside him. He hadn't noticed her come over. Ysolde was looking at the brazier, where the last of his impediment had become a thin curl of ash. She had not, he realized, joined in the chant. She was the only one who hadn't.

"Some things," she said, low, to no one in particular, "shouldn't be released."

Walter looked at her. "Thank you."

She shrugged, as if she hadn't said anything at all. Then, already turning to go: "Parking Lot's behind the stables. Watch the goats."

And she was gone through the archway, braid swinging, before he could ask what that meant.

In the corner of his eye:

**1 day, 3 hours (too late)**

---

The Parking Lot was behind the stables, exactly where Ysolde had said it would be.

It was a field. A wide, muddy field, fenced with split rails, with a wooden gate and a hand-painted sign: **PARKING LOT — ESCALATIONS — PLEASE CLOSE GATE**. Inside the fence, in long, neat rows stretching away toward the kitchen gardens, stood thousands upon thousands of wooden stakes. Nailed to each stake was a scroll. Some were fresh and white. Some were yellow. Some had been there so long they had faded to the color of the mud, and only the nail was still legible.

Among the stakes, unhurried and content, wandered a large herd of goats.

They were eating the scrolls.

Walter stood at the gate and watched a brown-and-white goat reach up, pull a crisp new escalation off its nail, and chew it thoughtfully, like a man reading the morning paper. Three rows over, a black goat finished one and moved on to the next.

An old man was leaning on the fence beside the gate. He had a shepherd's crook, a ledger under one arm and a battered felt hat with a brass badge pinned to the band. The badge said **HEAD OF ESCALATIONS**.

"New?" said the old man.

"I have an escalation," said Walter.

"Course you do." The old man opened the gate for him. His name, he said, was Abner. He'd been Head of Escalations for thirty-one years. "Grab a stake. Pen's on the post."

"What happens to them?"

Abner nodded at the herd. "They get eaten in order," he said. He thought about it. "Mostly."

"And then what?"

"Then they're resolved." Abner opened his ledger and showed Walter a column of neat figures. "Average time to resolution, four minutes. Best in the guild. Before the goats it was eleven years." He sounded genuinely proud.

Walter looked at the goats, at the stakes and at the ledger. Then, because he had nineteen years of experience and a very small, very stubborn flicker of hope that refused to go out, he wrote his escalation anyway. He kept it short. *Well quest wrong. Client says harmless. Party en route. Send a rider. URGENT.* He nailed it to an empty stake near the gate.

A large gray goat with one curling horn and a cold yellow eye trotted straight over, read it — Walter would have sworn she read it — and ate it.

It took about six seconds.

"Oh, that's the Senior Vice President," said Abner fondly. "She takes the urgent ones."

---

Walter walked back through the main hall slowly, the way you walk after a funeral. He was passing the Quest Board when he saw the clerk pinning up a fresh scroll. It was bright and new, with a gold seal. Brax Hollister stood nearby, shaking hands with someone and laughing. Walter stopped and read it.

> **TEMPLATE:** v14.8 **TITLE:** PREMIUM HEARTH & HOME — LOWER MUMBLING **DESCRIPTION:** Slay the well monster AND the chimney monster. Bonus for both heads. **VICTORY INDICATOR:** Monsters dead (×2). **VALOR POINTS:** 13 (The Tower) **ASSIGNED TO:** THE GILDED LANCES

It was Brax's upsell from yesterday, the one he'd sold Mother Tansy on the bench. It had gone in as a brand-new quest, with its own number, its own seal and its own party, as if the first one had never existed.

Through the great doors, out in the main courtyard, a crowd was gathering. Walter went to look.

The Gilded Lances were mounting up. There were four of them, and they looked exactly like the illustrations on the guild's recruiting posters, possibly because they *were* the illustrations on the guild's recruiting posters. Their armor gleamed. Their lances were gilded. Their horses were white. Behind them, on a gray pony, rode a court painter with an easel strapped to his back, already sketching.

At their head, on the largest and whitest horse, sat Sir Percival Brightshield.

He was tall and broad and golden, with hair that caught the light like it was being paid to. His armor didn't have a scratch on it. Neither did his face. He was waving to the crowd with the easy, generous warmth of a man who had never, in his whole life, been told no, and had never needed to be. As he wheeled his horse toward the gate, his eye fell on Walter, standing at the edge of the crowd with ink on his fingers. Percival flashed him a dazzling smile and a thumbs-up.

"Another monster!" he called cheerfully. "Busy week, eh, scribe?"

"Did you read the quest?" Walter called back.

Sir Percival looked down at him. For a moment he seemed genuinely puzzled, the way a man might be puzzled if you asked whether he'd read the sky.

"Read it?" he said. "Why would I read it? It says *monster*." He laughed, a big, warm, golden laugh, and spurred his horse. The Gilded Lances thundered out through the main gate in a flash of gold and a spray of mud, with the painter bouncing along behind.

Walter turned to the Quest Board clerk, who had come out to watch.

"That's the same well," he said. "That's the same well as the Slayers' quest."

The clerk checked her list. "Different quest number," she said, and went back inside.

---

That night, in the barracks, by the light of a single candle, Walter opened his notebook and wrote:

> *12. Impediments are burned. Escalations are eaten. Duplicate quests are not checked.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he added, underneath, in smaller letters:

> *Ysolde didn't chant.*

Outside the window, something hooted.

It was a large, round, extremely self-satisfied owl, sitting on the sill and looking at Walter the way a debt collector looks at a front door. Around its leg was a small scroll with a green seal. When Walter opened the window, the owl held out its leg, waited while he untied the scroll, and then, instead of leaving, settled down on the sill to watch him read it.

> **LEARNING & ENRICHMENT** You have **1** overdue training. **SWORD HANDLING FOR SCRIBES** — *Mandatory.* Due: yesterday. Overdue training may affect lanyard privileges.

"I'm a scribe," said Walter to the owl. "I don't have a sword."

The owl blinked slowly, in a way that suggested this had been noted, and that it would be included in the next report.

He closed the book. Through the high barracks window, the road south was dark. Somewhere along it, three farm boys, a one-eyed veteran and a bard were camped by a fire, probably singing about a head they didn't have yet. And somewhere behind them, on very fast white horses, the Gilded Lances were closing the distance.

Above the dark road, the blue numbers drifted into place, quiet and certain, like the last line of a report nobody would read.

**Parties arriving at the same well: 2.** **Arrival gap: 11 minutes.** **Parties aware of each other: 0.**
