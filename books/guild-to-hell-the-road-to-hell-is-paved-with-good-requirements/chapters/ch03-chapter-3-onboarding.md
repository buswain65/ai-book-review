# Chapter 3: Onboarding

> **WELCOME TO THE FAMILY** **(SEE VALUE #37)**
>
> *— Banner over the main gate, Grand Consolidated Adventurers' Guild of Waterfell*

Walter lay in the mud for a while and made a plan.

It was a good plan, and he was pleased with it, because it was the first plan in nineteen years that did not involve anyone else's deadline. It had three rules:

1. Do exactly what is asked. Nothing more.
2. Never volunteer.
3. Never, under any circumstances, say "Sure, I'll take a look."

He would be invisible. A gray man in a gray job. He would arrive on time, leave on time, and let other people be heroes. He even had the stats for it. All tens. Average in every way that could be measured. For the first time in his life, the universe had given him perfect camouflage.

He got up, wiped the mud off his face with a sleeve that was also mud, and walked to the gate.

The gate guard was a large man in a dented breastplate, leaning on a halberd in the way some people lean on a bar. He had a clipboard tucked under one arm and the expression of a man who had been asked the same question every day for eleven years. A painted wooden name tag on his chest said **HOB**.

"Applicant, client, adventurer, or delivery?" said Hob, without opening his eyes.

Walter considered the options. "Applicant?"

Hob made a mark on the clipboard. "Small door on the left. Follow the signs that say *Talent*. Ignore the signs that say *Talent (Old)*. Don't go through the door that says *Talent (New)*. Nobody knows where that goes."

"Thanks."

"Welcome to the family," said Hob, in the tone of a man reading a hostage statement.

The signs led Walter down a stone corridor, around two corners, and into a small, round room with a round table and a sign on the door that read **TALENT ACQUISITION HUDDLE — IN PROGRESS**. Walter had been inside the walls for exactly four minutes. He noticed this. He tried not to.

Behind the table sat a young halfling woman with a bright green waistcoat and the relentless smile of someone in their first year of a job with the word *Talent* in the title. Her name plate said **PIP THISTLEWOOD, JUNIOR TALENT PARTNER**. She gestured for him to sit, took out a quill, and leaned forward.

"I just have one question," she said seriously. "Can you read?"

"Yes."

Pip gasped. She actually gasped. She wrote something down, underlined it twice, and looked up at him with something close to awe.

"You have no idea," she said, "how hard it is to find scribes. Anyone who can read gets promoted into management within a month. We lost three Quest Scribes last quarter. One of them's a Director now. He's *nineteen*." She pushed a scroll across the table. "You're hired. Junior Quest Scribe, Quest Intake. Starting immediately."

Walter looked at the scroll. It was about as thick as his wrist.

"What's the pay?"

"Competitive."

"Competitive with what?"

Pip blinked. "With… other things."

Above the scroll, as Walter had known it would, a soft blue line of text appeared:

**Time to read this contract: 6 hours. Times anyone has: 0.**

Rule one, Walter reminded himself. *Do exactly what is asked.* Nobody had asked him to read it. He signed at the bottom, in the spot marked with a little drawing of a sword, and Pip clapped her hands.

---

The office of Hearts & Resources was at the end of a long corridor lit by candles that burned with a faint green flame. The name on the door was **MORTIMER GRAVES, HEAD OF HEARTS & RESOURCES**, and underneath it, in smaller letters, **My door is always open**. The door was locked. Walter knocked.

"Come in, come in!" said a voice like a cellar door swinging in the wind.

Mortimer Graves had been dead for three hundred years and was dressed for it. He was a skeleton in beautiful purple robes, with a gold chain of office around his neck vertebrae and a pair of half-moon reading glasses perched on the place where his nose used to be. Two small blue lights burned in his eye sockets. They twinkled when he was pleased, which, Walter would learn, was always.

"Welcome to the family!" Mortimer spread his arms. Something in his left shoulder clicked. "Let's get you set up."

Setup took an hour. At the end of it, Walter had:

- **A lanyard**, with a brass badge and a tiny portrait that painted itself by magic the moment he put it on. It showed a pale, muddy man with wet hair and the eyes of someone who had recently died. Walter compared it, in his mind, to his old SynergyStack badge photo from 2019, in which he had looked hopeful. This one, he felt, was more honest.
- **A welcome gift**: a pewter tankard engraved with the words **I'M PART OF THE GUILD!** Mortimer handed it over as if it were a medal.
- **A crate of orientation materials**: three hundred and twelve scrolls, to be "reviewed at your own pace, by tomorrow."
- **A bunk** in the open-plan barracks, at a long shared table that Mortimer described as "great for collaboration."
- **Unlimited rest days.** "Completely unlimited," said Mortimer, eye-lights twinkling. "Nobody has ever taken one. That's how much they love it here."

"And here's the best part." Mortimer slid across a card. "Every new member gets an onboarding buddy! Someone experienced, to show you the ropes. Yours is Sir Aldric of Fennmoor."

Walter looked at the card. Under the name, a little blue estimate glowed softly into being:

**Time since Sir Aldric was last available: 724 years**

"He's been a bit quiet lately," Mortimer admitted. "But he's still on the list!"

There was one more thing. The badge on Walter's lanyard was enchanted to open the guild's doors. However, the enchantment had to be approved by four separate managers, which normally took three to five business days.

"Until then you won't be able to open any doors," Mortimer said. "Just ask someone to let you in." He leaned forward, eye-lights dimming slightly. "But don't follow anyone *through* a door. That's tailgating. Value #29: Every Door Is a Commitment. People have been culled for less."

"So I can't open doors myself," said Walter slowly, "and I can't go through doors that other people open."

"Exactly!" Mortimer beamed, as much as a skull can beam. "You're going to fit right in."

That night, Walter stood for forty minutes outside the door of the barracks, holding his crate of scrolls and his tankard, until a goblin janitor came along with a mop and opened it for him, and then stood aside and waited, pointedly, for Walter to go through first. Walter decided this counted as being *let in*, not *following*.

It was, he felt, a very invisible way to spend an evening.

---

Orientation took place in the Hall of Enrichment, a long, drafty room with a vaulted ceiling, rows of benches, and a lectern carved in the shape of an eagle that someone had later, and less skillfully, tried to turn into a goose.

There were about thirty new hires. Walter sat near the back, which is where invisible people sit. Around him was the usual crowd of a fantasy guild's intake week. There were farm boys with swords they'd clearly bought that morning. There was a bard who had already tried to sell three people a song about themselves. There was a troll named Brennic who took up a whole bench and was quietly reading the orientation scrolls, all of them, which made him the only person in the room who had. And in the front row sat an old adventurer with one eye, a beard like a burned hedge, and a long scar from his ear to his collarbone. His name was Grenn. He was there, he explained to anyone who asked, because his last orientation had expired.

The facilitator was a round, cheerful man in green robes named Bartholomew Quill, Senior Associate of Learning & Enrichment. He clapped his hands.

"Let's start with an icebreaker!"

There was a low collective groan, the same groan Walter had heard in every conference room of his previous life. It was, he realized, apparently universal. Something about it comforted him.

The icebreaker was Two Truths and a Lie. Bartholomew pointed at Grenn first.

Grenn stood, slowly, with the creak of a man whose knees had seen combat. "One," he said. "I was swallowed whole by a cave wyrm, and I climbed out the other end. Two. I've buried four entire parties. Three." He paused, and looked around the room with his one eye. "I enjoy these sessions."

Nobody needed to vote. Bartholomew laughed a little too loudly and moved on.

Then it was Walter's turn. He stood up and remembered rule two: *never volunteer*. He said the three dullest things he could think of.

"I like soup. I have two nephews. I'm from far away."

"Lovely! And which one's the lie?"

"None of them."

Bartholomew's smile wobbled. "That's not how it works."

"I know," said Walter, and sat down, feeling he had done well. A few people turned and looked at him in the faintly puzzled way you look at a blank wall. That was exactly what he wanted.

It lasted about a minute.

Bartholomew returned to the lectern, unrolled a long schedule, and beamed at the room. "Now! Here's what you can expect. Orientation will take three days."

Above his head, in the familiar soft blue, a number appeared:

**11 days**

And under it, smaller:

*(there is a fourth scroll crate he doesn't know about)*

Walter laughed.

He didn't mean to. It came out of him all at once — a single, loud, helpless bark of laughter, the laugh of a man who has heard *three days* from a thousand presenters at a thousand lecterns, and has finally, after death, been shown the receipt. It echoed off the vaulted ceiling. The bard stopped humming. Brennic the troll looked up from his scroll. Thirty heads turned.

"Something funny, Junior Scribe…" Bartholomew squinted at the badge on Walter's lanyard. "Dunleavy?"

"No," said Walter. "Sorry. Allergies."

Bartholomew wrote something on a small pink slip of paper, folded it carefully, and dropped it into a wooden box on the lectern labeled **CONDUCT NOTES**. It was Walter's first official record in the Grand Consolidated Adventurers' Guild of Waterfell. He had been invisible for one day.

The rest of the morning was the mandatory Dragonfire Safety training. It came as a beautifully illuminated scroll, nine feet long, painted with forty small panels featuring a cheerful cartoon knight named Sir Safely. The panels covered every possible scenario, from a dragon in a field to a dragon in a tavern to, inexplicably, a dragon in a bathtub. Every one of them ended with the same advice, lettered in gold:

**DO NOT BE ON FIRE.**

The final panel showed Sir Safely, slightly singed, giving a thumbs-up. Below him was a box headed **IF YOU ARE ON FIRE**, which said: *See above.*

At the end there was a quiz. It had one question.

*Were you on fire at any point during this training? ☐ Yes ☐ No*

Walter ticked *No*. Next to him, a farm boy chewed his pencil for a long time, then asked to borrow an eraser.

That afternoon Walter tried to slip out to the privy and got stuck between two doors he wasn't allowed to open, for twenty minutes, until Brennic the troll came by, opened both doors at once with one enormous hand, and held them wide, without a word, until Walter had gone through.

"Thank you," said Walter.

"Read the scrolls," said Brennic, kindly, and went back to reading his.

---

The tour, on the second day, was led by Wren.

She was an elf, small and quick, with freckles, pointed ears that poked out through short copper hair, and an apprentice's sash of faded green that had clearly been mended many times. She looked about nineteen. She was, she told them brightly, two hundred years old, and had been an intern for the last hundred and forty of them.

She led the tour walking backward. She never tripped once, not on the stairs, not on the uneven flagstones, not on the dog that was asleep in the second corridor for reasons nobody explained. Walter had worked with a lot of people who had done one job for a very long time. He recognized the particular grace of it.

"First stop!" Wren swept an arm out. "The Quest Board!"

It was an entire wall, two stories high, covered from floor to ceiling with pinned parchment. Fourteen thousand quests, Wren said proudly, possibly more. Each was stamped with a status. Almost all of them said **IN PROGRESS**. Next to many of the adventurers' names, someone had drawn a very small skull.

"Those ones," said Wren, following Walter's eyes, "are technically still in progress. We don't take them down. It would hurt the metrics."

The Map Room was next. In the center, on a stone pedestal, under a glass dome, lay the Grand Roadmap of the Guild. It was a single sheet of fine vellum, beautifully lettered in gold leaf. It said, in full:

*Q3: Dragons?*

"Which year's Q3?" Walter asked.

"Every year's," said Wren.

Then the Library: a vast and silent room of tall dark shelves and rolling ladders, with thousands upon thousands of handsome leather-bound books. Walter pulled one down at random. The gold letters on its spine said **TODO**. He opened it. It was blank. He pulled down another one. **TODO.** Blank.

"They're all like that," Wren said cheerfully. "Take one if you like! Nobody's ever checked one out."

Rule two, Walter reminded himself: *never volunteer*. Then he tucked the book under his arm anyway. A blank book wasn't volunteering. It was stationery.

The last stop was in the lower hall. In the middle of the floor was a wide, round hole, ringed with an iron railing. A brass plaque on the railing read: **THE BACKLOG — PLEASE DO NOT LEAN**.

Warm air rose out of it, and the dry smell of old paper. Walter looked over the edge. He couldn't see the bottom. He wasn't sure it had one.

"Nobody's ever been down," said Wren. "Well. Lots of people have been down. Nobody's ever come *up*."

As if offended by the remark, the Backlog burped.

It was a deep, wet, rumbling sound, like a very large stomach after a very large meal. A gust of warm air whooshed out of the pit, and on it, spinning lazily, rose a single yellowed scroll. It floated up past the railing and hung there in the air, in front of the whole tour group. Across the top, in faded red ink, it said **URGENT: Rescue the Duke's daughter from the tower**. At the bottom it said **DUE:** and gave a date about two hundred years ago.

Wren, without breaking stride, hooked it with her foot and kicked it neatly back into the hole.

"We don't look at those," she said.

The scroll spun away into the dark. And from somewhere very far below — faint and echoing and terribly familiar — a voice called up:

"*Status open!* … *Status op—*"

Walter froze. "Gerald?"

"Oh, that's new," said Wren. "Started yesterday. Things live down there."

The Backlog burped again, more gently this time. A second scroll floated up out of the pit, turning slowly, directly toward Walter.

He knew what he should do. He should let it fall. He should step back. He was a gray man in a gray job, and this was not his scroll.

But nineteen years is nineteen years. When something falls toward an engineer, the engineer catches it. His hand shot out on its own.

The parchment was warm. As his fingers closed on it, a little line of ink appeared at the bottom, writing itself, as if a ghostly clerk were filling it out: **RECEIVED — W. DUNLEAVY, QUEST INTAKE.**

Walter looked at the top of the scroll. It said, in its entirety:

*Deal with the thing in the well.*

"Oh!" said Wren, delighted. "That's an Intake one. And now it's yours. That's how assignment works here. You touch it, you own it."

"Can I put it back?"

Wren looked at him with great kindness, as if he had asked whether he could un-eat a sandwich.

On the way back up, Walter found himself walking next to her. "A hundred and forty years," he said. "As an intern."

"A hundred and forty-one in spring." She pulled the brass badge out from under her sash and held it up. Walter had thought it was decorated. It wasn't. It was stamped, all over, both sides, edge to edge, with tiny overlapping marks, each one saying **RENEWED**. There were so many that the badge had grown thick. It made a faint rattling sound when she moved.

"Every year they say next year," said Wren. And she smiled, and it was a real smile, and there was something underneath it that wasn't.

Walter looked up above her head without meaning to. The blue text tried to appear. It flickered. It started to form the words **Time to full-time offer:**, and then the rest of the line came out as a short gray dash, then a little flickering square, and then nothing at all, as if the universe itself had opened the question, looked at it, and quietly closed it again.

He didn't tell her.

When he looked down again, he realized someone was watching him.

She was leaning against the corridor wall a little way ahead, tall and still, with a long dark braid and a gray-green cloak that had been patched in a dozen places by someone who knew what they were doing. She was cleaning an arrowhead with a small cloth. She had clearly just watched Walter stare, for several seconds, at an empty patch of air above an intern's head.

Most people would have looked away, or laughed. She didn't do either. She just met his eyes and gave him a very small nod — the nod of someone who knows exactly what it is to see a thing nobody else in the room will look at.

Then she pushed off the wall and was gone around the corner.

"Who was that?" Walter asked.

"That's Ysolde," said Wren. "Ranger. She's very good." She thought about it. "She's never been promoted."

---

The Values Assessment took place on the morning of the third day, in a cold stone exam room with high narrow windows, under the supervision of Mortimer Graves.

"There are no wrong answers!" Mortimer told them, handing out the papers with bony, delighted fingers. He paused. "Well. There are quite a lot of wrong answers. But there's no *one* wrong answer."

Walter had a plan for this, too. He would score sixty percent. Not high enough to be noticed, not low enough to be culled. Sixty percent was the grade of a man nobody would ever think about again. He would choose his answers carefully and get exactly a forgettable number of them right.

He turned over the paper.

> **1.** A dragon attacks the guild hall during the Daily Standing Ritual. Do you: **(a)** Attack it immediately *(Value #1: Bias for Action)* **(b)** Plan your response carefully first *(Value #2: Measure Twice, Quest Once)* **(c)** Report it to your manager *(Value #8: Escalate Everything)* **(d)** Handle it yourself *(Value #7: Own Your Quest)*

Walter read it twice. He picked (a), the obvious one. Then he stopped, pen in the air.

If you attacked immediately, you hadn't measured twice. That broke #2. If you planned first, you'd broken #1. Reporting it broke #7, because you hadn't owned it. Handling it yourself broke #8, because you hadn't escalated.

Every answer was correct. Every answer was also a firing offense.

He read on.

> **7.** A client asks for something impossible. Do you: **(a)** Say yes *(Value #23: Customer Obsession)* **(b)** Explain honestly that it is impossible *(Value #11: Radical Transparency)* **(c)** Tell only those who need to know *(Value #12: Strictly Need-to-Know)* **(d)** Refer them to Contracts & Patronage *(Value #24: Never Speak to the Client Directly)*

> **12.** You have finished your work early. Do you: **(a)** Rest *(Value #15: Work-Life Balance)* **(b)** Keep working *(Value #16: The Quest Never Sleeps)*

> **20.** Which of the 47 Values is the most important?

Walter put down his pen and looked around the room.

The farm boys were circling answers with the panicked speed of people who believe there's a trick and hope to outrun it. The bard was writing a song in the margin. Grenn, in the front row, was asleep. Brennic the troll, who had read all three hundred and twelve scrolls, was staring at question one with an expression of real anguish. Walter suspected the troll had already found the problem. He had read every value, and the values disagreed, and nothing in the scrolls explained what to do about that.

And Walter understood, with a strange sinking warmth, exactly what kind of test this was.

It wasn't a test of the right answer. There wasn't one. It was a test of whether you could pick *any* answer and then defend it with a value number, the way every senior engineer he had ever known had defended every decision in every postmortem. You didn't win by being right. You won by citing the correct policy for whatever you'd already done.

He had been doing it for nineteen years. He was, God help him, very good at it.

He picked up his pen. Next to question one, very small, in the margin, he wrote:

*(b) — per #2. Note: #1 is also satisfied, as planning was performed quickly. #8 satisfied by escalating the plan. #7 satisfied by owning the plan.*

He moved on to question seven.

*(d) — per #24. Satisfies #23 (client is routed to the department best placed to obsess over them), #11 (Contracts will be radically transparent about it) and #12 (I now no longer need to know).*

It was about halfway through the paper, at question eleven, that Walter remembered his plan. Sixty percent. Forgettable. Gray.

He looked at his margins. They were full of tiny, beautiful, airtight justifications, every one of them citing at least three values. It was the best work he'd done in years.

He really should stop.

For question twenty, he wrote: *#47 — Embody All 47 Values at All Times — since it's the only value that requires all the others, and therefore cannot be in conflict with any of them, except #46, which allows it to change without notice.*

Then he put down the pen, feeling the warm, terrible satisfaction of a man who has just done excellent work he will definitely regret.

---

The results were posted the next morning on the noticeboard outside Hearts & Resources.

Most of the farm boys had scored in the thirties. The bard had scored eleven percent, plus a note that said *song was not requested*. Grenn had scored zero, as he had, he said, on all his previous orientations, and he seemed at peace with it. Brennic had scored forty-one percent. He stood in front of the board for a long time.

"I read them all," he said quietly.

"I know," said Walter. "You're the only one who did."

"It didn't help."

Walter didn't have anything to say to that, because it was true.

At the very top of the list, by itself, in gold ink, with a small hand-drawn star beside it, was:

**W. DUNLEAVY — 100%**

Mortimer Graves was waiting for him, eye-lights blazing like a pair of birthday candles.

"A perfect score!" the lich cried. "The first in eighty years! Do you know who the last one was? The Vice Guildmaster!" He produced a heavy rubber stamp, breathed on it, and brought it down on Walter's personnel file with a sound like a coffin lid closing. Walter read it upside down.

**STRONG CULTURAL FIT**

Underneath it, Mortimer added, with great ceremony, a smaller stamp: **HIGH POTENTIAL — WATCH LIST**.

Walter looked at the stamps for a long, cold moment.

His plan to be invisible had lasted three days. He had, he calculated, been noticed by the head of HR, written up by Learning & Enrichment, assigned a quest by a hole in the floor, and placed on a watch list.

It was, he had to admit, very on brand.

---

That night the barracks were full of the sounds of thirty people asleep at one long table: snoring, mumbling, the creak of the troll's bench, and, somewhere near the far end, the bard softly singing in his sleep about himself.

Walter sat at his place with a candle, his pewter tankard, and the scroll that said *Deal with the thing in the well*. He was looking at the scroll the way he had once looked at a ticket assigned to him at 4:55 on a Friday.

"You're up."

Wren slid onto the bench across from him. She had a small bundle wrapped in a cloth napkin, which she set down in front of him with some ceremony.

"Honey cake," she said. "For emergencies. You'll have one tomorrow. Intake always does."

Walter thought of a granola bar with chocolate in it, left on a desk in another world, and for a moment he couldn't say anything at all.

"Thank you," he managed.

"A hundred percent," Wren said, shaking her head, admiring. "Nobody gets a hundred. I got ninety-four, my first time." She shrugged, and the badge under her sash rattled faintly. "Mortimer says that's why I'm still an intern. Too high to cull. Too low to promote."

She said it as if it were a fun fact about the weather. Then she said good night, and went off between the sleeping rows, walking backward, and was gone.

Walter sat for a while.

Then he took out the blank book from the Library. Someone had left a pot of ink and a quill at the end of the table, probably by mistake, and he borrowed them. He opened the book to the first page, where someone, a very long time ago, had written **TODO** in tidy gold letters and then, apparently, never done anything else.

He crossed it out. Underneath it, he wrote:

*Things Nobody Wrote Down*

He looked at it for a moment. The last thought he'd had on Earth was that he should've written it down. He'd been right about that, as he was right about most estimates. He'd just been late.

He dipped the quill again and started a list.

> *1. The display in the afterlife is frozen. Do not trust it.* *2. The Backlog burps. Do not catch what comes out.* *3. You touch it, you own it.* *4. Doors: see Value #29. If stuck, ask Brennic.* *5. Every answer on the Values Assessment is correct and also a firing offense. Cite numbers.* *6. Gerald is in the Backlog. Possibly fine.* *7. Wren: 141 years in spring.* *8. Ysolde. Ranger. Never promoted. Nodded.*

He stopped. He looked at number eight for a while, not entirely sure why he'd written it.

Then, above the notebook, softly, in a familiar shade of blue, one last estimate drifted into being. It hung over the page like a weather forecast he already knew he wasn't going to like.

**Estimated time until you say "Sure, I'll take a look": 1 day**

Walter looked at it.

He looked at the scroll about the thing in the well.

He closed the book, blew out the candle, and lay down on his bunk in the dark. Above the table, the little blue number went on glowing, patient and certain, the way they always did.
