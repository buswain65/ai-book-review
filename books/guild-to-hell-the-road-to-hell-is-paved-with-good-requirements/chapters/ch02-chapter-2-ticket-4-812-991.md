# Chapter 2: Ticket #4,812,991

The first thing Walter noticed about the afterlife was the carpet.

It was an industrial carpet, gray-blue, with a pattern of small interlocking squiggles that had clearly been designed by a committee to hide stains. He knew this carpet. Every office he had ever worked in had this carpet. He had once spent a full afternoon at a previous job staring at it during a meeting about another meeting. Seeing it here, stretching away in every direction, he felt something that was not quite surprise. It was closer to recognition.

He was sitting in a plastic chair. The chair was bolted to the chairs on either side of it, which were bolted to more chairs, in long straight rows that ran toward a horizon he couldn't quite see. The walls, where there were walls, were beige. The light came from long fluorescent tubes overhead. One of them was buzzing.

Walter looked down at himself. He was wearing what he'd died in: gray hoodie, jeans, the SynergyStack lanyard with his badge photo from 2019, in which he looked hopeful. Nothing hurt. That was the second thing he noticed. For the first time in roughly a decade, his lower back did not hurt.

*Ah,* he thought. *I'm dead.*

He waited to feel something about it. What came, after a moment, was a single clear question: *Did the deploy finish?*

He decided not to examine that too closely.

In his right hand was a small paper ticket, the kind you pull from a red plastic dispenser at a deli counter. It was faintly warm. Printed on it, in blocky black numerals, was:

**4,812,991**

Under the number, in very small type: *Please retain this ticket. Lost tickets will be reincarnated as lost tickets.*

High on the far wall hung a digital display, red numbers on black, the kind that announces whose turn it is at the pharmacy. It read:

**NOW SERVING: 3,107,446**

Beneath the display, a sign in a cheerful font said: **YOUR AFTERLIFE IS IMPORTANT TO US. PLEASE CONTINUE TO HOLD.**

From a speaker somewhere in the ceiling, softly, came a lazy bossa nova rhythm and a breathy saxophone. Walter knew the song within two bars. It was "The Girl from Ipanema." It was, specifically, the elevator version.

"Of course," he said. "Of course it is."

The room was full. In the row in front of him, a Roman legionary in full armor was working on a crossword. Next to the legionary sat an elderly woman in a Victorian mourning dress, knitting something enormous. Further along were a man in pajamas, a teenage girl in a prom dress, a medieval monk who appeared to be asleep, and a guy in a hockey jersey who kept patting his pockets for a phone that wasn't there. Nobody was talking much. They had the glazed, patient expressions of people who had been waiting for a very long time and had stopped expecting anything to change.

Walter looked at his ticket. Then he looked at the display. Then he did the math, out of habit.

Before he could finish, the answer appeared.

It didn't arrive in his head this time, as a feeling, the way it always had on Earth. It appeared *in front* of him, in the air, about a foot above the display: a row of soft blue glowing numbers, hanging there like a subtitle.

**11,412 years**

Walter stared at it. He blinked. It stayed. He reached out and waved his hand through it, and the numbers rippled like light on water and settled again. As he watched, a second, smaller line of text faded in underneath:

*(if nothing goes wrong)*

He sat back slowly. It was exactly the feeling he'd had his whole life — the shoe-size feeling, the quiet certainty about how long things would take. Except now the universe had apparently decided to put it in writing.

He looked around to see if anyone else could see it. The legionary was chewing his pencil. The Victorian woman was knitting. Nobody was looking at the glowing numbers. Nobody, Walter thought, was ever going to look at the glowing numbers.

"First time dying?" said a voice beside him.

---

The voice belonged to a young man in the next chair. He was about twenty-three, thin, with a mop of dark hair and a T-shirt showing a cartoon girl with enormous eyes and a sword taller than she was. He was holding ticket number 4,812,992, and he was vibrating with joy.

"Is it that obvious?" Walter said.

"You've got the look. Everybody gets the look the first time." The young man stuck out his hand. "Gerald. Delivery truck."

It took Walter a second to realize this was a cause of death. "Walter," he said, shaking it. "Server rack."

Gerald's face fell slightly. "Huh. That's not canon."

"Canon?"

"For isekai." Gerald leaned in. "You don't know isekai? Okay. Okay, this is so great. Okay." He took a breath, the way people do before explaining the one subject they have been waiting their whole life to explain. "*Isekai* is Japanese. It means 'another world.' It's a genre. Light novels, manga, anime. A regular guy dies — usually he gets hit by a truck, there's actually a name for the truck, it's a whole thing — and he wakes up in a fantasy world. With magic. And swords. And he's *special.*"

"Special how?"

"Cheat skills!" Gerald's eyes shone. "The goddess gives you a skill that's totally broken. Infinite mana. Copy any spell. Level up by eating. One guy could just make really good mayonnaise and he basically took over a kingdom with it."

Walter thought about this. "Mayonnaise."

"It's a long story. The point is, you get reborn as a noble's son, or a hero, or a demon lord, and you're overpowered, and everybody's amazed by you, and you get a party, and probably a harem—"

"A what?"

"It's — a lot of people like you. Romantically. It's a whole thing. Not important." Gerald turned over his left hand. On the back of it, in blue ballpoint pen, was a numbered list. "I wrote down the rules, just in case. I've been ready for this since I was twelve."

Walter read it upside down:

> 1. Truck ✔
> 2. Goddess (apologetic, probably)
> 3. Cheat skill — ASK FOR THE GOOD ONE
> 4. Status window
> 5. Do NOT get reincarnated as a vending machine

"What's wrong with a vending machine?"

"It's B-tier," Gerald said darkly. "Same as spiders. Same as slimes. Those guys always end up fine, but it takes, like, nine volumes." He brightened again. "Watch this. Every isekai guy can do this." He straightened in his chair, raised one hand dramatically, and announced to the room:

"STATUS OPEN!"

Nothing happened. The legionary in front of them didn't look up from his crossword. The Victorian woman sighed and kept knitting. Several people within earshot had the expression of commuters hearing the same subway announcement for the ten-thousandth time.

"It's probably disabled until after assignment," Gerald said, unbothered. "That makes sense. That's good design."

Walter found that he liked Gerald. It was hard not to. He had the same quality as Josh — a total, unarmored hopefulness, the kind that SynergyStack usually ground out of people in about eight months. Gerald had apparently brought his all the way into the afterlife intact.

That was when the numbers appeared over Gerald's head.

Walter hadn't asked for them. He hadn't even been thinking about estimates. But there they were, glowing softly in the air just above Gerald's hair, as calm and certain as a weather report:

**Hero potential: 0%**

And under that, smaller, as if the universe had briefly felt bad about it:

*(he'll be fine, though)*

Walter looked away quickly.

"What?" said Gerald. "Is there something on my head?"

"No," said Walter. "No. You're good."

"We're basically next, you know." Gerald waved his ticket. "Four million eight hundred twelve thousand nine hundred and ninety-two. We're right next to each other! That's got to mean something. Maybe we'll be in the same party."

Above the display on the far wall, the blue numbers still hung in the air: **11,412 years.** The display itself still read 3,107,446. Walter realized, now that he was really looking, that it had read 3,107,446 the entire time he had been sitting there.

It had not moved once.

---

Walter watched the room the way he had once watched server logs: quietly, without blinking, waiting for a pattern.

It took about ten minutes. Or possibly ten years. Time in the waiting room was hard to measure. There were no clocks, and the song on the speakers ended and restarted so smoothly that it was impossible to say how many times it had played.

Every so often, a soft chime sounded. A calm, sexless voice from the ceiling said something like *"Ticket two million, four hundred thousand and six, please proceed to the window,"* and somewhere in the rows a soul would look up in astonishment, gather its things, and shuffle off toward a frosted-glass window at the far end of the room. The display on the wall never changed. It said 3,107,446, and it went on saying 3,107,446, as if it had decided long ago that this was the number and saw no reason to revisit it.

Walter began to keep track.

The chime called 2,400,006. Then 4,950,113. Then 12. Then 3,999,999. Then 812. Twice, a number was called, and nobody stood up — possibly because that soul was asleep, or had wandered off down some distant row, or had simply given up listening years ago. The voice waited about three seconds, then moved on. The skipped tickets were never called again.

Walter felt a familiar sensation at the back of his neck. It was the feeling of looking at a system and slowly realizing that nobody was in charge of it.

"It's not a queue," he said aloud.

Gerald looked up. "What?"

"The display's frozen. It's showing an old number — probably whatever it said when something crashed. Whoever's calling the numbers is working from a different list, and that list isn't in order. It's random. Or close to it." He pointed at a sleeping monk two rows over. "If you miss your call, you lose your place. Permanently. You'd just sit here forever." He sat back. "It's not a queue. It's a race condition with chairs."

Gerald stared at him with deep respect. "Is that your cheat skill? Seeing the matrix?"

"No," said Walter. "That's just nineteen years in software."

He knew what he should do next. He should do nothing. He was dead. This was not his system. Somebody, somewhere, was responsible for it, and that somebody was not Walter Dunleavy, who had died about an hour ago (or possibly a decade ago) trying to fix a system that was also not his. There was a lesson here, and it was not subtle.

On the other hand, nobody could fire him anymore.

And on the third hand — the one that had always been the problem — he could see exactly what was broken, and he could not stand to leave it that way.

He stood up.

"Where are you going?" Gerald asked, already standing too.

"To file a bug report."

The walk to the window took longer than it should have. The rows of chairs seemed to stretch politely as he walked, the way rows do in dreams. They passed a Viking, a flapper, two astronauts sharing a magazine, and a man in a powdered wig who said "Don't bother" without looking up.

The window, when they finally reached it, was a pane of frosted glass in a wooden frame, with a little slot at the bottom and a little shelf in front. On the shelf sat a small brass bell.

Taped to the glass above the bell, printed on official-looking card stock, was a sign:

**RING FOR SERVICE**

Taped directly beneath it, handwritten in angry red marker on the back of what looked like a form, was a second sign:

**DO NOT RING FOR SERVICE**

Walter studied the two signs for a moment. He had worked at SynergyStack for six years. He knew how to read a building.

The first sign was policy. The second sign was the person who had to live with the policy.

"Which one do we do?" Gerald whispered.

Walter thought of every unread ticket he'd ever seen at the bottom of every backlog. Then he reached out and rang the bell.

*Ding.*

From behind the frosted glass came a long, slow, heartfelt groan — the groan of someone who had heard that bell a great many times and had loved none of them.

"I *said*," said a woman's voice, "do not—"

The window slid up.

---

The goddess behind the window did not look like the goddesses in Gerald's light novels.

She looked about thirty-five, in the way that a person who has worked a help desk for four hundred years might look thirty-five. Her dark hair was piled on top of her head and held in place by two quill pens. She wore flowing white robes of some impossibly fine fabric, and over the robes a shapeless gray cardigan with a coffee stain on one cuff. A faint halo hovered above her head. It flickered every few seconds, exactly like the fluorescent tube back at SynergyStack, and made a very faint buzzing sound.

The nameplate on her desk read:

**VELLORIA** **Goddess of Things Being Assigned** **(Interim)**

Behind her, the office went back farther than an office should. There were filing cabinets stacked to the ceiling, then more filing cabinets stacked on those. There were towers of paper scrolls leaning against each other like drunks at closing time. On the desk sat three large crystal balls, each glowing a sickly gray, and in the middle of each one a small rainbow-colored wheel turned slowly and endlessly.

Walter knew that wheel. Everyone who had ever used a computer knew that wheel.

"Oh my gosh," Gerald breathed. "It's her. It's the goddess." He elbowed Walter. "Ask for the good skill."

Velloria looked at them both with the flat, exhausted stare of someone at the end of a very long shift that had started before the invention of the printing press. "Tickets."

They held them up.

"Four million," she said. "Wonderful. Sit down. You'll be called."

"We won't, though," said Walter.

She had already begun to lower the window. She stopped.

"Your display's frozen," Walter said. "It's stuck on whatever number it was showing when your system went down. Whatever's calling tickets is pulling from a separate list, out of order. Anybody who misses their call gets skipped, and nothing ever calls them back. Some of those people have been waiting—" he did the estimate without meaning to, and it appeared in the air beside her nameplate in glowing blue — "about four hundred years."

There was a long pause.

"Four hundred and twelve," said Velloria quietly. "Since the upgrade."

She raised the window the rest of the way and leaned her elbows on the counter, as if her body had made the decision before the rest of her could argue.

"The system's called FATE," she said. "Final Assignment and Transfer Engine. It ran fine for nine thousand years. Every soul in, every soul out, matched to the right world, the right life, the right class. Then Upstairs decided it needed to be modernized."

"Upstairs?"

She pointed at the ceiling without looking at it. "The Seven. Archangels of Oversight. They decided FATE 9 was 'legacy.' They pushed FATE 10 on a Friday, said it would be 'seamless,' and went on retreat." She gestured at the crystal balls and their spinning wheels. "It has not been seamless."

As if on cue, a small golden scroll drifted down from somewhere above, unrolled itself in midair in front of her, and displayed a message in glowing, beautifully illuminated script:

> *Hi Team! Just checking in on FATE 10! We know you've got this! Remember: every soul matters. — The Seven* ✨

Velloria waited until it had finished glowing, then picked it out of the air, crumpled it, and dropped it into a bin that was already overflowing with crumpled golden scrolls.

"They send one of those every decade," she said. "Sometimes there's a survey."

"So you've been doing all of it," Walter said slowly. "By hand. Four million souls."

"Four million, eight hundred twelve thousand, nine hundred and ninety-two," said Velloria, glancing at Gerald's ticket. "Give or take."

Walter looked at her — at the cardigan, and the quills in her hair, and the coffee stain, and the halo buzzing like a broken light — and saw, very clearly, every help-desk worker he had ever known. The ones who answered the phone at three in the morning. The ones who were blamed for every outage and thanked for none of the fixes. The ones whose names nobody in management could remember.

And before he could stop himself, he heard his own voice say it.

"Sure. I'll take a look."

Velloria stared at him. It was clearly not a sentence that had been said to her before.

She hesitated. Then she lifted a section of the counter and let him through.

Up close, the crystal balls were warm and humming. Walter leaned over the nearest one and watched the little wheel spin. Above it, almost at once, the glowing blue numbers appeared:

**3 days**

He followed the cables down. There were dozens of them, running from the backs of the crystal balls, down the side of the desk, and into the dark beneath the counter. Walter crouched and looked underneath.

There, wedged under the desk, was the Wheel of Fate.

He knew what it was at once. It was enormous — an ancient spinning wheel of dark wood, taller than he was, its spokes worn smooth by uncountable ages of turning. Golden thread ran from it in every direction, into the walls and the floor and the dark. It was also, somewhere along the line, extremely upgraded. Modern cables had been zip-tied around its spokes. Someone had attached a cooling fan to its hub with duct tape. One spoke was cracked and held together with a splint and what looked like an entire roll of masking tape.

It was turning very slowly, and every few turns it made a small, unhappy creak.

Taped to the rim of the wheel was a sticky note. It was yellow and curling and very, very old. On it, in a handwriting Walter felt he had seen somewhere before, were four words:

**DO NOT TOUCH — ask G.**

Walter went very still.

"Who's G?" he asked.

Velloria crouched down next to him. "Nobody knows. It was here before me. Some people say it means God." She shrugged. "Some people say it was a guy named Gary who had the job before me. He left very suddenly. Something about alpacas."

Walter looked at the note for a long time.

He could see the problem. He could see it *exactly* — a loose thread near the hub, one strand of gold pulled half out of its slot. Just loose enough to time out. Just connected enough to look fine. He knew, the way he knew his shoe size, that if he reached in and pushed it back, FATE 10 would very probably start working.

His hand was already moving.

He thought of a sticky note in a closet. He thought of a folded 2014 Employee Handbook. He thought of a creak, like a ship deciding something.

He took his hand back.

"No," Walter said. "I'm not touching that. Not without documentation."

Velloria looked at him the way someone might look at a unicorn that had wandered into the break room. "You're not going to touch it?"

"Absolutely not." He stood up, brushed off his jeans, and took a pen from the cup on her desk. "But here's what you can do without touching anything." He wrote on the back of a crumpled golden scroll, in big block letters:

**IF YOU MISSED YOUR NUMBER, COME TO THE WINDOW. YOU WILL NOT LOSE YOUR PLACE.**

"Tape that to the glass," he said. "Under both other signs. It won't fix anything. But nobody else gets stuck for four hundred years."

Velloria read it twice. Then, for the first time, something that might once have been a smile showed up at the corner of her mouth.

"Fine," she said. "For that, you're next."

She reached into a drawer and pulled out a form.

---

The form was printed on heavy cream-colored paper that smelled faintly of incense and toner. Across the top it said:

**FORM 1-D: SOUL INTAKE** **(Manual Backup Procedure — For Use Only When FATE Is Unavailable)**

The form was very worn at the corners. It had clearly been used for four hundred and twelve years.

Velloria pulled a quill from her hair, dipped it, and began to write without looking up.

"Name."

"Walter Dunleavy."

"Cause of death." She didn't wait for an answer. Her quill moved down a column of little checkboxes — *Old Age*, *Illness*, *Misadventure*, *Truck* — and ticked one near the bottom. Walter leaned over to read it.

☑ *Crushed by Legacy System*

"That's a checkbox?"

"Fourth most common in your era," said Velloria. "Just behind 'Truck.'" Gerald nodded proudly.

She moved on. **Desired World** and **Desired Class** both had a little paper flap beside them that was meant to flip through the options, like the dial on an old-fashioned calendar. Velloria flicked the World flap with her finger. It flipped, flipped, flipped, and settled on **DEFAULT**. She flicked it again. **DEFAULT.** She flicked the Class flap. **DEFAULT.**

"It does that," she said. "Since the upgrade."

Next came a box of stats. Without FATE, she explained, there was nothing to calculate them from, so they all came in at the baseline. She wrote them in quickly, a column of identical numbers:

**STR 10 · DEX 10 · INT 10 · WIS 10 · CHA 10 · LUCK 10**

Gerald, reading over Walter's shoulder, sucked air through his teeth. "All tens is average," he whispered. "All tens is NPC."

"Thank you, Gerald."

"Sorry."

"Now. Skill." Velloria reached under the desk and hauled up a huge dusty book, bound in leather and stamped in gold: **CATALOG OF GRANTED SKILLS, 4th Edition**. "Without FATE, skills get assigned by hand, based on the trait the soul used most in its last life." She studied Walter for a moment, the way a tailor sizes up a customer. Her eyes drifted up — to the glowing blue numbers that, Walter realized, were still faintly hanging in the air beside the crystal ball. **3 days.**

"Ah," she said. "Obviously."

She flipped almost to the back of the catalog, ran a finger down a page, and turned the book around so he could read the entry.

> **\[TRUE ESTIMATE\]** *The bearer perceives the true duration of any task, journey or undertaking.* **Rarity:** Common **Usefulness:** Situational **Known side effects:** Being ignored. **Reviewer comment:** "Nobody will listen."

Walter read it twice.

"That's… accurate," he said.

"Most of them are." Velloria copied it onto the form. "Questions?"

"Can I appeal?"

She looked at him with something close to sympathy. Then she reached under the counter, tore a fresh ticket from a fresh red dispenser, and handed it to him. It said **APPEAL — #1**.

"You'll be the first," she said. "Congratulations."

Above the ticket, the glowing blue numbers appeared before Walter could stop them:

**3–5 centuries**

He put the ticket in his pocket.

Velloria picked up a large wooden rubber stamp, breathed on it, and brought it down on the bottom of the form with a sound like a small door slamming. When she lifted it, the words were there in red:

**NO REFUNDS**

She paused, with her hand on the form, and looked up at him properly for the first time.

"Mr. Dunleavy," she said. "Can I tell you something?"

"Sure."

"Try not to work yourself to death this time." She said it kindly, and also wearily, like a nurse who has given the same advice to a great many patients. "We see a lot of you."

"A lot of who?"

"People who say 'Sure, I'll take a look.'"

Walter opened his mouth and couldn't think of a single thing to say.

Velloria turned to Gerald. Gerald held out his ticket with both hands, like an offering.

His form took about forty seconds. Name, Gerald Pike. Cause of death, *Truck* (he cheered). World, *Default*. Class, *Default*. Stats, all tens. Then she reached the last flap on the form, a small one Walter hadn't noticed on his own, labeled **SPECIES**. She flicked it. It spun, and stuck.

"Wait," said Gerald. "What does 'default species' mean?"

Velloria glanced at it. "Slime."

There was a pause.

"That's B-tier," said Gerald faintly.

Then, slowly, his whole face changed. He straightened. A light came on behind his eyes.

"No — you know what? Those guys always end up fine!"

Above his head, very softly, the old blue text flickered once: *(he'll be fine, though)*. Walter smiled, for the first time since dying.

"Good luck, Gerald."

"See you on the other side, Walter! Status open! Status op—"

Velloria pulled a lever. A trapdoor opened under Gerald, and he was gone, still shouting.

She turned back to Walter, her hand still on the lever.

"Thank you for the sign," she said. "Nobody ever fixes anything down here. They just complain about the wait."

"It doesn't fix anything," said Walter.

"I know," said Velloria. "It helps, though."

She pulled the lever again.

The floor disappeared, and Walter fell.

He fell for a long time, through a darkness that was not quite empty. Things drifted past him as he went: stars, or something like stars; a filing cabinet, drawers open, spilling paper; a single golden scroll that read *Great job, Team!* And once, far off in the dark, floating all by itself, a thin glowing bar, almost full, frozen at **99%**.

Then there was light, and air, and wind, and a sudden enormous wet *thud*.

Walter lay face-down in something cold and soft. It was mud. It was in his mouth. It smelled strongly of horse.

He lifted his head.

In front of him stood a pair of tall wooden gates, iron-banded, set into a gray stone wall. Above the gates hung a long cloth banner, freshly painted, flapping in the wind. On it, in tall gold letters, were three words:

**SYNERGY THROUGH VALOR**

Walter looked at the banner for a long moment.

Above it, softly, in a familiar shade of blue, a line of glowing text appeared:

**Estimated time to first meeting: 4 minutes**

Walter put his face back down in the mud.
