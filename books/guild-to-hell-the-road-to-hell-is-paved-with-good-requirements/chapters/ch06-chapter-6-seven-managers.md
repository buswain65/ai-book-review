# Chapter 6: Seven Managers

> **OUR PEOPLE ARE OUR GREATEST ASSET** **(ASSET MANAGEMENT IS ON THE 4TH FLOOR)**
>
> *— Banner in the Hall of Hearts & Resources*

On the morning of his sixth day in the guild, Walter walked up to the barracks door, and it opened.

It just opened. He didn't touch it. He didn't wait. He didn't stand there holding a crate of scrolls until a goblin with a mop took pity on him. The brass badge on his lanyard gave a small, warm hum, and the heavy oak door swung inward on its hinges as if it had always wanted to.

Walter stood very still.

Then he stepped back. The door closed. He stepped forward. It opened again. He did this three more times, and was aware that he was smiling like an idiot, and did not care. On Earth he had once waited eleven weeks for a new laptop to be approved. He had not felt anything like this when it arrived.

Above the door, kindly, the blue numbers appeared and made a note:

**Time until this stops working: 41 minutes**

He decided not to look at them.

The other number was harder to ignore. It had been there when he woke, hanging in the gray dawn light at the edge of his vision, smaller than ever:

**11 hours, 40 minutes (too late)**

Somewhere south of here, the Well Monster Slayers were breaking camp and walking the last stretch toward Lower Mumbling. Somewhere behind them, the Gilded Lances were riding very fast white horses. And somewhere in between, at the bottom of a well, was a thing that was *only shy*.

Walter had said he'd take a look.

He'd said it automatically, the way he'd said every "sure" of his old life. But this one hadn't gone upward, to someone with a deadline. It had gone to an old woman on a bench who had waited three weeks for anybody to listen. He couldn't stand the idea of it being one more thing he'd said and not done.

So he was going to Lower Mumbling. He was going to look at the thing in the well himself, before anyone with a sword got there.

There was only one problem. Guild staff were not allowed to leave the grounds during working hours. It was in the 312 scrolls. Brennic had told him. The only exception was a rest day.

And nobody had ever taken a rest day.

---

"A rest day!" Mortimer Graves rose from behind his desk so fast that something in his pelvis clacked. The little blue lights in his eye sockets flared up like a fire someone had thrown oil on. "You want to take a *rest day*?"

"They're unlimited," said Walter. "You said so."

"Oh, they are! They *are*!" Mortimer hurried to a tall filing cabinet in the corner and began pulling out drawers. Dust rose. Something small and gray scuttled out of the third drawer and fled. "It's just that nobody's ever… Ah. Here."

He blew on a sheet of yellowed paper, and a cloud of dust the size of a sheep rolled across the desk. When it cleared, Walter could read the heading.

**FORM RD-1: REQUEST FOR REST**

It had been printed, judging by the typeface, about two hundred years ago. It had clearly never been used. It had seven signature boxes running down the right-hand side, each labeled with a title. Walter recognized some of the titles from the orientation scrolls. The others he didn't recognize at all. At the bottom was one more box:

**REASON FOR REST** *(must not be restful)*

"You just need all seven signatures," said Mortimer happily. "Then you can rest as much as you like!"

Above the form, the blue estimate appeared:

**Time to collect seven signatures: 4 hours, 15 minutes**

Walter did the arithmetic. Four hours fifteen for the signatures. Half a day to get to Lower Mumbling, at the very least. Against eleven hours and forty minutes. It was tight. It was going to be very, very tight.

Under *Reason for Rest*, he wrote: *Client follow-up.* Mortimer read it upside down and looked briefly troubled, then decided, visibly, not to have seen it.

"Splendid," said the lich. "*Splendid.* I'll put it in the newsletter."

Walter thanked him and stepped out into the corridor, form in hand, already walking fast toward the stairs. He got exactly as far as the window at the end of the hall.

Something hooted.

The Enrichment Owl was sitting on the sill, round and smug, with not one but two small scrolls tied to its leg. It looked at Walter with the deep satisfaction of a creature that has been waiting for this. Walter untied the scrolls.

> **LEARNING & ENRICHMENT — FINAL NOTICE** **SWORD HANDLING FOR SCRIBES** — Overdue (2 days). **LADDER SAFETY** — Overdue (newly assigned; due yesterday). Lanyard privileges suspended pending completion.

"I've never used a ladder," Walter said.

The owl leaned forward and tapped his brass badge, once, sharply, with its beak.

The badge stopped humming. It went cold. A tiny red light appeared in the middle of the little portrait of Walter's muddy, hopeless face, and stayed there.

Walter walked to the door at the top of the stairs. It did not open.

He looked back at the window. The owl was gone. Down the corridor, Mortimer leaned out of his office, saw Walter standing in front of the closed door, and gave him a sympathetic little wave.

"Ah! Training lock," called the lich. "Very common!"

Above the door, the blue numbers updated themselves with what Walter could only describe as quiet satisfaction:

**Time to collect seven signatures: 5 hours, 30 minutes**

---

The seven boxes ran up the side of the form like rungs on a ladder. Walter, who had now been assigned a training in ladder safety, tried not to think about that.

As he went, he opened his notebook to a fresh page and started to draw. At the bottom, one small box: *Me*. Above it, as he collected each signature, he added another. He had drawn a great many org charts in his previous life, usually to explain to someone why nothing was getting done. He had a feeling this was going to be one of those.

**Box one** said *Immediate Supervisor*. Since Tobble's promotion, that meant the Associate Director of Intake Excellence. That meant Tobble.

The Directors' wing was all carpet and hush and tall oak doors. Tobble's office was enormous. It had a gold-edged desk the size of a rowboat and three tall windows. In the middle of all that space sat Tobble, very small. He had been a Director for two days, and he already looked the way Walter remembered the hollow-eyed men in the elevator at SynergyStack looking on the way up to the fourteenth floor.

"I have fourteen meetings today," said Tobble, in a faraway voice. "Twelve of them are about the other two."

He read the form. He read it fast, with no finger at all. Walter raised an eyebrow.

"No point hiding it now," said Tobble, and signed. He held out the form, then held on to it for a moment. "Is it nice out there?" he asked quietly. "Outside? I've only seen it through the window."

"It's raining," said Walter.

"Oh," said Tobble wistfully. "Rain." He let go. "*Go*."

**Box two** said *Scrum Cleric* and **box four** said *Senior Manager of Quest Management*, and Walter got both, out of order, in the same corridor. Brother Tobiah was there drawing tarot cards and writing numbers on passing quests, like a man feeding ducks. He read the form, called a rest day "an impediment to velocity," murmured "We acknowledge it," and began, quite naturally, to move it toward a candle, until Walter took it back. "But signing," Tobiah decided, brightening, "is also a ceremony," and signed with a looping flourish and a tiny hourglass. Sir Wendell Prawle, a large, damp man eating a pie as he walked, signed it against Tobiah's back without putting the pie down. "This the one about the goblins?" "No." "Great," said Sir Wendell, and that was the whole meeting.

**Box three** said *Quest Owner*. Pell Odwyn's office was papered with ideas, every one ending in a question mark: *Dragon-themed tavern?* *Quest subscriptions??* *Heroes but smaller?* Pell was young and eager, with a long striped scarf and the shining eyes of a man who had never once been asked to build anything he'd thought of.

"A rest day!" said Pell. "Love it. Rest. Very on-trend." He signed with a quill shaped like a feather from a bird that didn't exist. Then he grabbed Walter's sleeve. "Oh — quick thought. While you're at the well. Hear me out." He spread his hands in the air, framing an invisible picture. "*Purple.*"

"Purple what?"

"The well! Purple well. Royal. Premium. Brax would *love* it."

"It's a well," said Walter. "In a village. It's made of stones."

"Not with that attitude," said Pell warmly, and pinned a fresh sketch to the wall: *Purple?* Walter suspected that it was not the last he would hear of purple.

**Box five** said *Director of Questing*. It took Walter forty minutes to get into the office of Director Hesper Gallowmere, because there was a queue.

The office was spotless. Every wall was covered with charts, beautifully drawn: Valor Velocity by party, by month, by season, by phase of the moon, every line climbing gently upward. On the desk, arranged in a neat row, sat seven small brass bells on little stands, each with a tiny engraved plate. While Walter stood waiting, three of them rang, one after another, and each time the woman behind the desk flinched very slightly, like a horse hearing a whip.

Director Hesper was perhaps fifty, sharp-featured, immaculately dressed, with her gray hair pulled back so tightly it seemed to be holding her face in place. She looked like a woman who had not slept properly in eleven years and had made peace with it by never sitting down.

She was the only one of the seven who actually read the form.

She read every line of it, including the small print. When she reached *Reason for Rest: Client follow-up*, she stopped, and looked up at him for a long, cool moment.

"Rest days," she said at last, "count as zero Valor. Your velocity will drop. It will show on my charts." She nodded at the walls. "Up there. Where they can see it."

Walter waited.

She signed.

Then, as he reached for the form, she held on to it, the way Tobble had, and for a moment something moved behind her face. It looked like a question she hadn't asked out loud in a very long time.

"Scribe," she said quietly. "Is it—"

One of the little brass bells rang.

Hesper straightened. The thing behind her face went away. "That's all," she said, and let go.

**Box six** said *Vice Guildmaster of Strategy*. It was at the very top of the west tower, up a staircase so long that Walter had time to finish his whole org chart and start doubting it. The office was dim and dusty and full of tall stacks of bound documents, every one of them titled *Strategy (Draft)*. Among them, at a desk by the window, sat an extremely old man with a white beard and soft, tired eyes: Lord Ambrose Fenn.

Lord Fenn looked at the form. Then he looked, with interest, at Walter's personnel file, which had arrived ahead of him by Chute. He saw the stamp on the front — **STRONG CULTURAL FIT** — and he laughed, very softly.

"They put that on mine, too," he said. "Eighty years ago. A hundred percent on the Values. First in my year." He signed the form, slowly and carefully. Then he held it up, and looked at Walter over the top of it with a gentleness that was hard to bear.

"Take the day," said Lord Fenn. "I never took one. I meant to. There was always next quarter." He smiled. "Then it was eighty years."

Walter took the form. He couldn't think of anything to say. Lord Fenn didn't seem to need him to.

**Box seven** said simply *Guildmaster*. The Guildmaster's antechamber was at the top of the east tower, and it was as close as Walter got. Through a half-open door beyond it, he could see a vast room, and in the middle of it, half a dozen painters on scaffolding working on an enormous new banner. All he could read so far was: **SYNERGY IS NOT A DESTINATION, IT IS A**—. Of the Guildmaster himself there was no sign.

At a small, very tidy desk in front of the door sat a brisk gnome in spectacles, with a nameplate that read **MISTRESS PENNYWHISTLE — OFFICE OF THE GUILDMASTER**. She took the form without a word, checked all six signatures, inked a large stamp and brought it down.

**APPROVED (PENDING)**

"Pending what?" said Walter.

Mistress Pennywhistle looked at him over her spectacles. "Pending," she said.

Then, flatly, the way other people mention rain: "Form RD-1 was retired by the Template Governance Council at nine o'clock this morning. The current form is RD-2." She tapped a fresh form on the corner of her desk. It had nine signature boxes.

Walter looked at the nine boxes. He looked at the number in the corner of his eye.

"I'll take pending," he said.

It wasn't really permission, and he knew it. If he left now, he'd be leaving because he chose to. He looked at his notebook: seven boxes, stacked one above another, with his own small box at the bottom. Then he went anyway.

It had taken five hours and thirty minutes, exactly. In the corner of his eye, the other number was waiting.

**6 hours, 10 minutes (too late)**

---

Mother Tansy was still on her bench in the lobby, behind the velvet rope, with her turnips and her wooden token. She had been there for three weeks and two days.

Walter ducked under the rope and sat down beside her.

"Mother Tansy," he said, "I'm going to your well. Right now. Would you like to come home?"

He had never seen anyone of her age move so fast. She was on her feet with her basket on her arm before he'd finished the sentence. Her straw hat was already on her head. As far as Walter could tell, she had been wearing it all along, in case.

The clerk from Contracts & Patronage appeared at Walter's elbow like a draft from under a door. His face was as closed as ever.

"Sir," he said. "Value #24."

Walter held up Form RD-1, with all seven signatures and the large red stamp. "I'm on a rest day," he said. "Today I'm not staff."

The clerk took the form and read it. He reached the stamp — **APPROVED (PENDING)** — and stopped. His face, for the first time, opened a crack. Behind it, for a moment, Walter could see real confusion, the confusion of a man discovering a situation that the rules had not prepared him for. He began to leaf through a small book of regulations he kept on a chain at his belt.

By the time he looked up, they were gone.

---

The stables were warm and smelled of hay. Walter explained to the head groom that he needed something to ride, and that he couldn't ride.

The groom looked him up and down, considered his options and brought out a mule. It was small, gray and middle-aged, with an expression of deep, long-suffering patience.

"His name's Deliverable," said the groom. "He's slow. But he gets there eventually."

"Story of my life," said Walter.

At the far end of the stables, a tall woman with a long dark braid was saddling a gray mare. She didn't look up as they came in. But when Walter led Deliverable past, she glanced at the mule, then at Mother Tansy, then at the form still in Walter's hand, and stopped what she was doing.

"Where are you going?" said Ysolde.

"Lower Mumbling. Do you know the road?"

"Main road's a day and a half." She tightened a strap. "Ranger's road's half a day."

Walter looked at the countdown in the corner of his eye. Six hours and change. "Half a day," he said, "is about all I've got."

Ysolde looked at the form. "You got all seven?"

"It says pending."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. It was too small to be called a smile. Walter decided to count it anyway.

"I've filed eleven of those," she said. "None of them ever came back."

"What did you do?"

"Went anyway." She swung up into the saddle in one smooth motion. Then she reached down a hand to Mother Tansy, and lifted the old woman, basket and turnips and all, up onto the horse behind her as if she weighed nothing. "Keep up," she told Walter, and rode out into the rain.

---

The ranger's road was not, strictly speaking, a road. It was a narrow, muddy track that wound through forests and over hills and along the edges of streams, and in places it was really just a direction Ysolde seemed to know. She rode in front, silent and sure. Mother Tansy sat behind her, holding on with one arm and her basket with the other, and talked.

She talked about the village, and about her late husband, and about the price of turnips. She talked about the well. She called whatever was in it "my little one" and "the dear," and said it had been there since her grandmother's grandmother's day. She never quite said what it *was*. Walter got the impression that she had never had to explain it to anyone before. Everyone in Lower Mumbling simply knew.

Walter came behind on Deliverable, who trotted when he felt like it, which was rarely, and stopped to think about things, which was often. Walter bounced. It began to rain harder. Somewhere around the third hour, his legs stopped reporting in.

Above the track ahead, the blue estimate hung in the drizzle like a lantern:

**Arrival: 2 hours, 58 minutes**

And beside it, in the corner of his eye, the other one:

**3 hours, 2 minutes (too late)**

Four minutes. It was going to be close. It was going to be very close.

Somewhere in the fourth hour, the track widened for a stretch beside a river, and Deliverable, in a rare burst of ambition, drew level with Ysolde's mare. Mother Tansy had fallen asleep against Ysolde's back. The rain had eased to a fine mist. For a while the three of them rode side by side in silence, listening to the river.

"Why are you helping me?" Walter asked, finally.

Ysolde didn't answer right away. She looked ahead at the track. Then she looked at him — not for very long, but straight on, in the same steady way she'd looked at him in the corridor, as if she were reading something in his face that most people didn't bother to look for.

"You said it was harmless," she said.

"And you believe me?"

"If you say it's harmless," said Ysolde, "it's harmless."

Then she touched her heels to the mare and rode on ahead, around a bend in the river, and was gone into the trees.

Walter rode on behind her for a long time without saying anything. He was thinking about it. In nineteen years, he could not remember a single time someone had simply taken his word for something — a number, an estimate, a warning — without arguing, or asking for it in writing, or scheduling a meeting about it. He hadn't known how much he'd wanted that until it happened.

Deliverable, sensing a moment, stopped dead to eat a thistle.

---

They came over the last hill at dusk. Below them, in a shallow green valley, lay the village of Lower Mumbling: two dozen thatched cottages, a mill, a duck pond and a little village green. In the middle of the green stood a round stone well with a small wooden roof.

There was smoke rising from the chimneys. There were torches on the green.

And there was shouting.

---

The village green of Lower Mumbling had been turned, in the space of about an hour, into a small war.

On one side of the well stood the Well Monster Slayers: three muddy farm boys behind a shield whose paint had finally dried, and which now said, a little crookedly, **THE WELL MONSTER SLAYERS**. On the other side stood the Gilded Lances, four gleaming knights in a perfect line, their gilded lances lowered. Their white horses stamped behind them. Between the two groups, around the well, lay about twenty feet of trampled grass, a dropped helmet, and a great deal of shouting.

"We were here *first*!" Hamm was yelling. "It's *our* quest!"

"Ours has a higher quest number!" called a tall knight on the Lances' side, whose name, Walter would later learn, was Sir Tristan.

"Ours was posted first!"

"Ours has a *gold seal*!"

"Ours," cried Little Jory, "has a *ballad*!"

This turned out to be no longer exclusive. Lysander the bard sat on a barrel, singing two songs in alternating verses: "The Ballad of the Well Monster's Head" for the Slayers, and, for the Lances, "Sir Percival and the Beast of Mumbling," written in the last twenty minutes for a fee. Both ended with the monster's head on a pole. Neither had a monster in it.

Behind the Lances, on his gray pony, the court painter had set up his easel and was working fast, getting the whole scene down in bold heroic strokes.

At the head of the Lances, on his tall white horse, sat Sir Percival Brightshield. He was not shouting. He was smiling, warmly, at everybody, the way the sun might smile at a minor dispute between two ant colonies.

"Friends! *Friends*," he called, holding up a gauntleted hand. "There's no need for this. I have a solution." He beamed. "A contest! Whoever slays the beast first, wins!"

This was, Walter would later reflect, exactly the kind of solution a man like Sir Percival would think of. It was fair, it was simple, and it made everything worse. Both parties charged the well at once.

They did not reach it. They reached each other.

What followed was not so much a battle as a sort of large, clanking shove. Hamm went sideways over a Lance's boot and came down hard on his ankle. Little Jory, ducking under a horse, was bitten on the shoulder by Sir Percival's white stallion, which seemed to feel it was being left out. One of the Lances' squires, a pink-faced boy carrying spare lances, was hit squarely on the head by the Well Monster Slayers' shield, swung by Corwin with more enthusiasm than aim, and sat down very suddenly in the duck pond.

Above the green, as calm and neat as a scoreboard, the blue numbers tallied it up:

**Injuries: 3 (all friendly)**

And in the middle of all of it, sitting on the stone rim of the well itself with his legs crossed and his back to the whole affair, was Grenn. He had his pipe out. He was smoking it slowly, gazing off across the darkening valley, with the deep peace of a man who has seen this before and expects to see it again.

It was into the middle of this that Deliverable the mule, carrying Walter Dunleavy, walked at an unhurried pace and stopped, precisely between the two parties, to eat a patch of grass.

The shoving slowed. People looked at the mule. People looked at the man on the mule. Walter was soaked through, splattered with mud from the ranger's road up to his collar, and had not felt his legs for two hours. In the corner of his eye, the other number had reached the end.

**0 minutes (too late)**

He took a breath.

"*What*," said Walter, very loudly, "*is the thing?*"

The green went silent. Even the bard stopped.

"The *monster*," said Sir Percival, as if explaining that water was wet. "Obviously."

"Has anyone," said Walter, "actually looked?"

Nobody answered. Hamm, holding his ankle, looked at Sir Tristan. Sir Tristan looked at Sir Percival. Sir Percival looked, for the first time, faintly uncertain, as if somebody had asked him a question in a language he only partly spoke.

On the rim of the well, Grenn took the pipe out of his mouth.

"Nobody's looked," he said. "They've been arguing for an hour about who gets to." He put the pipe back in. "I said I'd wait."

It was then that Mother Tansy slid down off the back of Ysolde's horse, took a firm grip on her basket of turnips and marched straight across the green. She went between the two parties like a small ship through a fleet. When one of the Lances failed to move his lance out of her way quickly enough, she rapped him smartly across the knuckles with a turnip, and he said "Ow," and moved it.

She reached the well. She set down her basket. She put both hands on the old stone rim, right next to Grenn, who moved over politely to make room. And she leaned over the dark water and spoke into it, gently, the way you'd speak to a frightened cat under a bed.

"Come out, love," said Mother Tansy. "It's only me."

---

For a moment nothing happened.

Then, deep down in the well, the water began to glow.

It was a soft light, blue-green, like sunlight through a pond. It rose slowly up the stone shaft, brighter and brighter, and nine armed adventurers, a bard, a painter, a ranger, a mule and a scribe all leaned in, despite themselves, to look.

What came up out of the well was about the size of a cat.

It was made of water. Clear, shimmering water, held in a small, round, wobbly shape, glowing faintly from inside. It had two large dark eyes like smooth river pebbles. On its head, pulled down low like a helmet, it wore a very old wooden bucket.

It looked at the circle of swords and lances and knights and farm boys. It looked at the court painter. It looked at the mule. It began, very gently, to tremble, so that little ripples ran across its whole body. Then it made a small, unhappy sound, exactly like a single drop of water falling into a still pool.

*Plink.*

"There," said Mother Tansy, with enormous tenderness. "See? *Shy.*"

Nobody said anything. Sir Percival slowly lowered his lance. So, one by one, did the other Lances. Corwin put the shield down. Lysander, on his barrel, stopped mid-strum.

Above the little creature, quiet and blue, Walter's estimate gave its name at last:

**Well-sprite (age: approx. 300 years) (very confused)**

"It's kept our water sweet since my grandmother's grandmother's day," said Mother Tansy, stroking the stone rim. "Best water in the valley. Never a fever in Lower Mumbling, not once. Never a bad well." She looked up at the wooden roof over the well, where a crank handle and a coil of chain hung from a wooden roller. "Only, the thing's gone rusty. Every morning when somebody draws water, it screeches like a cat in a door. Wakes the little one up, poor dear. Frightens it. Then it hides down at the bottom all day, and won't let the water come up. Half the time the bucket comes up empty."

Walter looked at the crank. He looked at the chain. He looked at the old wooden roller, black with age, wound round with rusty links.

"The thing," he said slowly. "When you wrote *deal with the thing in the well*. You meant…"

"The *thing*," said Mother Tansy, pointing at the roller. "The winding thing. The— oh, what do they call it—"

"The windlass," said Walter.

"*That's* it!" She beamed at him. "I never could remember the word. So I just put *the thing*."

Walter closed his eyes.

Three weeks on a bench. Seven managers. Four offices, a template migration, a tarot card drawn in a corridor, two parties, three injuries and a painted shield. All of it because one old woman didn't know the word *windlass*, and nobody in the whole Grand Consolidated Adventurers' Guild of Waterfell had ever walked out to the lobby and asked her.

He opened his eyes. Above the rusty windlass, the blue numbers appeared, calm and certain, exactly as they had appeared over his very first draft of the quest, four days and a whole world ago:

**1 afternoon**

---

It took an afternoon's worth of work, which meant it took until well after dark.

Grenn turned out to have been a blacksmith's boy, long ago, before the guild. He knocked out his pipe, rolled up his sleeves and took the windlass apart with the calm hands of a man who had been waiting all day to do something useful. Ysolde produced a new iron pin from a pouch on her saddle without being asked, as if she had known all along that someone would need one. The farm boys held torches. Hamm, propped against the well on his good ankle, held the bucket. The pink-faced squire, who had dried out somewhat, held the other torch.

And Sir Percival Brightshield, to Walter's considerable surprise, rode over, swung down from his white horse and held out a small silver tin.

"Armor polish," he said. "Finest in the kingdom. Will it help?"

Walter looked at him. "It might."

"Then have it!" said Percival generously, and stood by, beaming, holding his own torch, as if being helpful was a new kind of quest that he was enjoying very much.

They cleaned the rust off every link. They greased the roller with the finest armor polish in the kingdom. Walter fitted Ysolde's pin. When it was done, he took hold of the crank and turned it.

The windlass turned without a sound.

The bucket went down into the dark, smoothly, silently, and came back up full to the brim with clean, cold, shining water.

From the bottom of the well came a glow. The sprite rose up the shaft again, slowly, cautiously, and peered over the rim with its pebble eyes. It looked at the silent windlass. It looked at the full bucket. Then, very slowly, it took the old wooden bucket off its own head and held it out with two small watery arms to Mother Tansy, the way a child gives back something it has borrowed.

Then it did a little spin in the air, glowing brighter for a moment, sank back down into the well and was gone.

The water in the well shone faintly blue for the rest of the night.

Not everyone had been at the well for all of it.

Sir Tristan of the Gilded Lances had remembered, somewhere in the middle of the repairs, that his quest had *two* parts. He had gone looking for the second one. A few minutes later, a muffled clanking and a great deal of muffled swearing began to come from inside Mother Tansy's cottage, and then from inside Mother Tansy's chimney.

It took two farm boys, two Lances and a rope to get him out. He came down the chimney feet first in a cloud of soot, black from helmet to spurs, and stood up in the fireplace with enormous dignity. In his gauntleted fist, flapping and outraged, he held a single pigeon.

"Chimney monster," said Sir Tristan. "Secured."

The pigeon bit him.

"I didn't order a chimney," said Mother Tansy, to nobody in particular. Nobody was listening. The painter was already sketching the pigeon, much larger.

Above the fireplace, the blue numbers made a small correction to the day's figures, with the air of a clerk initialing a margin:

**Injuries: 3 (all friendly) (pigeon not counted)**

---

The court painter had finished his picture by then.

He had been working the whole time, by torchlight, without looking up. When he turned his easel round, the whole green gathered to look. It was a beautiful painting, truly. It showed Sir Percival Brightshield, magnificent on a rearing white horse, his gilded lance raised against an enormous coiling sea serpent that burst, fanged and terrible, out of the mouth of the well. Kneeling villagers watched in awe. Behind Sir Percival, the Gilded Lances stood in a gleaming row. Nobody else was in it.

Across the bottom, in gold letters, the painter had written: **SIR PERCIVAL TAMES THE BEAST OF MUMBLING**.

Sir Percival looked at it for a long time. He seemed honestly impressed.

"Gosh," he said. "Is that what happened?"

"It is now," said the painter, and began packing up his brushes.

---

The painting arrived back at the guild two days before the Well Monster Slayers did, because it traveled on a fast white horse. By the time Hamm limped in through the main gate, it had already been hung in the great hall, just under the banner, with a small brass plaque.

Somewhere on the ride home, Lysander had quietly merged his two ballads into one. It was about Sir Percival.

The Quest Board told the rest.

> **PREMIUM HEARTH & HOME — LOWER MUMBLING** **GILDED LANCES** — *VICTORY (Beast Tamed). Chimney monster: SECURED.* 13 Valor.

> **KILL THE WELL MONSTER** **WELL MONSTER SLAYERS** — *FAILED. Victory Indicator not met: monster not dead.* 0 Valor.

Grenn read it, nodded slowly, as if it were the weather, and went to get a drink. "Told you," he said in passing. "Always a monster."

Little Jory stayed in front of the board after the others had gone. He had a bandage on his shoulder where the horse had bitten him, and he read the line about the Slayers three times, his lips moving.

"Did we fail?" he asked Walter.

"You held the torch," said Walter. "You helped fix it."

Little Jory looked at the board for a long moment.

"That's not on the board," he said.

On Walter's desk in Quest Intake, waiting for him, was a small pink slip.

**CONDUCT NOTE: Unauthorized Client Contact (while resting).**

On the fourth floor, the next morning, the newest chart on Director Hesper Gallowmere's wall showed a small but distinct dip in Quest Intake's Valor Velocity. Someone had labeled it, in neat red ink: **W. DUNLEAVY — REST DAY**. One of the seven little brass bells on her desk had been ringing since dawn, and showed no sign of stopping.

Deliverable the mule arrived back at the guild a full day after everyone else. The stable ledger recorded him as *Deliverable — late*. After some thought, the stable master issued the mule a pink slip.

---

That night, in the barracks, Walter opened his notebook to the page with the org chart on it.

Seven boxes, stacked neatly one above another. Immediate Supervisor. Scrum Cleric. Quest Owner. Senior Manager. Director. Vice Guildmaster. Guildmaster. And at the very bottom, one small box that said *Me*.

He looked at it for a while. Then, off to the side of the page, away from all the boxes and all the lines, he drew one more small box, and wrote in it: *Mother Tansy (client)*.

No line connected it to anything. He didn't draw one. There wasn't one to draw.

Underneath the chart, he added an entry to his list:

> *13. Ask what the thing is. Ask first.*

Above the page, quietly, in the familiar soft blue, the numbers drifted into place, like a footnote to the whole day:

**Managers between you and the client: 7.** **Managers who have met the client: 0.**
