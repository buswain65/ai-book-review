# Chapter 7: Sold

> **WE DON'T SELL QUESTS. WE SELL DREAMS.** **(DREAMS ARE NON-REFUNDABLE.)**
>
> *— Banner over the door of Contracts & Patronage*

The Enrichment Owl came in through the refectory window during breakfast, and the whole guild went quiet the way a pond goes quiet when a hawk's shadow passes over it.

It flew the length of the long hall, low and unhurried, over two hundred bowls of porridge. As it flew, it dropped a small scroll onto every plate. *Plop. Plop. Plop.* Nobody was missed. Nobody ever was. When it reached the far end, it settled on a ceiling beam, folded its wings and watched everybody open their scrolls with the bright, patient interest of a cat watching mice discover a cupboard is locked.

Walter opened his.

> **LEARNING & ENRICHMENT — GUILD-WIDE MANDATORY TRAINING** **MARITIME SAFETY** Includes: Knots, Bilge Procedure, Man Overboard, and Lifeboat Drill (courtyard, noon). Completion is a condition of employment.

Across the table, Grenn read his, folded it neatly and put it in his porridge.

"Third time," he said. "I've never seen the sea."

"Does the guild own a boat?" Walter asked.

"No," said Grenn.

"Is there a lake?"

"There's the duck pond behind the stables."

"So what do they do at the lifeboat drill?"

Grenn looked at him for a long time with his one eye. "We sit in a line in the courtyard," he said, "and we row."

At the end of the table, Brennic the troll held up his scroll and showed them, a little shyly, that it was already stamped **COMPLETE**. He had done it, he explained, last night, in case.

---

Walter was halfway through his porridge when a runner from the fourth floor appeared at his elbow with a folded note sealed in red wax.

> **FROM: Office of the Director of Questing** **TO: W. Dunleavy, Quest Intake** **RE: Velocity Recovery Assignment** You are assigned as minute-taker for today's Royal Meeting. Great Hall, ten o'clock. Do not be late. Do not be early. Do not be noticed. — H.G.

"Velocity recovery," said Wren, reading over his shoulder, with sympathy. "It's what they give you after a dip. To show you're committed."

"What's a Royal Meeting?"

"Someone from the palace comes to buy a quest." Wren's eyes went wide and bright. "It's very exciting. There's *bunting*."

On his way up to collect the minute-book, Walter passed the door of Director Hesper Gallowmere's office. It was closed. Behind it, faint but steady, one small brass bell was still ringing. It had been ringing, Wren had told him, for three days.

The minute-book was kept in the office of Mistress Pennywhistle, the Guildmaster's secretary. It was a vast, leather-bound volume with brass clasps and a lock, and it weighed about as much as a small dog. Mistress Pennywhistle handed it over along with a quill, an inkpot and a small card of instructions, which she read aloud to him, briskly, over her spectacles.

"Record what is said," she said. "Do not record what is meant. The minute-taker sits at the side table. The minute-taker does not speak." She looked at him over the top of the card. "The minute-taker is *furniture*. Do you understand?"

"I'm furniture," said Walter.

"Furniture," said Mistress Pennywhistle, "does not have opinions."

She went back to her work. Walter stood holding the heavy book against his chest, thinking about the last time he had been furniture. It had been about four thousand meetings ago, in another world. He had been very, very good at it.

Above the minute-book, in soft blue, a small estimate formed:

**Time until the furniture has an opinion: 1 hour, 6 minutes**

---

There was, as promised, bunting.

It hung in red and gold loops from every beam of the Great Hall. Below the main banner, freshly hung, was the court painter's picture of Sir Percival Taming the Beast of Mumbling, sea serpent and all. A long oak table had been set in the middle of the floor with silver jugs and little plates of candied figs. At the great double doors stood two rangers in polished leather as an honor guard, spears grounded, perfectly still. One of them was Ysolde. She didn't look at Walter as he came in. Furniture, he reminded himself, isn't looked at.

He took his place at the small side table, opened the minute-book and dipped his quill.

Brax Hollister was there already, in a cape even more fur-trimmed than usual. So were Pell Odwyn, scarf and all, with a sketchbook; Brother Tobiah, with a small hourglass; and Director Hesper Gallowmere, standing very straight against the wall behind the Guildmaster's chair, her face giving away nothing at all.

Then the far doors opened, and the Guildmaster came in.

Lord Aldous Vayle was enormously tall. He had a great silver mane of hair, robes of purple and gold that moved around him like weather, and a voice like a cathedral organ that someone had taught to give speeches. Walter had worked for men like him before. Not exactly like him, but close. The kind of man who could walk into a room and make everyone in it feel, for about twenty minutes, that the future was going to be wonderful, and then walk out again before anyone asked how.

He had never, Wren had told him, been on a quest. He had never needed to.

Walter wrote:

> *10:00. Guildmaster enters. Applause.*

The King's Chamberlain arrived a minute later, escorted by two pages. Lord Oswy Thorne was a thin, elderly courtier with a heavy gold chain of office and the permanently anxious face of a man whose job was to keep a king happy. He sat down at the table and immediately began to worry out loud.

"The Royal Jubilee," he said, "is in thirty days. Forty years on the throne. His Majesty wants something *spectacular*. Something no one has ever seen."

"At the Guild," said Lord Vayle, spreading his hands, "we don't solve problems. We solve *tomorrow's* problems, *today*."

Walter wrote it down. He did not know what it meant. He was fairly sure that nobody did.

> *10:04. Guildmaster: "We solve tomorrow's problems today." Applause.*

The Chamberlain went through the options the palace had already considered. Fireworks: done, at the last Jubilee. A cake the size of a cottage: done, and someone had fallen in. A parade: done, every year, and the King had begun to fall asleep during it.

"What about," said Brax, leaning forward with his most dazzling smile, "a *dragon*?"

The Chamberlain blinked. "We have a dragon. Mount Kessel. He's been up there for four hundred years."

"Is he a problem?"

"Well. He sends letters." The Chamberlain looked unhappy. "For years now. Long ones. We assume they're threats. But nobody can actually read his handwriting — it's all loops and flourishes, very ornate. The Duke of Kessel's steward keeps them in a drawer." He shook his head. "The last three heroes who went up the mountain came back very confused."

He drew a folded sheet from his sleeve and laid it on the table, the way you might put down a dead mouse a cat had brought in. "This one came last month."

From the side table, Walter could just see it. It was a single sheet of heavy, cream-colored paper, covered edge to edge in the most beautiful handwriting he had ever seen: great looping, swirling lines of ink, curling round each other like vines, every capital letter decorated with tiny flourishes. It was impossible to read. It looked less like a threat than like a wedding invitation written by someone with a great deal of time on his hands.

Above it, soft and blue, an estimate drifted into place:

**Time to read this properly: 20 minutes. Times anyone has: 0.**

The Chamberlain folded it up again and put it back in his sleeve.

Walter wrote that down, too. Something about it snagged in his mind, the way a loose thread catches on a nail. *Letters. Nobody can read them. A drawer.* He underlined it, very lightly.

"Then here's what we'll do," said Brax. He stood. He walked around the table. He put both hands flat on the oak, the way men in his profession always did when they were about to say a number. "The Guild will deal with the Dragon of Mount Kessel. By the Jubilee. Thirty days."

The Chamberlain's eyes went wide.

Above the table, where Brax's hands lay flat on the oak, the blue numbers appeared, calm and certain:

**9 months**

"And," said Brax, holding up one finger, "*complimentary*. A castle."

"What castle?"

"The *dragon's!*" Brax spread his arms. "Once it's dealt with, it's yours. Full lair-to-palace conversion. Our people handle everything."

Above the table, the blue number flickered and grew:

**11 months**

Walter had no idea who "our people" were. He suspected, with a sinking feeling, that they might turn out to be him.

"Quick thought," said Pell Odwyn, looking up from his sketchbook with shining eyes. "For the Jubilee parade. Could the dragon be *purple*?"

"Done!" said Brax.

**13 months (purple)**

"And for the royal grandchildren," Brax went on, warming to it, "*dragon rides!*"

The Chamberlain clasped his hands. "Oh, the King would *love* that."

**14 months, plus insurance**

Walter watched the number grow over the table like a debt. Nobody else looked at it. Nobody else could.

Lord Vayle rose to his full magnificent height and snapped his fingers. In the corner of the hall, two painters who had apparently been waiting there all morning on scaffolding unrolled a long blank banner and began, at once, to paint in tall gold letters: **DRAGONS BY THE JUBILEE**.

A clerk slid a contract across the oak table to the Chamberlain. It was beautifully lettered, with a red seal and a ribbon. The Chamberlain picked up a quill.

And above the contract, all the numbers settled into one last soft blue line, perfectly calm. Under it, smaller, the original one was still there, like the first line of a ledger:

**14 months, plus insurance** **(the dragon alone: 9 months)**

Walter looked at it. Then, without meaning to, he looked at the little estimate still hanging over the minute-book. It said **Time until the furniture has an opinion: 0 minutes**.

He put down his quill.

He stood up.

"It's nine months," he said.

The whole Great Hall went silent. The painters stopped mid-letter. The Chamberlain's quill hovered over the contract. Thirty faces turned toward the side table, where, as everybody knew, the furniture sat.

"Who," said the Chamberlain faintly, "is that?"

"*Wally!*" Brax laughed, a big warm laugh, and came across the floor to clap Walter on the shoulder in exactly the place Trent always had. "Our minute-taker! Very thorough. Love the energy." His fingers tightened, very slightly.

"Dealing with a dragon, from here, safely, with a party that hasn't been chosen yet," said Walter, "is nine months. The castle, the purple and the rides make it fourteen. If nothing goes wrong."

Lord Vayle turned. He looked down at Walter from his great height, with enormous kindness, as a father might look at a small son who has said something charmingly wrong about the moon. He laid one large hand on Walter's other shoulder.

"Son," said the Guildmaster, in his cathedral voice. "Nine months is a *mindset*."

Behind him, very softly, Brother Tobiah murmured, "We do not speak of time." Against the wall, Director Hesper did not move at all, but somewhere in her office, four floors up, Walter was sure a second little bell had started to ring.

The Chamberlain looked at Walter. He looked at the Guildmaster. He looked at the half-painted banner in the corner, at the gold letters spelling out DRAGONS BY THE, and he signed.

The moment the ink touched the parchment, Brax produced a small velvet bag from his cape, held it out, and a royal page filled it with gold coins from a strongbox. Brax counted them, right there at the table, without any embarrassment at all.

"Commission," he explained to the Chamberlain cheerfully. "Paid on signing. Industry standard."

Walter sat back down at the side table. He picked up his quill. After a moment, he wrote:

> *10:51. Contract signed. Thirty days.* *10:51. Minute-taker stated: nine months.*

He looked up. Across the hall, at the great doors, Ysolde hadn't moved a muscle. Her spear was still grounded, and her face was as still as the stone. But she was looking at him.

---

The memo was waiting on Walter's desk when he got back to Quest Intake. It had also, he soon discovered, been pinned to every noticeboard in the guild, slid under every door and read aloud by a herald in the refectory.

> **FROM: Office of the Guildmaster** **ANNOUNCEMENT** Sir Harlowe, Senior Party Lead and designated Lead of the Grand Quest (*Dragons by the Jubilee*), has left the Guild to pursue other opportunities. We thank him for his many contributions and wish him every success in his next chapter. *Synergy Through Valor.*

Walter read it twice. He had seen this memo before. He'd seen it at SynergyStack, and at the two companies before SynergyStack. The names changed. The memo never did. *Left to pursue other opportunities.* *Next chapter.* It was the corporate way of saying that someone had gone through a door and nobody was going to talk about which door.

"What actually happened to him?" he asked.

Wren looked around the scriptorium to make sure nobody was listening. Then she leaned close and whispered.

"They sent him down to the lower hall this morning. Right after the Ritual. To get the old dragon records out of the Backlog. From the last time." She lowered her voice even more. "Eighty years ago. When the guild tried Mount Kessel before."

"And?"

"And he read the plaque out loud," Wren whispered. "*Please do not lean.* To show respect. He has a very big voice." She paused. "And while he was reading it, he leaned."

"He had the records with him," she added, even more quietly. "All of them. The whole box."

They went down to the lower hall together. A small crowd had gathered at the iron railing around the Backlog, the way crowds gather at a hole in the road. Somebody had already chalked a neat outline on the floor where Sir Harlowe had last been standing. Across the hall, a clerk with a screwdriver was taking the brass nameplate off Sir Harlowe's office door.

Warm air rose out of the pit, smelling of old paper. And with it, very faint, booming up out of the dark from a great distance, came a voice that Walter recognized immediately.

"—and the *third* meeting, which I want to say was really *very* well run, opened with a review of the minutes of the *second* meeting—"

There was a pause. Then a second voice answered from the depths. It was wet and bright and cheerful, and Walter would have known it anywhere.

"Status open?"

"Not now," boomed Sir Harlowe. "I'm still on yesterday."

Walter gripped the railing. "Is that *Gerald*?"

"Who's Gerald?" said Wren.

"Shouldn't somebody go down there and get him? Get them?"

Wren looked at him with great kindness and pointed to the memo pinned on the nearest pillar. "He's left to pursue other opportunities," she said. "It says so."

A porter, to his credit, did lower a long rope over the railing. Everyone waited. After a while there was a tug, and then Sir Harlowe's voice, rather offended, floated up the rope:

"I'll come up when I've *finished my update*."

The porter pulled the rope back up. It was empty. On the end of it, someone down there had tied a very small, very neat bow.

Above the pit, the blue estimate drifted into view:

**Time until Sir Harlowe finishes his update: 6 days**

Walter was still looking at it when a runner from the fourth floor touched his elbow.

"Junior Scribe Dunleavy? Director Gallowmere will see you now."

The way to the fourth floor led past the main courtyard. It was noon.

In the courtyard, on the gravel, sat two hundred adventurers in long straight rows, one behind another, each holding an invisible oar. At the front, on an upturned barrel, stood a red-faced instructor from Learning & Enrichment with a whistle.

"*Stroke!*" he bellowed. "*Stroke!* And — *man overboard!*"

Two hundred adventurers leaned dutifully to the left and peered at the gravel. Grenn rowed with grave, steady dignity, his one eye fixed on a horizon that wasn't there. Sir Godric rowed in full plate, clanking at every stroke. Brennic the troll rowed perfectly, in time, with excellent form, and was the only one who had brought a life jacket, which he had made himself.

On the stable roof, the Enrichment Owl watched it all with deep satisfaction. As Walter hurried past, it turned its head all the way round to follow him, and he knew, with total certainty, that his absence had been noted.

---

There were two bells ringing in Director Hesper Gallowmere's office now.

They rang on their little brass stands, out of time with each other, a faint, steady, maddening *ting… ting… ting* that filled the room the way a dripping tap fills a house at night. The charts on the walls still climbed gracefully upward. There was a new one, Walter noticed, freshly inked, titled **DRAGONS BY THE JUBILEE — PROJECTED VELOCITY**. Its line went straight up and off the top of the page.

Hesper stood at the window with her back to him. She didn't sit down. Walter was beginning to suspect she never did.

"Close the door," she said.

He closed it. The bells went on ringing.

She turned around. Her face was as sharp and composed as ever, every hair in place. But her eyes looked as if she had been awake for a very long time, and had spent some of that time thinking about one question, over and over, without ever being allowed to ask it.

She asked it now.

"Is it really nine months?"

"Nine months," said Walter. "If nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?"

"Something always goes wrong."

Hesper closed her eyes. She stood like that for a moment, very straight, with the bells ringing behind her.

"How do you *know*?" she said.

Walter thought about how to answer. He thought about Gerald, and Velloria, and the glowing blue numbers that nobody else could see.

"I can see it," he said finally. "I've always been able to see it. It's the only thing I've ever been good at."

He waited for her to laugh. Brax would have. The Guildmaster would have put a hand on his shoulder and called it a mindset. But Hesper didn't laugh. She looked at him the way a sailor looks at a man who claims he can smell a storm: not quite believing, but not quite willing to bet against it either.

"I have seven people above me," she said quietly. She nodded at the little row of bells. "One for each. Every one of them will come to me on the thirty-first day and ask why there is no dragon. I can't tell them nine months. Do you know what happens to Directors who say nine months?"

"They leave to pursue other opportunities?"

"They go into the Backlog," said Hesper. "Some of them on purpose."

One of the bells rang twice in quick succession, as if it agreed.

"Sir Harlowe was the Lead," she went on. "Now there's no Lead. The Guildmaster wants a name on the quest by sundown. Any name." She looked at Walter steadily. "I want a name that knows the real number. So here's my offer. You'll be Party Lead of the Grand Quest. You'll pick your own party. You'll plan it however you like. And if it fails—" she spread one hand, very slightly "—it's on you. And if it works, it's on my chart." She nodded at the wall. "That's the deal."

Walter looked at her for a long moment.

"Why would I take that deal?"

"Because if you don't," said Hesper, "the name will be someone who thinks it's thirty days."

Walter thought about the farm boys, painting the name of a monster on a shield before they'd seen it. He thought about Sir Percival, asking why he would read a quest. He thought about who else in the building might be handed a dragon and a deadline and a smile.

He could feel the old words rising in his throat, warm and familiar and easy. *Sure. I'll take a look.*

He didn't say them.

"Yes," he said.

It was a different word. It felt different, too. It felt like something chosen.

Hesper nodded once. Then she reached for a quill and wrote one line on a slip of paper, signed it and held it out. As he took it, she spoke again, almost too quietly to hear under the bells.

"There's one more thing. The Guildmaster has also assigned the Gilded Lances to the dragon."

Walter stared at her. "The same dragon?"

"For visibility," said Hesper. "Sir Percival will be in the Jubilee parade either way. The Guildmaster likes to have… options."

"So it's two parties. One dragon."

"Different quest numbers," said Hesper, and for just a second the corner of her mouth did something tired and wry that, on another face, might have been the beginning of a laugh. Then one of the bells rang, and it was gone.

At the door, she stopped him.

"Scribe."

He turned.

"Thank you," said Director Hesper Gallowmere. "For the number."

---

Mortimer Graves was so delighted that he dropped his reading glasses into his own rib cage and had to fish them out.

"Party Lead!" cried the lich, eye-lights blazing. "From Junior Scribe to Party Lead in eight days! A *record*!" He brought down his stamp on Walter's personnel file with a crash like a portcullis. **PARTY LEAD — GRAND QUEST.** Then he presented Walter with a brand-new lanyard, silver this time, with a slightly larger badge and a slightly less hopeless portrait.

Walter put it on. It hummed. The door of Mortimer's office swung open by itself.

Walter had time to feel one moment of pure, uncomplicated joy before the window rattled. Something round and smug landed on the sill, and a small scroll with a green seal dropped onto the desk.

> **LEARNING & ENRICHMENT** New role detected. **LEADERSHIP ESSENTIALS** (12 modules) is now mandatory. Lanyard privileges suspended pending completion.

The silver badge stopped humming. The door swung gently shut.

"Ah," said Mortimer, with deep sympathy. "Training lock."

---

Walter couldn't sleep.

He lay on his bunk in the dark barracks for a long time, listening to thirty people snore, with the notebook open on his chest. He had tried to make a plan. At the top of a fresh page he had written **GRAND QUEST** and underlined it twice, and then, under that, **PARTY:**, and then nothing at all. Above the empty page, all evening, the same blue number had hung in the air, patient and certain.

**9 months**

At about midnight he gave up, put on his boots and went out.

Or tried to. The barracks door did not open, because his brand-new silver lanyard was locked pending twelve modules of Leadership Essentials. He stood in front of it in the dark for some time, a Party Lead who couldn't leave his own bedroom. Eventually he noticed that someone, possibly a stable boy coming in late, had wedged the side door open with a boot. He decided that a boot wasn't a person, and that going through a door held open by a boot was therefore not tailgating. He went out.

The night was cool and clear. There was a light in the stables.

The first lantern, just inside the door, belonged to Sir Percival Brightshield.

He was packing too. He had his great white stallion in the nearest stall, and on a clean blanket in front of it he had laid out everything he meant to take to Mount Kessel. It was a single elegant leather bag, a silver hand mirror, a spare set of gilded spurs, and a small framed portrait of himself, "for morale." On a hay bale nearby, the court painter was asleep sitting up, still holding a brush.

"Party Lead!" Percival beamed, with real warmth, as if they were old friends. "Congratulations! I heard. We're both off to see the dragon, then." He snapped the leather bag shut. "Thirty days! Plenty of time. I'm told dragons are mostly about *attitude*."

Above the elegant bag, soft and blue, the numbers appeared:

**Supplies packed: 30 days. Incorrect.**

Walter opened his mouth to say something. Then he closed it again, because Percival was already holding the hand mirror up to the lantern and practicing a heroic expression, and there didn't seem to be anything to say.

Ysolde was there, at the far end of the long row of stalls, in a second pool of lantern-light. Her gray mare stood patiently beside her. Next to the mare was a second horse, a sturdy brown packhorse, already half loaded. And all around Ysolde, on the straw, in neat, careful piles, was more equipment than Walter had ever seen one person own.

There was a heavy winter cloak, lined with fur, though it was barely autumn. There were three spare pairs of boots. There were oilskins, a coil of rope, a coil of a different, thinner rope, a whetstone, a small iron pot, a sack of oats and a row of sealed clay jars, labeled in a small neat hand: *Apples. Apples. Pickled onions. Apples.* There was a thin leather book of ranger's maps, with a ribbon marking a page, and the page was headed **KESSEL**.

She was packing all of it. Carefully. Item by item. The way you pack when you mean to be gone for a very long time.

She didn't look up when he came in. "You're up late, Party Lead."

"So are you." Walter looked at the piles. At the winter cloak. At the jars of apples. "Going somewhere?"

"Mount Kessel."

"The quest's for thirty days."

"No, it isn't." Ysolde tucked a jar of pickled onions into a saddlebag, wrapped in a spare shirt so it wouldn't break. "Nine months, you said."

Walter stood very still.

In nineteen years in his old life, and eight days in this one, he had said the real number out loud to a great many people. To Trent. To managers. To Brother Tobiah, and to a Guildmaster, and to the King's own Chamberlain that very morning. Every one of them had smiled, or frowned, or put a hand on his shoulder, or burned it in a brazier, or called it a mindset. None of them had ever done the one thing a number was for, which was to plan around it.

She had. Without being asked. Before anyone had asked her to come.

"I haven't picked a party," he said. His voice came out strange.

"I know," said Ysolde. She kept packing.

"So you don't actually know you'll be on it."

She paused, with a pair of boots in her hands, and looked at him for the first time since he'd come in. It was the same steady look as in the corridor and on the ranger's road. It went straight through all the furniture and found the person behind it.

"You said the real number," she said, "in front of the Guildmaster. Nobody does that." She went back to the boots. "Nine months is a long time to be wrong about. I'd rather pack for it."

Walter sat down on a bale of hay, because his legs seemed to want him to. For a while he just watched her pack. The lantern hissed softly. The mare shifted her weight. The packhorse ate some of the oats when Ysolde wasn't looking, and she took them back without comment.

After a while, he opened the notebook on his knee. Under the entries from the past eight days, in the light from the lantern, he wrote:

> *14. Nine months. Someone believed it.*

Then he turned back to the empty page headed **GRAND QUEST**. Under **PARTY:**, he wrote a number one, and after it, a name.

> *1. Ysolde.*

He looked at it. It looked right. He closed the book.

When he looked up, Ysolde was buckling the last strap on the last saddlebag, and above the bulging, careful, absurdly complete pile of everything she owned, soft and blue in the lantern-light, one more estimate drifted into place. It was, Walter realized, the first one he had ever seen that was not a warning.

**Supplies packed: 9 months. Correct.**
