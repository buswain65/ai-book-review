# Chapter 8: Kickoff

> **TEAMWORK MAKES THE DREAM WORK** **(DREAM ASSIGNED SEPARATELY)**
>
> *— Banner over the Great Hall, newly hung*

When Walter woke on the first morning of the Grand Quest, there was a new number waiting for him in the gray light above his bunk.

**Days until Jubilee: 29**

It sat there quietly beside the older one, the one that had been there all night. That one simply said **9 months**, the way a mountain simply says *mountain*.

The Enrichment Owl was waiting for him at breakfast. It had saved him a seat.

> **LEARNING & ENRICHMENT — LEADERSHIP ESSENTIALS** **Module 1 of 12: Delegation: Assigning Accountability to Others**

The module was a short illuminated scroll in three panels. In the first, a cheerful Party Lead pointed at a task. In the second, he pointed at a smaller person standing beneath him. In the third, the task had caught fire, and the smaller person was holding it, and the Party Lead was standing a good distance away, smiling and giving a thumbs-up. Underneath was a quiz.

*When a quest fails, who is accountable?* *☐ The Party Lead ☐ The Party ☐ Both (the Party)*

Walter ticked a box at random, the way he had once clicked through every mandatory compliance module of his previous life, and gave the scroll back to the owl. The owl read it, looked at him for a long, flat moment, and made a small note on a second scroll attached to its other leg.

---

Director Hesper was in a meeting. Her office sent the headcount down by clerk.

The clerk was a young man in spectacles who read the allocation letter aloud in the corridor, slowly, like a doctor delivering results.

> **GRAND QUEST — PARTY ALLOCATION** **Approved headcount: 2.5 adventurers.** *(Formula: Valor budget ÷ standard adventurer cost × synergy coefficient.)*

"Two and a half," said Walter.

"Yes."

"Which half?"

The clerk looked at the letter carefully, as though the answer might be in the small print. "Whichever half you like," he said at last.

He turned the page. To save time, he explained, the Office of Questing had already prepared a recommended party, "pre-selected for fit."

> **RECOMMENDED PARTY:** **Sir Godric** — experienced; stands well. **Lysander** (bard) — for morale and documentation. **Quentin Vane-Whitlock, Synergy Officer** — *(has not been seen since his hiring; believed to be in the building)*.

Walter thought about Sir Godric toppling over in full plate on frosty gravel. He thought about Lysander's ballads, which always had a head on a pole at the end, whether or not there'd been a monster. He thought about a Synergy Officer who had been successfully avoiding the guild for some unknown length of time, and felt a brief flash of something like professional admiration.

"No," said Walter. "Thank you."

The clerk looked distressed. "You must stay within two and a half."

"I will."

Walter took out his notebook and turned to the page headed **PARTY**. Under *1. Ysolde*, he had added three more names. Two of them had come from Wren, over a honey cake, the night before: "If you want something broken properly, ask Gorrum. If you want something old read, ask Dorrit." He hadn't met either of them yet. He wrote the guild's own numbers beside each one, and then the total, and held it out.

> *Walter (Party Lead) — 1* *Ysolde — 1* *Wren — 0.5 (interns are half-weighted)* *Gorrum — 0 (Order of Testers defunded; does not officially exist)* *Dorrit — 0 (on loan; still billed to Maintenance)* *TOTAL: 2.5*

The clerk read it. He read it again. He took a small brass abacus from his pocket and checked it, bead by bead, with his lips moving. Then he looked up at Walter with something close to awe.

"It balances," he whispered.

"It does."

The clerk stamped it on the spot. **APPROVED.** Hesper's office, Walter learned later, approved it without comment. He suspected that somebody up there had looked at the math for a long time and decided not to ask.

---

Ysolde's transfer needed only one signature, from her manager, the Coordinator of the Ranger Corps. His office was a cluttered room over the stables that smelled of leather and wet dog.

The Coordinator was a cheerful, distracted man with a large mustache and a ledger the size of a door. He read the transfer request. He frowned at it.

"Ysolde," he said. "Ysolde. Do we have a Ysolde?"

He opened the ledger and began to search. He went through the A's. He went through the R's, for *Ranger*. He went through a section labeled *Promotions Pending* that was clearly very old and full of names with small skulls beside them. Finally, near the very back, on a page by itself, he found her. It was under a heading that said **MISCELLANEOUS**, and next to her name, in faded ink, someone had once written a single word: *Excellent.*

"Ah," said the Coordinator, pleased. "There she is." He signed the transfer without asking who she was, blotted it and handed it over. "Good luck with her. Excellent, apparently."

"She is," said Walter.

---

"I can't," said Wren.

She said it brightly, the way she said everything, but she didn't look up from the shelf she was dusting. She was in the Library, shelving blank books marked TODO, which she had been doing, she said, since the shelves were put in.

"Interns aren't allowed on quests." She pointed with her duster at a small brass plaque by the door that Walter had never noticed: **INTERNS MAY NOT ACCOMPANY QUESTS (NOT COVERED BY GUILD INSURANCE)**. "I've asked. Every year. A hundred and forty times." She smiled. "Every year they say next year."

Walter looked at the plaque for a while. Then he remembered something: a painted chart on a standing stone, in a cold courtyard, and a little card with a man in motley stepping off a cliff.

"Wren," he said, "where would Brother Tobiah be right now?"

---

He was in a corridor, of course. Between the second and third floors, drawing cards and writing numbers on quests in passing, like a man feeding ducks.

"Brother," said Walter, "would you estimate the Grand Quest for me? For efficiency."

Brother Tobiah looked up. His long, mild face lit up with a pure, startled happiness that Walter wasn't prepared for.

"Nobody *asks*," he said softly. "They just… let me."

He shuffled the great gilt-edged deck with reverent hands, three times, cut it, and drew. He turned the card over.

On it, a young man in a jester's motley strolled cheerfully toward the edge of a cliff, a small dog at his heels and a bundle on a stick over his shoulder, looking up at the sky instead of at his feet.

The Fool.

Tobiah stared at it. All the color drained slowly out of his face, as if out of a bucket with a hole in it.

"The Fool," he said, "means *assign to the intern*."

"Yes," said Walter. "I remember."

"But interns may not accompany quests." Tobiah looked at the card. He looked at Walter. "It is not covered by *insurance*."

"The cards have spoken," said Walter gently.

It was, he could see, the hardest moment of Brother Tobiah's professional life. The rules said one thing. The Ritual said another. And Tobiah, for all his hourglasses and his gentle tyranny, really did believe in the Ritual more than any god. His lips moved silently. He seemed to be consulting something deep inside himself. At one point he looked at a nearby candle as if he were thinking of releasing the entire situation into it.

Then he drew a long breath, took out his small silver pencil, and wrote on the back of the card, in a careful, trembling hand:

*THE FOOL. Assigned: Intern (Wren). The cards have spoken.*

He signed it, and drew a tiny hourglass beside his name.

"And the Valor?" said Walter.

"The Fool," said Tobiah faintly, "has no number." He handed over the card as if it were very hot. "The quest is… unestimated."

Walter thought that this was the most accurate estimate the guild had produced all week.

---

Wren read the card three times, in the Library, standing between two tall shelves of TODO.

She didn't say anything. Walter saw her hands begin to shake, a little at first and then a lot, and the brass badge under her sash, stamped a hundred and forty times with **RENEWED**, began to rattle. It made a small, steady, tinny sound in the quiet room, like rain on a tin roof.

"A hundred and forty years," she said. Her voice had gone very small. "I packed a bag, my first year. For my first quest. I still have it." She laughed, a strange, wet, wobbly laugh. "It's under my bunk. The bread's gone a bit hard."

Then she was crying. Not delicately, not elegantly, but all at once, the way people cry when something they had stopped letting themselves hope for turns up anyway. She held the card against her chest with both hands, and cried, and laughed, and cried, and the badge rattled and rattled.

Walter looked up above her head, half afraid of what he'd see.

On his second day in the guild, in a corridor, he had watched the blue text try to form there and fail, and flicker, and give up, as if the universe had opened the question and quietly closed it again. Now the letters came all at once, clear and bright and steady, the way they did for everything else.

**Time to first quest: 0 days**

"Wren," said Walter. "Go and get your bag."

She hugged him so hard that a TODO book fell off the shelf behind them and landed open on the floor. It was blank, of course. But for the first time Walter could remember, it didn't look like a list of things nobody would ever do. It looked like room.

---

The Chapel of the Order of Testers was at the end of a long, cold corridor in the second cellar, past the laundry and a door marked **SPARE BANNERS**. There were two signs on its door. The first was carved into the wood, very old and very fine: **THE ORDER OF TESTERS — WE FIND IT SO YOU DON'T**. The second was a piece of parchment nailed underneath it, newer and already yellow: **DEFUNDED. PLEASE DO NOT DISTURB.** Under that, in smaller writing, someone had added: *(Nobody will.)*

Walter knocked. Nobody answered. Inside, faintly, something went *tink*.

He went in.

It had been a real chapel once: long and vaulted, with pale stone pillars and a high round window full of dusty colored glass. The pews were gone. In their place were rows and rows of tall wooden racks, and on the racks, neatly arranged, were broken things.

There were swords and shields, helmets and boots, lanterns and maps, potions in little glass bottles and coils of rope. Every single item had a small paper tag tied to it with string, and on every tag, in tiny, careful handwriting, was a note.

*Longsword. BROKE: 3 WAYS (see ledger).*

*Shield, kite. BROKE: on Tuesdays.*

*Potion of Healing. BROKE: heals the person standing nearest, not the drinker.*

*Map of the Northern Marches. BROKE: north is wrong.*

At the far end, where the altar had once been, stood a wooden door frame on a stand, with a brand-new door hanging in it, the kind of door that opened for lanyards. In front of it, with his back to Walter, was the largest person Walter had ever seen.

He was a half-orc, broad as a cart, wearing a monk's robe of patched gray wool. His head was shaved, and his skin was a deep olive green. From his lower jaw rose two gray tusks, each capped neatly with a small silver band. Spectacles hung round his neck on a chain. He was holding a little brass hammer in one huge hand and a lanyard badge in the other, and he was tapping the badge against the lock of the door, very gently, in different places, listening to it. Every so often he stopped and wrote something in a ledger.

*Tink.*

"Excuse me," said Walter. "Are you Gorrum?"

The half-orc turned. Up close, his face was craggy and scarred and, unexpectedly, very kind. He put on his spectacles and looked at Walter over the top of them, the way a librarian looks at someone who has asked a genuinely interesting question.

"I am," he said. His voice was deep, soft and precise. "Last Abbot of the Order of Testers. Also the first. Also, currently, the entire Order." He gestured at the racks. "We used to test quests before they went out. We walked the route. We read the maps. We poked the lair with sticks. We asked the client the stupid questions." He sighed. "Quests failed less. But it was felt that testing slowed velocity. So the Order was defunded, and the guild decided that adventurers could test their own quests."

"How's that going?"

Gorrum gestured at a rack near the door. It was labeled **RETURNED FROM THE FIELD** and was very, very full.

His eyes went to Walter's chest. The little red light was still glowing in the middle of Walter's silver badge.

"Training lock," said Gorrum. "May I?"

Walter held out the lanyard. Gorrum took the badge between two enormous, careful fingers, looked at it closely, and turned it upside down.

The red light went green. Behind him, the test door swung open by itself.

"Flaw," said Gorrum. "Hold it upside down and it thinks you've completed the training." He wrote a note in the ledger. "I reported it this morning."

"To who?"

"To the Order." Gorrum closed the ledger. "Which is me. It's been noted."

Walter found that he was smiling. It was a strange feeling. He had once worked with someone who broke everything he built, and was always right, and kept a bug-shaped stress ball on her desk. On the altar behind Gorrum, he noticed, next to the ledger, there was a small stone carved into the shape of a beetle.

He told Gorrum about the Grand Quest. He opened his notebook and showed him the nine months, and the thirty days, and the dragon, and the contract that said *deal with*.

Gorrum read all of it. He read it slowly, all the way to the end, moving his lips slightly, the way Walter suspected he read everything. Then he took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve and put them back on.

"And what happens," said Gorrum, "if the dragon isn't home?"

Walter opened his mouth. Then he closed it.

"And what happens if he says *no*?" Gorrum turned a page. "And what happens—" he tapped the notebook gently with the end of his hammer "—if he says *yes*?"

"Yes to what?"

"Exactly." Gorrum looked at him over the spectacles. "Yes to *what*. The contract says *deal with*. Nobody has decided what you're asking him for. You can't test a quest if nobody knows what it's for."

Walter thought of a scroll that had said *Deal with the thing in the well*, and a crank called a windlass, and an old woman who couldn't remember the word for it.

Gorrum was still going, gently and relentlessly, turning pages. "And what happens if the Gilded Lances get there first? And what happens if the castle is occupied? And what happens if it rains? Dragons are very particular about rain. And what happens if—"

Above the ledger, as he spoke, the blue numbers began to count quietly upward, like a tally kept by an invisible clerk:

**Risks identified: 41. Risks previously identified by the guild: 0.**

"Gorrum," said Walter. "Will you come?"

The half-orc stopped. He looked at the racks of broken things, all tagged and noted and filed away where nobody would ever read them. Then he looked at Walter for a long moment, and something moved in his craggy face that Walter recognized, because he had felt it himself in a stable the night before.

"Nobody," said Gorrum quietly, "has let me break a quest in eleven years."

He tucked the little brass hammer into his belt, picked up the ledger and the beetle-shaped stone, and put them in his pocket.

"I'll need to bring the hammer," he said.

---

Maintenance was below everything else.

You reached it by a narrow spiral stair that went down past the cellars, past the wine store, past a locked iron door with no sign on it at all, and down into a long, warm, low-roofed hall that smelled of lamp oil, hot metal and very old stone. Pipes ran along the ceiling, ticking and gurgling. Here and there, set deep into the walls, carved symbols glowed with a faint, steady amber light, like coals that had been banked for the night and then forgotten for a few centuries.

Walter didn't need to be told what they were. He could feel them, a low hum in his back teeth. The Founding Wards.

Above the nearest one, quietly, the blue numbers appeared:

**Age: 812 years. Documentation: none.**

At a workbench at the far end, under the brightest of the glowing symbols, sat an old dwarf.

He was short and wide and solid, like a stump that had decided to sit up. His white beard was so long that it had been braided into three thick plaits and tucked neatly into a broad leather belt. He wore a scorched leather apron, and he was oiling a brass valve the size of a dinner plate with a rag and a great deal of concentration. On the wall above the bench hung a calendar. On the calendar, a single word, **RETIRE**, had been written on a date, crossed out, and written again on the same date in the following month. Then crossed out again. Then written again. It had happened so many times that the page had gone soft and gray and furry with ink, and the calendar had clearly been replaced many times, each new one starting where the last had stopped.

"Dorrit Ironsbane?" said Walter.

"Next month," said the dwarf, without looking up.

"Sorry?"

"Whatever it is. I'm retiring next month." He rubbed at the valve. "Have been for forty years. Make it quick."

Walter made it quick. The old dragon records, from the guild's last attempt on Mount Kessel. Sir Rakkan's notes, scattered all over the building. They were all written in Cobold, the old kobold tongue that nobody spoke anymore, and Wren said Dorrit was the only one left who could read it.

At the word *Kessel*, Dorrit stopped rubbing the valve.

He looked up for the first time. His eyes were small and dark and very sharp under heavy white brows, and they went over Walter carefully, the way a craftsman looks over a tool someone has handed him, wondering whether it will break.

"No," said a voice behind Walter.

It belonged to a small, harassed gnome with a clipboard, a pencil behind each ear, and the hollow look of a man who manages the one employee he cannot afford to lose. His badge said **TIBBET GRINDLE — FOREMAN OF MAINTENANCE**.

"Absolutely not," said Foreman Grindle. "Dorrit doesn't leave the building. If Dorrit leaves the building, who turns the Thirteenth Valve at midnight?"

Walter looked at the brass valve on the workbench. "What does the Thirteenth Valve do?"

Foreman Grindle opened his mouth, and then, slowly, closed it again. He looked at Dorrit.

"Nobody knows," said Dorrit, with some satisfaction. "Forty years ago I had the flu. Missed a night." He went back to oiling. "The east wing moved."

"*Moved?*"

"About a foot. To the left." Dorrit shrugged. "Took us a week to push it back."

In the end they came to an agreement. They did it the guild way, which meant nobody got what they wanted and everybody signed something. Dorrit would be on loan to the Grand Quest for reading, consulting and general grumbling, but only "until you leave the walls." Once the party passed the main gate, he was Maintenance's again, and the Thirteenth Valve would be turned at midnight as it always had been.

"I don't leave the walls," said Dorrit, as Foreman Grindle hurried off with the signed form. "The walls don't leave me."

Walter turned to go. But at the foot of the spiral stair, Dorrit's voice stopped him.

"Scribe."

Walter looked back. The old dwarf was still bent over the valve. He didn't look up.

"Eighty years ago, they asked me to read those dragon records too," Dorrit said. "Before the last lot went up the mountain." He rubbed at a spot of tarnish that wasn't there. "Nobody listened then, either."

"What did the records say?"

Dorrit was quiet for a moment.

"Get 'em out of the Backlog," he said, "and I'll read 'em to you. Then you'll know."

Above the bent white head and the three neat braids and the brass valve, the blue numbers formed, steady and calm and faintly ominous, like a sign on a bridge:

**Wagon factor: 1**

---

The kickoff was held that afternoon in the Lesser Hall, which was only slightly smaller than the Great Hall and had more banners.

At the front, behind a long table draped in purple, sat six managers. At the back, on a single wooden bench against the wall, sat four adventurers.

Walter had been given a chair at the end of the managers' table, with a little card that said **PARTY LEAD (LEADERSHIP)**. He looked at the card. He looked at the bench at the back, where Ysolde sat very straight, and Wren was holding a canvas bag a hundred and forty years old on her knees, and Gorrum had his ledger open, and Dorrit had his arms folded and his eyes closed. He picked up his chair and moved it halfway down the hall, and sat in the middle, belonging to neither end. Several managers noticed. Nobody said anything. Somewhere four floors up, Walter was fairly sure, a bell rang.

Above the hall, before anyone had spoken, the numbers appeared:

**Managers: 6. Adventurers: 4. Questions asked of adventurers: 0.**

Each of the managers had brought a kickoff deliverable.

Pell Odwyn went first. He unveiled a large painted board on an easel with a flourish of his scarf. It showed a purple dragon, smiling, wearing a small crown and holding a bunch of balloons. Across the top, in gold paint, was one word: **IMAGINE**. "It's a *vision board*," Pell explained, glowing. "It's not a plan. It's a *feeling*."

Madam Quickthorn went next. She snapped her fingers, and two weavers from the guild workshop carried in a long rolled bundle and unfurled it across the floor of the hall. It was a tapestry, twelve feet long, woven overnight. It was beautiful. Thirty columns ran across it, one for each day, and colored threads ran through the columns, showing who would be doing what, and when. Day 1: *Depart.* Days 2 to 28: *Travel / Slay.* Day 29, in bright red thread: *Dragon Dies.* Day 30, in gold: *Castle Conversion & Jubilee.*

Above it, very calmly, Walter's estimate read:

**Accuracy: decorative**

Fennimore of Communications & Tone was next. He was wearing even more brooches than usual. He revealed a small painted shield showing a purple sun rising over a golden lance. "The quest's *brand*," he said. "We workshopped *Operation Dragonslayer*, but it tested as a bit negative. So." He spread his hands. "*Operation Purple Dawn.*"

Brother Tobiah shuffled his great deck, drew a card for the whole Grand Quest and turned it over with reverence. It was The Tower. "Thirteen," he announced, with deep satisfaction. "About thirteen days."

"This morning," said Walter, "it was The Fool."

Tobiah went slightly pale. "This morning," he said, with dignity, "was for *efficiency*. This is the *Ritual*."

Brax Hollister reported, with enormous enthusiasm, that the client was thrilled, absolutely thrilled. The King had also requested a dragon-shaped cake for the Jubilee banquet.

"Made *of* the dragon?" said Walter.

"Wally! Love the energy." Brax pointed at him with two fingers. "The royal pastry chef will need the dragon's measurements. Put that on the list."

The sixth manager was Director Hesper Gallowmere. She stood against the wall behind the table, as always, and said nothing at all.

At the back of the hall, for the whole of the kickoff, Gorrum had held his hand up. He held it patiently, steadily, the way a tree holds a branch. At one point Madam Quickthorn noticed it.

"Yes? Large gentleman at the back?"

"And what happens," said Gorrum, "if—"

"*Thank* you!" said Madam Quickthorn warmly. "Great energy." And she moved on to the next item.

Gorrum's hand stayed up.

At the end, Walter stood. He walked to the front, between the vision board and the tapestry, opened his notebook and told them. Nine months for the dragon. Fourteen with the castle, the purple and the rides. They would need to find out what the dragon actually was, and what he actually wanted, before anyone went near him with a lance. He said it clearly and simply, the way he'd said it to Hesper, and the Chamberlain, and the Guildmaster. He was getting better at saying it. Nobody was getting better at hearing it.

There was a polite silence.

"All in favor," said Madam Quickthorn, "of the tapestry?"

Five hands went up. Hesper's didn't, Walter noticed. But it didn't go down against it, either. It stayed exactly where it was, at her side.

"Adopted," said Madam Quickthorn. "By acclamation." She turned to Walter with a brisk smile. "The Party Lead will carry the plan with them at all times. For alignment." And the two weavers rolled the twelve-foot tapestry back up and put it into Walter's arms.

It weighed about as much as Wren.

Through the open double doors at the side of the hall, from the Great Hall next door, came a sudden burst of music. There were drums, and trumpets, and the pop of something that might have been a confetti cannon. Walter caught a glimpse of gold and white: the Gilded Lances' kickoff, in full swing, with a band. Sir Percival was standing on a table, waving. He saw Walter in the doorway, holding his rolled-up plan, and gave him a big, warm, generous thumbs-up.

---

That night, they met in the stables.

Walter hadn't called it a meeting. He'd just said "stables, after dinner," to each of them, and they'd come. Ysolde was already there when he arrived. Wren arrived with her ancient bag over her shoulder, as if she might be leaving at any moment. Gorrum ducked in under the doorway with his ledger and his hammer. Dorrit came last, grumbling about the hour, the straw and his knees, and announced that he had to be back down in Maintenance by a quarter to midnight for the Valve.

Walter unrolled Madam Quickthorn's tapestry on the straw.

Then he turned it over.

The back of a tapestry is where you see how it was actually made. It was all loose ends and knots and tangles, threads crossing where they shouldn't, colors jumping from one side to the other with no pattern at all. Every neat line on the front was a mess on the back. It was, Walter thought, the most honest picture of a guild plan he'd ever seen.

He took a stick of chalk from his pocket and knelt down on the rough back of the tapestry, and then he looked up at the four of them and did something nobody in the Grand Consolidated Adventurers' Guild of Waterfell had done all day.

"What do you think?" he said.

There was a silence. It was not the polite kind. It was the surprised kind. He watched it go across each of their faces in turn. Ysolde's barely moved. Wren's was wide-eyed. Gorrum's eyebrows went all the way up over his spectacles. Dorrit opened one eye.

None of them, Walter realized, had been asked that question in a very long time. Maybe ever.

Then Gorrum opened his ledger. "Fifty-eight," he said. "Risks. Since this morning." He began to read them out, and Walter began to write them on the back of the tapestry in chalk.

Ysolde knelt down beside him and unrolled her leather book of ranger's maps to the page marked **KESSEL**. She traced a route with one finger. "Six weeks by the road. Each way. Four, if the High Pass is open. It closes when the snow comes." She looked at him. "The snow comes in nine weeks."

Wren, it turned out, knew where everything in the guild was kept. She knew where the real rations store was, not the one on the map. She knew that behind the Grand Roadmap, under its glass dome, there was a drawer full of proper maps that nobody had opened in fifty years. She knew where the spare keys to the Library hung, and which of the stable boys could be trusted, and where the quartermaster hid the good rope. "I've had a lot of time," she explained, a little shyly, "to notice things."

And Dorrit, when they had all finished, unfolded his arms.

"The records," he said. "From last time. Eighty years back." He jerked his head toward the main hall. "They're at the bottom of the Backlog. With your Sir Harlowe."

Walter thought of the small blue estimate hanging over the pit. *Time until Sir Harlowe finishes his update.* It had said six days, the day before yesterday.

"Then we go down," he said.

"And what happens," said Gorrum, "if we can't get back up?"

Wren raised her hand. "I know where the good rope is."

At a quarter to midnight, exactly, Dorrit stood up, brushed the straw off his apron and went to turn the Valve. The rest of them stayed until the lantern burned low, working on the back of the tapestry, until the chalk was a stub and the knotted, tangled canvas was covered from edge to edge in writing. There were risks, routes, rations and questions. Most of it was questions. It was the first plan in the history of the guild that had been written from the back.

Walter opened his notebook and added a line.

> *15. Ask the people doing the work.*

Above the tangled underside of Operation Purple Dawn, soft and blue in the lantern-light, the numbers settled into place:

**Plan: 9 months.** **Plans written by the people who will do them: 1.**
