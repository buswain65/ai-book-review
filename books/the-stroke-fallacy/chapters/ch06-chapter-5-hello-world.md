### Chapter 5: Hello World

<br/>

The merchant's cart groaned rhythmically as its un-suspended wooden wheels ate up the miles of rutted dirt road heading south. Pine needles blanketed the trail, dulling the clatter of the draft horse's hooves, while the late afternoon sun filtered through the dense canopy in golden, hazy bars.

<br/>

Mark sat wedged between two burlap sacks of grain in the back of the wagon, staring down at his smooth, fourteen-year-old hands.

<br/>

In software engineering, before you build a sprawling enterprise architecture—or attempt to refactor a legacy codebase that three generations of developers have held together with duct tape and prayers—you write a tiny, foolproof script. You print `"Hello, World!"` to the console. You don't try to build a 3D graphics rendering engine on day one; you just verify that the compiler is live, the environment is responding, and your inputs produce a predictable output.

<br/>

Mark closed his eyes and began to tune his awareness inward.

<br/>

He deliberately avoided the grand, dramatic, mystic meditation postures taught in bad fantasy novels. He didn't cross his legs into an intimidating lotus position or chant pseudo-spiritual mantras. Instead, he defaulted to what his physical body already knew: the diaphragmatic breathwork from his decades of Kung Fu forms and Jiu-Jitsu sparring.

<br/>

Inhale through the nose for four counts, expand the abdomen, hold for two, exhale smoothly through the mouth.

<br/>

Within three breathing cycles, his heart rate slowed. The sensory noise of the forest faded into background hum. His perception of his own body sharpened with unnerving precision—the exact distribution of his weight against the wooden cart floorboards, the subtle tension in his rhomboids, the warm, oxygenated blood pulsing through his youthful capillaries.

<br/>

And then, just beneath his skin, he felt it.

<br/>

It wasn't a divine revelation or a chorus of celestial angels. It felt remarkably like static electricity—the faint, prickling buzz you get on your forearms when you stand too close to an ancient CRT monitor or touch a metal doorknob after dragging your socks across a shag carpet on a dry winter day in Raleigh. The air around the cart was practically dripping with it.

<br/>

*"The ambient Aether,"* Mark noted silently, his brain treating it with the emotional detachment of an electrician inspecting a breaker box. "Background voltage. A high-density electromagnetic or bio-field background layer."

<br/>

He opened his eyes, reached down into the dirt on the cart floor, and picked up a smooth, gray river pebble about the size of a quarter. Holding it loosely between his right thumb and index finger, he decided to compile his first local program.

<br/>

He didn't chant pseudo-Latin. He didn't make dramatic finger-gestures or picture a raging inferno. He treated the static prickle in his chest like an electrical fluid, channeling a tiny, micro-ampere droplet of it down his arm, past his elbow, through his forearm flexors, and straight into the molecular lattice of the stone.

<br/>

Target output: Execute thermal transfer. Raise stone temperature by five degrees Fahrenheit.

<br/>

For three seconds, absolutely nothing happened.

<br/>

Mark adjusted his mental alignment, focusing on the concept of thermal friction—the mental image he used to warm his icy fingers after a bad winter flip on the Green River in his kayak.

<br/>

*Zap!*

<br/>

A tiny, brilliant azure spark popped between his thumb and the pebble. It delivered a sharp, nasty static shock that made his radial nerve jump violently, causing him to throw the pebble onto the cart floor with a loud curse.

<br/>

"Ow! Son of a—" Mark hissed, clutching his stinging thumb and shaking his hand out wildly.

<br/>

Across the wagon, Vespera’s head snapped toward him so fast her hood fell back. Her sharp brown eyes locked onto the river pebble, which was currently resting on a burlap sack, emitting a faint, wispy curl of grey, foul-smelling smoke right where his thumb had touched it.

<br/>

Her expression shifted from bored indifference to sheer, unadulterated, jaw-dropping shock.

<br/>

"What in the name of the First Mages did you just do?" she demanded, her voice dropping an octave into pure suspicion.

<br/>

"Failed execution," Mark muttered grumpily, sucking on his sore thumb. "Voltage spiked on the output line. The impedance was way off. I need a better resistor."

<br/>

Vespera slid across the wooden bench, leaning over him like a hawk inspecting a weird suspicious mouse, her carved wooden staff held tight in her right hand. "Did you just draw raw Aether? Without a staff? Without a focus stone? Without speaking a single syllable of High Imperial?"

<br/>

"I was just testing the line!" Mark said defensively, holding his hands up. "I tried to transfer a tiny, controlled amount of thermal energy into the rock. Got a static discharge and a mild nerve sting instead. Standard hardware error."

<br/>

"You could have scorched your own nerve pathways, you unfathomably foolish child!" Vespera scolded, though her reprimand was thoroughly undermined by the tremor of utter disbelief in her voice. "Aether is not a toy to be poked with your bare fingers! The spoken incantation acts as the vessel—the boundary—to shape and isolate the force! Without the vessel, the energy has nowhere to go, so it grounds itself straight through *you*!"

<br/>

Mark’s eyes lit up instantly. The pain in his thumb vanished beneath the intoxicating rush of an engineer diagnosing a system defect.

<br/>

*"A grounding issue,"* Mark thought, a triumphant grin breaking out. "It’s an ungrounded circuit!"

<br/>

"So that's why you carry that giant broomstick?" Mark asked, pointing directly at the carved wooden staff in her hand. "It's an external conductor? An antenna to offload the thermal feedback and static backlash so it doesn't cook your internal wiring?"

<br/>

Vespera opened her mouth to deliver a grand, sweeping lecture on the sacred traditions of the Mages' Guild, then abruptly stopped. Her brows knitted together in visible confusion as her brain desperately tried to parse his bizarre corporate-technical vocabulary.

<br/>

"It... it acts as a focus and an anchor, yes," she admitted slowly, sounding thoroughly annoyed that his weird explanation actually made physical sense. "A properly attuned conduit of refined ironwood or silver absorbs the residual backlash. But no one—no student in the centuries-old history of the Academies—simply *feels* the Aetheric flow on their first attempt without months of guided vocal chanting!"

<br/>

"It's just body awareness," Mark explained casually, waving his hand. "If you understand basic breath mechanics and nervous system control, feeling where the static gathers isn't that hard. It's like finding your center of gravity when someone tries to execute a hip-throw on you in Jiu-Jitsu."

<br/>

Vespera stared at him. "In what?"

<br/>

"Never mind," Mark said, leaning down to scoop the river pebble off the floorboards, turning it over to inspect the tiny, charred black spot his spark had left behind. "So, if I want to run a script—a spell—safely without turning my arm into a fried sausage, I have two choices. I either need an external conduit to ground the excess voltage, or I need to speak... err, *format* the syntax very precisely to bound the execution parameters."

<br/>

Vespera continued to stare at the skinny fourteen-year-old kid as if he had just sprouted three additional heads and started singing in ancient Elvish. "You are, without exception, the strangest child I have ever encountered in my entire life."

<br/>

"I get that a lot," Mark said. "Usually from product managers."

<br/>

Before Vespera could ask what a 'product manager' was, the cart's wooden frame creaked as the horse slowed its pace, and Garin’s booming, gravelly voice echoed back from the front driver's bench.

<br/>

"End of the line, folks! Oakhaven ahead!"

<br/>

Mark pushed himself up in the wagon, stepping over the grain sacks to look over the merchant's broad shoulder.

<br/>

Where the dense pine forest cleared, a sprawling, walled settlement sat nestled in a wide, river-carved valley. High timber palisade walls protected a bustling town of timber-framed houses, blacksmith chimneys billowing plumes of dark woodsmoke, and cobblestone avenues filled with moving crowds of travelers, merchants, and armored guards. Beyond the town, rolling green hills stretched toward towering, snow-capped mountain peaks in the far distance.

<br/>

A heavy wooden sign hung above the main gatehouse, lit by glowing glass lanterns that flickered not with oil flames, but with a soft, steady, cool blue light—Aether-powered streetlamps.

<br/>

Garin turned back to Mark with a wide, gap-toothed grin. "Welcome to civilization, kid. First round of cider is on me at *The Pine & Anvil*."

<br/>

Mark looked at the glowing blue lanterns, the heavy timber gates, and his own smooth, un-arthritic, undamaged hands. His previous 62-year-old life of corporate deadlines, decaying spinal discs, endless Slack notifications, and Brad's agile buzzwords felt a million miles away.

<br/>

He hopped down from the moving wagon as it rolled through the gates, landing lightly on his feet with an easy, springy grace that made him grin.

<br/>

*"Hello, World,"* Mark thought, rolling up his sleeves. "Let's see what else this runtime environment can do."
