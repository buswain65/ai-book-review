## Chapter 13: Capacitor Calibration & The Commercial Pipeline

<br/>

Mark started his morning shift at the armory forge with a double portion of breakfast from the inn's kitchen: a bowl of coarse barley porridge swimming in melted lard, two salted sausages, and three hard-boiled eggs.

<br/>

The cook, a broad woman with forearms like hams, watched him work through it with her hands on her hips.

<br/>

"Growing boy?" she asked.

<br/>

"Growing infrastructure," Mark said through a mouthful of egg.

<br/>

She decided not to ask any follow-up questions and went back to the kitchen.

<br/>

He didn't eat for taste; he ate like a technician topping off a diesel generator's fuel tank. Yesterday's metabolic crash had been a stark warning. If his fourteen-year-old body was going to act as the primary ignition switch for channeling ambient Aether, he needed calories in the system.

<br/>

On his way out, he wrapped two extra sausages in a clean square of linen and tucked them into his tunic. Revised pocket-sausage storage protocol: now lint-resistant.

<br/>

By seven o'clock, he was seated at his heavy oak bench in the stone storage shed behind the coal pit. Lined up in front of him were the three broken Aether-assisted furnace blowers Torren had pulled from the armory's scrap reserve.

<br/>

"That's Mabel," Torren had said when he dropped them off, pointing at each one in turn. "That's Dot. And the dented one's Gertrude."

<br/>

"You named broken equipment you've never seen running," Mark had observed.

<br/>

"I name everything," Torren said, offended. "I named my hammer."

<br/>

"What's your hammer's name?"

<br/>

"Hammer," Torren said, as if this were obvious, and left.

<br/>

Mark approached the work with factory-line efficiency.

<br/>

For each bronze regulator, he executed a standardized repair protocol:

<br/>

1. Disassemble the cast casing and inspect the logic disc under his magnifying lens.
2. Needle-file away the melted, fused silver traces that caused the short-circuit bridge.
3. Insert a pine-resin-soaked parchment insulator between the parallel traces.
4. Drill a clean lead hole in the outer bronze housing and solder a thick copper grounding wire to the disc's outer ring.
5. Locate the retaining pin that has rolled under the bench.

<br/>

Step five turned out to be required for every single unit. Mark began to suspect the pins were doing it on purpose.

<br/>

It took him less than an hour per unit. He wasn't inventing new architecture; he was deploying a batch security patch to legacy hardware. He even caught himself humming. It had been years since he'd enjoyed a deployment.

<br/>

By mid-morning, he picked up the power sockets of the three blowers to inspect their mana-crystal shards. Mabel's crystal was still a pale, functional blue, but Dot's and Gertrude's were completely dull, opaque, and slate-grey—resembling worthless pieces of cheap gravel.

<br/>

Mark picked up one of the grey shards and walked out into the main forge yard where Torren was hammering a glowing red bar of iron on the central anvil. With Hammer.

<br/>

"Torren," Mark called over the rhythmic *clang*. "These two power shards are completely dead. Do you have spares in the supply bin?"

<br/>

Torren paused, wiping sweat from his forehead with his soot-stained forearm, and glanced at the grey stones in Mark's hand.

<br/>

"Dead glass," Torren grunted, waving a hand dismissively. "Throw 'em in the slag pit behind the scrap heap. Once the blue goes out, they're drained for good. The Chapterhouse charges two silver bits apiece for fresh ones."

<br/>

Mark looked down at the dull grey stone in his palm. *"Once the blue goes out, they're drained for good."*

<br/>

In forty years, Mark had learned that "that's just how it works" was the single most expensive sentence in any language. It had cost his old company roughly nine million dollars across his career, by his own conservative estimate.

<br/>

"Throw them out?" Mark asked.

<br/>

"Aye. Dead glass is useless to a smith," Torren said, turning back to his glowing iron bar. "Grab two fresh blue shards from the small locked chest on my desk. Key's hanging on the peg."

<br/>

Mark retrieved two fresh crystals from Torren's desk to complete Dot and Gertrude, but he didn't throw the "dead glass" into the slag pit. Instead, he slipped both grey shards into his tunic pocket—carefully, so as not to disturb the sausages—and retreated to the privacy of his shed.

---

Mark bolted the heavy oak door of his shed, sat at his workbench, and set the two dull grey crystals under his magnifying lens.

<br/>

He picked up his iron scribe, carefully angling the stone beneath the pale sunlight filtering through the high window.

<br/>

To the local mages and blacksmiths, a mana-crystal was a mystical battery filled with finite magic. When it stopped glowing, they assumed the "spirit" or "vitality" inside the stone had died.

<br/>

Mark saw something entirely different.

<br/>

Under the lens, the crystal wasn't cracked. The lattice structure was completely intact. The slate-grey color wasn't decay; it was simply a neutral, discharged state.

<br/>

"It's not a disposable battery," Mark whispered, tracing the clean geometric facets of the stone. "It's a lithium-ion cell. Well—" He frowned. "Technically, the way it holds and releases charge, it's closer to a capacitor. Or a supercapacitor with a battery-style discharge curve. Or—"

<br/>

He stopped.

<br/>

"It's rechargeable," he concluded firmly. "Nobody is grading this."

<br/>

The Mages' Guild discarded these stones by the thousands because their rigid, dogmatic doctrine only taught them how to draw energy *out* of a crystal through incantations, not how to pass a reverse current back *into* the lattice to realign its charge. They were throwing away perfectly good rechargeable cells because nobody had bothered to invent a charger.

<br/>

Mark took a ten-inch piece of fine silver wire from his scrap drawer, wrapping one end tightly around the facets of the grey crystal. He wrapped the other end around the outer copper plate of his wearable wrist cuff.

<br/>

He sat barefoot on the dirt floor, dropped into his low, rooted posture, and closed his eyes.

<br/>

*Inhale: four counts. Pull a thin, steady trickle of ambient Aether into his chest.*
*Exhale: four counts. Route the voltage down through his arm, past the wristband's silver traces, and directly into the wire bound to the stone.*

<br/>

He didn't rush it. He didn't force a high-voltage surge. He passed a smooth, continuous trickle of low-amperage current into the crystal lattice—a standard trickle-charge pass.

<br/>

Through the silver wire, Mark felt a faint, rhythmic vibration, like the delicate ticking of a fine mechanical watch.

<br/>

He opened his eyes.

<br/>

Where the silver wire touched the dull slate-grey stone, a tiny, brilliant point of azure blue light flickered. Slowly, like water seeping through dry sand, the blue glow expanded along the geometric facets of the crystal, filling the translucent lattice until the entire shard pulsed with a steady, vibrant, cool blue luminescence.

<br/>

Mark lifted his hand from the floor and unwrapped the wire.

<br/>

The crystal sat on his workbench, glowing with the exact same brilliance as a brand-new two-silver-piece mana shard from the Mages' Chapterhouse.

<br/>

Mark sat back on his stool, letting out a quiet, breathless laugh—which was promptly interrupted by a low, grumbling complaint from his stomach.

<br/>

Not the violent crash from the night before. Just a polite, insistent reminder that nothing in this world was entirely free. The trickle charge had been slow and gentle, but it had still drawn a small toll on his metabolism.

<br/>

Mark unwrapped one of the linen-wrapped sausages and ate it thoughtfully.

<br/>

"Near-zero-cost energy storage," he amended, chewing. "Price: five minutes and one sausage. They're throwing away depleted batteries, and I can recharge them for the cost of a light snack."

<br/>

From the corner of the doorframe, Legacy watched him eat with what he chose to interpret as envy.

---

Now came the real hardware refactor.

<br/>

Mark picked up his wearable leather wristband—Prototype 1.0—and unbuckled it from his forearm.

<br/>

The wristband had solved his nerve-damage bug by acting as a passive heatsink, offloading thermal backlash into the outer copper plate. But yesterday's test proved his body was still taking 100% of the ignition load, burning his own blood sugar to spark the spell.

<br/>

He needed an external starter motor.

<br/>

Using his iron chisel and a small file, Mark modified the outer copper plate on his wrist cuff, mounting a tiny, lead-padded brass socket directly over the heat-dissipation fins. He ran a fine silver wire from the socket down through the leather to connect directly with the inner pulse-point traces on his wrist.

<br/>

He picked up the newly recharged blue mana-crystal, seated it firmly into the brass socket on his cuff, and tightened the small copper retaining clip over top. Then he checked it. Then he checked it again. Right way round. He was not going to live through the backward-crystal incident twice.

<br/>

*"Hardware Prototype 1.1,"* Mark declared mentally. *"Capacitor-assisted wearable interface."*

<br/>

He buckled the cuff tight around his right wrist. The silver-and-copper assembly felt slightly heavier with the crystal mounted, but it remained low-profile—easily hidden under the sleeve of his linen tunic.

<br/>

He stepped to the center of the shed, facing a heavy granite block resting against the back wall.

<br/>

He called up his **Impact Macro**:

<br/>

1. **Ignition Spark:** Draw initial voltage directly from the wrist-mounted mana-crystal capacitor (bypassing biological blood sugar).
2. **Amplification:** Draw ambient Aether from the surrounding air to fill the volume.
3. **Conduit:** Route through silver traces + wristband heatsink.
4. **Target:** Granite block, eight feet forward. *(Still not a boot.)*

<br/>

Mark didn't sink into a deep, dramatic stance this time. He simply set his feet, relaxed his shoulders, and flicked his right palm forward toward the stone block.

<br/>

*Snap!*

<br/>

A sharp, compressed shock wave cracked through the air of the shed. The invisible pulse slammed into the granite block, knocking the thirty-pound stone backward two inches with a dull, heavy *thud*.

<br/>

The impact shook the entire back wall. Dust sifted down from the rafters. In the corner of the doorframe, Legacy's web quivered violently, and the spider herself swung around to face Mark with the unmistakable body language of a tenant composing a complaint to the landlord.

<br/>

"Sorry," Mark told her. "Load testing. It's in the lease."

<br/>

The blue mana-crystal on his wristband flared brightly for a fraction of a second, drawing a tiny fraction of its stored energy to spark the execution, while the outer copper fins hissed softly as they dumped the thermal backlash.

<br/>

Mark stood upright, testing his body.

<br/>

His heart rate was completely steady. No sudden wave of dizziness. No agonizing stomach cramps. No glycemic crash. His blood sugar remained entirely untouched because the crystal shard had provided the initial ignition voltage, leaving his body to act merely as a director, not the fuel tank.

<br/>

"Impact macro fully buffered," Mark whispered, a triumphant grin spreading across his face. "Zero metabolic cost."

<br/>

He patted the remaining sausage in his tunic. *"You're on standby,"* he told it. *"Backup power only."*

---

Late that afternoon, Mark carried the three fully repaired furnace blowers out to the main forge, setting them in a neat row beside Torren's central anvil.

<br/>

Torren set his tongs down, walked over, and tapped the copper actuator on the first unit. Mabel hummed to life instantly, sending a clean, steady, quiet draft of air into the forge hearth. He checked Dot, then Gertrude. Both ran perfectly, their copper grounding wires anchored securely into the stone floor.

<br/>

"Listen to 'em," Torren said softly, his eyes suspiciously damp. "Purring like kittens."

<br/>

"They're air blowers, Torren."

<br/>

"Purring," Torren insisted.

<br/>

He reached into his leather pouch and pulled out three heavy silver coins. He dropped them into Mark's palm with a solid, metallic *clink*.

<br/>

"Three silver pieces, just like I promised," Torren said, shaking his head in approval. "You do clean work, kid. Quick, too."

<br/>

"Thanks, Torren," Mark said, closing his fingers around the coins. Real capital.

<br/>

"If you want to keep working the scrap bin," Torren added, gesturing toward the rear yard, "there's a crate of discarded lighting regulators and five more broken blowers that came in from the timber mills this morning. You fix 'em up, I sell 'em, and we split the profits seventy-thirty."

<br/>

"Who gets the seventy?" Mark asked.

<br/>

"Me, obviously," Torren said. "It's my scrap."

<br/>

"It's my labor," Mark said. "And my expertise. Without me, it's still scrap."

<br/>

Torren narrowed his eyes. Mark narrowed his back. For a moment, the two of them stood beside the anvil like rival merchants at a market stall, each fully aware the other had done this before.

<br/>

"Sixty-forty," Torren offered.

<br/>

"Fifty-fifty."

<br/>

"Sixty-forty," Torren said firmly, "and I'll throw in all the dead glass you want. Every grey shard that comes through this yard. Take the lot. Save me hauling it to the slag pit."

<br/>

Mark paused, as if weighing this very carefully.

<br/>

He thought about the two grey shards he'd just turned into four silver pieces' worth of fresh crystals using nothing but a bit of wire and a sausage. He thought about every forge, mill, and lamp in Oakhaven discarding them every six months. He thought about the phrase *every grey shard that comes through this yard*.

<br/>

"Sixty-forty plus the dead glass," Mark said slowly, with the reluctant air of a man making a significant sacrifice. "Fine. You drive a hard bargain, Torren."

<br/>

Torren grinned, thoroughly pleased with himself, and slapped Mark on the back. "Pleasure doing business, kid."

<br/>

He walked off whistling, utterly convinced he'd just offloaded his garbage disposal problem onto a fourteen-year-old.

<br/>

Mark watched him go with the serene composure of a man who had just acquired the entire mineral rights to a gold mine in exchange for "that junk in the back."

---

That night, back in his quiet shed, Mark sat at his workbench under the cool blue light of his wristband's recharged capacitor. It was, he noted with satisfaction, the first time since Bessie's promotion that he'd had a proper desk lamp again.

<br/>

He opened his leather-bound ledger, picked up his charcoal stick, and wrote in neat, encrypted English cursive:

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 06: Capacitor Integration & Power Pipeline*
> *Key Discoveries:*
> *1. 'Dead' mana-crystals are fully intact, rechargeable Aether cells. Recharging requires a simple low-amperage trickle pass through conductive silver traces. Metabolic cost: minor (approx. one sausage per crystal).*
> *2. Prototype 1.1 (Capacitor-Assisted Cuff) deployed successfully. Mounting a charged crystal directly to the wristband offloads 100% of the ignition drain from biological metabolism.*
> *System Status:*
> *Hardware Prototype 1.1 fully operational. Non-verbal, staffless casting verified under load with zero physical fatigue.*
> *Financial Status: 3 silver coins secured. Repair contract negotiated (60/40, Torren's favor). Unlimited access to discarded crystals acquired as a "sweetener." Torren believes he won.*
> *Facilities: Legacy has filed a noise complaint.*
> *Next Phase:*
> *Expand local memory. Begin compiling higher-complexity macros (multi-target defense, structural analysis) into subconscious muscle-memory routines.*

<br/>

Mark closed the ledger, unbuckled the copper cuff from his wrist, and set it carefully beside his notebook.

<br/>

He leaned back on his cot, listening to the rain on the roof and, faintly, from the dark forge across the yard, the sound of Torren wishing Mabel, Dot, Gertrude, and Bessie a good night, one at a time.

<br/>

Mark smiled into the dark, completely at home in his new laboratory.
