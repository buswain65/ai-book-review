## Chapter 21: Onboarding

<br/>

The first official meeting of Project Root Access took place on a rest-day morning in Torren's shed, and it began, as all great projects do, with an argument about chairs.

<br/>

There were four people and one stool.

<br/>

"I'm the Project Lead," Mark said. "Traditionally, the Project Lead gets the stool."

<br/>

"I'm Facilities," Torren said. "I *own* the stool."

<br/>

"I am a mage of the Chapterhouse," Vespera said, "and I outrank all of you in every possible respect."

<br/>

Lyra simply sat down on the stool while they were arguing and opened her tool roll.

<br/>

The other three looked at her.

<br/>

"Possession," Lyra said, without looking up, "is nine-tenths of the stool."

<br/>

Mark ran the rest of the meeting standing up, holding his brass clipboard, while Torren leaned in the doorway, Vespera perched with great dignity on the edge of the cot, and Legacy observed from her corner of the doorframe with the detached interest of a board member who has seen many projects come and go.

<br/>

"Item one," Mark said. "Status. Since the pact, nobody has tried to kill us."

<br/>

"Promising," said Vespera.

<br/>

"Ignis has assigned me to the vaults every night indefinitely, which means I'm alone in a restricted archive full of First Era material for four hours a night, which is either the best or worst thing that could have happened." He made a note. "I'm calling it a net positive. Mott still doesn't know who I am."

<br/>

"Does Mott know who *anyone* is?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

"Unclear. Item two." Mark lowered the clipboard. "Team-building dinners."

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"What," Vespera asked carefully, "is a team-building dinner?"

<br/>

Mark considered how best to explain it. In Raleigh, he had attended perhaps two hundred of them. They had taken place in chain steakhouses, overpriced tapas bars, and, on one memorable occasion, an escape room with a buffet. He had hated every single one.

<br/>

"It's a meal," he said, "where everyone on a team pretends to enjoy each other's company, until eventually, against all odds, they actually do."

<br/>

"That sounds exhausting," Lyra said.

<br/>

"It is," Mark said. "It also works."

<br/>

"Who pays?" asked Torren.

<br/>

"The Facilities budget," Mark said.

<br/>

Torren thought about this. "*I'm* the Facilities budget."

<br/>

"Correct."

<br/>

Torren thought about it some more. Then he shrugged. "Fair enough. But I want to pick one of the places."

<br/>

"Everyone picks one," Mark said. "That's the rule. One dinner each. Four dinners." He wrote it down. "I'll go first."

---

Before the dinners, though, came the hardware.

<br/>

Back in Chapter Fourteen of his life—Mark had started privately thinking of it in chapters, which he suspected was a bad sign—he had promised Lyra a wristband. A deal was a deal. So that afternoon, he spread his Prototype 1.1 schematics across the oak workbench, pulled out a fresh band of pigskin leather, and prepared to teach.

<br/>

"Right," he said. "The first thing to understand is the trace layout. The silver channels run parallel along the radial pulse point, here, and the return path has to be—"

<br/>

Lyra was already cutting.

<br/>

Mark stopped talking.

<br/>

She had taken one look at his schematic, picked up her finest graver, set the point against the leather, and drawn it down the band in one long, continuous, perfectly even stroke. No hesitation. No overcut. Then another stroke beside it. Then a third, curving gently around the wrist and rejoining the first in a smooth, flowing junction.

<br/>

It looked exactly like the First Era plates.

<br/>

"You've been practicing," Mark said slowly.

<br/>

"Every night," Lyra said, not looking up. "Since the archive. On scrap brass. I couldn't stop thinking about them." Another long, flawless stroke. "I'm not as good as whoever cut those plates. I don't think anyone alive is. But I'm better than I was."

<br/>

Mark said nothing. He just watched.

<br/>

She inlaid the silver wire into the new channels with tweezers and a burnishing tool, so neatly that it sat flush with the leather. She didn't bother with Mark's three diagonal heat fins; instead, she cut a single long, spiraling channel into the copper plate that wrapped all the way around the cuff. It looked like decoration. Mark realized, with a jolt, that it had about four times the surface area of his fins.

<br/>

She finished, buckled it onto her wrist, and handed him a dull grey crystal.

<br/>

"Charge it for me?"

<br/>

Mark trickle-charged the crystal, carefully keeping to Alpha mode and the lowest possible draw, and passed it back. Lyra seated it in her cuff, set her feet, and flicked her palm toward a spare iron offcut on the floor.

<br/>

The iron bar slid smoothly across the dirt and stopped against the wall with a gentle *clink*.

<br/>

No hiss. No flare of light from the heatsink. Her cuff barely glowed.

<br/>

Mark picked up his own wristband and ran the same test on the same bar. It slid the same distance. His copper fins flashed bright blue and hissed as they dumped the backlash.

<br/>

He looked at her cuff. He looked at his.

<br/>

"Your thermal bleed," he said, "is about a third of mine."

<br/>

"Is that good?"

<br/>

"That is *statistically significant*," Mark said.

<br/>

Lyra didn't know what *statistically* meant, but she could tell from his face that it was a compliment, and she sat up a little straighter on her stool.

<br/>

Mark picked up his ledger and wrote:

<br/>

> *Log 13 (draft): Lyra's wristband (Prototype L-1) outperforms Prototype 2.0 by ~3x on thermal efficiency. Lead Engineer has surpassed Project Lead. Do not tell Lead Engineer.*

<br/>

"What does that say?" Lyra asked, leaning over his shoulder.

<br/>

"Nothing," Mark said, closing the ledger.

<br/>

Lyra narrowed her eyes. She couldn't read a single word of English. Somehow, she still looked like she knew exactly what it said.

---

### The First Dinner: *The Pine & Anvil*

<br/>

Mark's pick, as everyone had expected, was nostalgic.

<br/>

"This is where it all started," he explained, as the four of them pushed through the heavy door of *The Pine & Anvil* into the warm, loud common room. "First real meal. First cider. First failed magic experiment. It's legacy infrastructure, but it's *our* legacy infrastructure."

<br/>

"You set a boot on fire here," Vespera recalled.

<br/>

"Allegedly."

<br/>

The common room was exactly as he remembered it: roasting pork, stale ale, tallow candles, woodsmoke. As they took a table near the hearth, the innkeeper tapped the iron button on his copper hearth-lighter, mumbled the two-word password, waited, sighed, smacked the rod twice with his palm, and repeated the phrase louder and slower.

<br/>

The blue flame erupted beneath the logs.

<br/>

Mark felt something unexpected tighten in his chest. *Home,* he thought, and then, immediately, *that is a deeply strange thing to feel about a broken hearth-lighter.*

<br/>

The cook came out from the kitchen to take their order, wiping her ham-sized forearms on her apron. She took one look at Mark and her eyebrows rose.

<br/>

"Growing infrastructure?" she asked.

<br/>

"Still growing," Mark said.

<br/>

She nodded approvingly and turned to the others. Before anyone else could speak, Torren leaned forward, laced his fingers together on the table, and ordered for the entire table with the alarming confidence of a man who had been eating in this inn for thirty years: four plates of roast pork, a whole loaf, a crock of butter, four ciders, a side of fried onions, a second side of fried onions "for the table," and "whatever pie you've got, all of it."

<br/>

"Torren," Vespera said faintly. "There are four of us."

<br/>

"Aye," Torren agreed. "That's why I said four."

---

They were halfway through the pork—and Torren was halfway through the pie, all of it—when the inn's front door banged open and a familiar, booming voice filled the room.

<br/>

"Well, *I'll* be damned!"

<br/>

Mark turned.

<br/>

Standing in the doorway, broad and muddy and grinning through his beard, was Garin.

<br/>

"Garin!" Mark was on his feet before he knew it.

<br/>

"*Kid!*" Garin crossed the room in four enormous strides and enveloped Mark in a hug that lifted him clean off the floor, which hurt his still-healing forearms considerably. "Look at you! Last I heard, you were hauling coal for Torren!"

<br/>

"He's at the Academy now," Torren said proudly.

<br/>

Garin set Mark down and gaped at him. "The *Academy*? *You?*" He looked at Vespera. "Is this true?"

<br/>

"It's a long story," Vespera said.

<br/>

"I've got time and an empty stomach," Garin said, pulling up a chair and helping himself to a fistful of fried onions from the second side, which was, as Torren had foreseen, for the table. "Just got back from a run down the eastern road. Iron and grain, same as always." He looked down at Mark and squinted. "You look good, kid. Better than good. Less like you fell out of a pine tree."

<br/>

"I've been eating more," Mark said. "Power budget."

<br/>

"Whatever that means," Garin said, and grinned. Then he paused, frowned, and lifted one boot off the floor. "Funny thing. You remember my boot? The left one?"

<br/>

Mark kept his face perfectly still. "Vaguely."

<br/>

"Still smells," Garin said darkly. "Months now. Like cheese caught fire inside it. I've washed it, I've aired it, I've left it out in the rain. Nothing." He set his foot back down. "Must be the humidity."

<br/>

"Very humid region," Mark agreed.

<br/>

Vespera made a small noise into her cider that might have been a cough.

<br/>

Lyra, who had never heard the boot story, looked between Mark and Vespera with sharp suspicion. Mark pretended not to notice. He made a mental note to add an entry to the *Strange Things Mark Says* list on her behalf, since she clearly wanted to.

<br/>

They stayed until well past dark. Garin told stories about the eastern road. Torren told stories about Bessie, at length. Lyra told the story of the tower of plates—carefully edited for a merchant's ears, with the construct quietly rewritten as "a very heavy statue"—and Garin laughed until he cried.

<br/>

At some point, Mark looked across the table and caught Vespera watching Garin with a strange, quiet expression on her face.

<br/>

"What?" he asked her softly.

<br/>

"I was just remembering," Vespera said, "a broken cart wheel. On a muddy riverbank. And a strange child who walked out of the woods and asked if we needed a lever." She shook her head slowly. "It feels like a hundred years ago."

<br/>

"It was a few months," Mark said.

<br/>

"I know," Vespera said. "That's what's so strange."

<br/>

Then the clock in the corner struck half past seven, and Mark leaped up so fast he knocked his chair over.

<br/>

"The *vaults*," he said. "I've got duty at eight."

<br/>

"Go," Torren said, waving his fork. "We'll finish the pie."

<br/>

"You've *already* finished the pie."

<br/>

"We'll finish the *crumbs*," Torren said with dignity.

<br/>

Mark ran the whole way back to the Academy with a full stomach and a stitch in his side, and he arrived at the first landing at precisely eight o'clock, red-faced and gasping.

<br/>

Mott looked up from his ledger and smiled warmly.

<br/>

"Are you one of the new ones?"

<br/>

"Yes," Mark wheezed, and collapsed onto a crate.

---

Vespera's wristband was a harder sell.

<br/>

"Absolutely not," she said, when Mark first brought it up.

<br/>

"It'll protect you," Mark said. "You saw what happened in the archive. Clean casting is the future. And the staff—"

<br/>

"The staff stays."

<br/>

"I'm not asking you to give up the staff. Just to have a backup. In case you ever need to cast without one."

<br/>

"Why would I ever need to cast without my staff?"

<br/>

Mark thought about seven-foot silver constructs, and gold glyphs, and Julian's ring. "I can think of a few reasons."

<br/>

Vespera crossed her arms and looked out the shed window for a long moment.

<br/>

"Fine," she said at last. "But it must not *look* like one. I will not wear a blacksmith's apprentice's spark-guard on my wrist like a—a *coal boy*."

<br/>

"No offense taken," Mark said.

<br/>

"It was not aimed at you. Mostly."

<br/>

So Lyra designed it. She spent three evenings on it, in between her own work at the Chapterhouse workshop, and when she finally set it down on the oak workbench, even Torren whistled.

<br/>

It didn't look like a wristband at all. It looked like a lady's bracelet: a delicate band of polished silver, finely engraved with flowing, curling vines and tiny leaves. The traces were hidden inside the vines; the heatsink was a ring of silver leaves around the outside; the crystal socket was set like a gemstone in the clasp. It was, quite simply, the most beautiful thing Mark had seen in this world.

<br/>

Vespera picked it up. She turned it over in her hands. Something in her stern face softened, very briefly, before she caught it and smoothed it back.

<br/>

"It is... adequate," she said.

<br/>

"She loves it," Lyra whispered to Mark.

<br/>

"I *heard* that," Vespera said, and clasped it onto her wrist.

---

Teaching Vespera to use it was where the real trouble started.

<br/>

"Clear your mind," Mark instructed. "Draw the Aether in on the breath. Route it down the arm, into the band. Then just release it at the target. No words."

<br/>

Vespera closed her eyes. She breathed in. She raised her hand toward a candle on the workbench.

<br/>

And, very softly, under her breath, she began to murmur: "*Ignis-parvus, flamma orietur...*"

<br/>

The candle lit.

<br/>

"You chanted," Mark said.

<br/>

Vespera opened her eyes. "I did not."

<br/>

"You chanted the entire incantation. Eleven syllables."

<br/>

"I most certainly did not—"

<br/>

"*Ignis-parvus, flamma orietur*," Lyra repeated helpfully from the stool. "You did a little bow at the end too."

<br/>

Vespera went pink. "That was—I wasn't—" She closed her mouth. "Again."

<br/>

They tried again. And again. And again. Every single time, without fail, the moment Vespera went to cast, her lips began to move. Thirty years of High Imperial training ran so deep that she didn't even hear herself doing it.

<br/>

"It's a muscle-memory problem," Mark said at last. "Back where I come from, we had senior developers who had been typing the same commands by hand for so long that, even when we gave them a script that did it all automatically, they'd still type it out manually. Every time. Out of habit."

<br/>

"I am not a 'senior developer,'" Vespera said frostily.

<br/>

"No," Mark agreed. "You're much better dressed."

<br/>

There was a second problem, too. Every time Vespera began a cast, her right hand drifted sideways and closed on empty air, reaching for a staff that wasn't there. It happened so consistently that by the end of the evening, Torren had started placing a broom handle beside her chair, just so she'd have something to grab.

<br/>

"A phantom limb," Mark said. "Thirty years with a staff. Your hand doesn't believe it's gone."

<br/>

Vespera stared down at her empty right hand. Then she sank down onto the cot, looking more tired and defeated than Mark had ever seen her.

<br/>

"I am too old," she said quietly, "to learn a new Art."

<br/>

"You're not."

<br/>

"I have done it one way my entire life, Mark. Every spell. Every day. Since I was twelve years old." She looked down at the beautiful silver bracelet on her wrist. "Perhaps some of us are simply... legacy systems."

<br/>

Mark sat down on the stool across from her.

<br/>

"I was sixty years old," he said, "when I had to learn all of this from scratch. A new world. A new body. A new kind of magic. A new language I didn't even know I was speaking." He shrugged. "Legacy systems can be upgraded. It just takes longer. And it's more annoying. And everyone keeps telling you to clear your cache."

<br/>

Vespera looked at him. "I have no idea what that means."

<br/>

"It means you're not too old," Mark said. "Try again tomorrow."

---

### The Second Dinner: *The Silver Ladle*

<br/>

Vespera's pick was, she announced firmly, "civilized."

<br/>

*The Silver Ladle* sat at the top of a hill in Oakhaven's noble quarter, a three-story townhouse of pale stone with tall glass windows, a liveried doorman, and a dress code.

<br/>

"A *dress code*?" Torren said, when Vespera told him.

<br/>

"A *coat*, Torren," Vespera said. "With *sleeves*. That is all I ask."

<br/>

Torren did not own a coat with sleeves. He borrowed one from his cousin. His cousin, it emerged, was considerably smaller than Torren. When Torren arrived at the foot of the hill, the coat was stretched so tightly across his shoulders that the seams creaked when he breathed, and he could not lift his arms above chest height.

<br/>

"How do I look?" he asked.

<br/>

"Like a sausage that's been dressed for court," Lyra said.

<br/>

"Thank you," said Torren, who chose to take it as a compliment.

<br/>

The doorman looked at Torren for a long time. Then he looked at Mark's linen tunic and scuffed work boots, and Lyra's workshop apron with its twelve bulging pockets, and finally at Vespera, elegant and composed in deep indigo silk with a silver bracelet gleaming at her wrist.

<br/>

"Your party, madam?" he asked Vespera, with the faint hope of a man looking for a reason to say no.

<br/>

"My *colleagues*," Vespera said icily, and swept past him.

<br/>

Inside, *The Silver Ladle* was all polished wood, white linen, and silverware arranged in such quantities that Torren stared at his place setting with open alarm.

<br/>

"Why are there *five* forks?"

<br/>

"Each course has its own fork," Vespera murmured. "Work from the outside in."

<br/>

"How many courses are there?"

<br/>

"Seven."

<br/>

Torren looked at the forks. Then he looked at Vespera. "And how big are the courses?"

<br/>

The first course arrived.

<br/>

It was a single, perfect, very small slice of pickled radish, balanced artfully on a large white plate, with one dot of green sauce beside it.

<br/>

The table regarded it in silence.

<br/>

"Is that," Torren asked slowly, "the *radish*? Or is that a picture of where the radish is going to be?"

<br/>

"That is the first course," Vespera said.

<br/>

"Where's the rest of it?"

<br/>

"That *is* the rest of it."

<br/>

Torren ate the radish in one bite, chewed it twice, and looked around the dining room as if he expected more radish to appear.

<br/>

The courses kept coming. A thimble of chilled soup. A single scallop on a spoon. A sliver of fish beneath a translucent shaving of something nobody could identify. And, as the centerpiece, the house specialty: a "deconstructed stew," which turned out to be three carrots, one cube of beef, and a drizzle of brown sauce, arranged in what the waiter assured them was "a meditation on autumn."

<br/>

Torren looked down at the meditation on autumn.

<br/>

"Somebody," he said, "has stolen this stew."

<br/>

"It's *deconstructed*," Vespera said, through gritted teeth.

<br/>

"Somebody's *taken it apart* and *kept most of it*."

<br/>

Mark looked at his own plate and sighed. "Fine dining," he said, "is a scam in every universe."

<br/>

Lyra, meanwhile, had worked out that the bread basket was free and endlessly refilled, and was steadily transferring bread rolls into her apron, one per pocket. By the fourth course, she had filled nine pockets. The waiter had noticed and decided, very wisely, not to say anything.

---

It was during the fifth course that two things happened at once.

<br/>

The first was the soup.

<br/>

Vespera's second soup course—a creamy, elegant bisque of some sort—arrived stone cold. She tasted it, set her spoon down, and turned to signal the waiter. But the waiter had just disappeared into the kitchen, and the next nearest staff member was busy pouring wine for a table of very loud merchants.

<br/>

Vespera's mouth tightened. Mark could see the irritation building in her: a long week, a hard evening of being stared at by doormen, and now cold soup at the end of it.

<br/>

Without thinking, without looking, she flicked her left wrist at the bowl.

<br/>

The bisque began to steam.

<br/>

Gently. Evenly. The surface shivered, a lazy curl of steam rose up, and the soup settled at precisely the right temperature, not one degree too hot.

<br/>

Vespera picked up her spoon and took a sip.

<br/>

Then she froze.

<br/>

She looked down at the soup. Then at the silver bracelet on her wrist, its crystal glowing faintly blue beneath the clasp. Then at her right hand, which was resting on the white tablecloth, completely still, not reaching for anything.

<br/>

She hadn't chanted. She hadn't reached for a staff. She hadn't even *thought* about it.

<br/>

She had simply been annoyed about her soup.

<br/>

Very slowly, Vespera lifted her eyes and looked across the table at Mark.

<br/>

Mark didn't say anything. Neither did Lyra. Neither did Torren, who had just lifted a fork halfway to his mouth and stopped there. The four of them sat in total silence around a white linen table in the most expensive restaurant in Oakhaven, all staring at a bowl of bisque.

<br/>

Then Lyra, very quietly, under the table, raised a fist in triumph.

<br/>

Torren, who could not lift his arms above chest height, raised his fork as high as the coat would allow, which was not very high.

<br/>

And Mark, grinning, raised his glass.

<br/>

"To soup," he said softly.

<br/>

"To soup," Lyra and Torren whispered back.

<br/>

Vespera looked at the three of them. For a moment, her composure wavered dangerously. Then she lifted her own glass, very slightly, and touched it to Mark's.

<br/>

"To soup," she said.

---

The second thing that happened was Valerius.

<br/>

Mark spotted him first: a tall, lean figure in a dark coat, sitting alone at a small corner table by the window, eating his seventh course in silence with a book propped open beside his plate. The razor-thin mustache was unmistakable.

<br/>

Mark's stomach tightened. He nudged Vespera's foot under the table and inclined his head very slightly toward the window.

<br/>

Vespera turned. She saw Valerius.

<br/>

At exactly that moment, Valerius looked up from his book and saw her.

<br/>

For a long, frozen second, the High Inspector and the treasonous mage looked at each other across the dining room of *The Silver Ladle*.

<br/>

Then Valerius inclined his head. A small, grave, perfectly polite nod.

<br/>

Vespera returned it. A small, grave, perfectly polite nod.

<br/>

Valerius went back to his book.

<br/>

Vespera turned back to the table. Her cheeks had gone very slightly pink, and she picked up her spoon and began to eat her bisque with tremendous concentration.

<br/>

"Was that—" Lyra began.

<br/>

"No," said Vespera.

<br/>

"I didn't even finish the—"

<br/>

"*No*," said Vespera.

<br/>

Mark took out his ledger under the table, and wrote very small:

<br/>

> *Valerius: dines alone. Reads at dinner. Nodded at V. V. turned pink. Watch.*

<br/>

When the bill came, Torren looked at it for a long, long time. Then he handed it silently to Mark.

<br/>

Mark looked at it.

<br/>

"For that money," Torren said hollowly, "I could have fed the four of us at the *Pine & Anvil* for a month. With *pie*."

<br/>

"Fine dining," Mark repeated, sliding the bill back across the table to the Facilities budget, "is a scam in *every* universe."

<br/>

On the walk back down the hill, Torren finally managed to wriggle out of his cousin's coat, and they could hear the seams sigh with relief. Lyra handed each of them a bread roll from her apron. They ate them under the two moons, and they were, everyone agreed, the best part of the meal.

---

### Facilities Has Requested a Budget

<br/>

Torren's plans for the workshop had been growing quietly for weeks. Now, with the pact signed and the blower business booming, he called a meeting of his own.

<br/>

He had clearly been taking notes on Mark's style. He arrived with an agenda written on the back of an old order slip and held it up proudly. It said, in large, careful letters:

<br/>

*1. BUDGET*

<br/>

*2. MORE BUDGET*

<br/>

"That's the entire agenda," Lyra observed.

<br/>

"It's a very important agenda," Torren said.

<br/>

Funding, it turned out, was not a problem. Between the blower-repair business—now serving three timber mills, two other forges, and the town's tannery—and the endless stream of "dead glass" Torren had so generously agreed to give away back in the sixty-forty deal, the project had money for the first time.

<br/>

The dead glass was the real secret. Mark recharged the discarded crystals in quiet batches in the evenings before his vault shifts, always in Alpha mode, always at the lowest possible draw, in short, careful bursts—"rate-limiting myself," he explained, "so the Thorne family's rings don't light up like a festival every time I top off a battery." Torren then sold them, discreetly and at a fair price, to every smith, miller, and craftsman in the lower district who was sick of paying the Chapterhouse two silver bits apiece.

<br/>

"So," Torren said, beaming. "Budget."

<br/>

The new workshop took shape over the next two weeks. Torren knocked through the back wall of the shed into the old storage room behind it, doubling the floor space. He installed a proper engraver's vise for Lyra ("Finally," she said, almost tearfully), a stone casting bench, two new workbenches, a rack of tools, and a small forge of its own, powered by a fifth blower named, inevitably, Edna.

<br/>

On the final day, he made them all close their eyes while he hung something over the new front door.

<br/>

"Right," Torren said. "Open."

<br/>

They opened their eyes.

<br/>

Hanging above the door was a carved wooden sign: a fish, holding a hammer.

<br/>

Mark stared at it.

<br/>

He knew this sign. He had stood in front of this exact sign on his first week in Oakhaven, outside a shop near the apothecary, for a full minute, completely unable to work out whether it sold fish, built boats, or was making some kind of threat.

<br/>

"Where did you *get* that?" he asked.

<br/>

"Shop by the apothecary closed down," Torren said. "Got it cheap. Fine piece of carving, that."

<br/>

"Torren," Mark said slowly. "What does it *mean*?"

<br/>

Torren looked up at the sign with great pride.

<br/>

"It means we fix things," he said. Then, after a moment: "And fish."

<br/>

Mark looked at him. Torren looked back, perfectly sincere.

<br/>

"Do we fish?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

"We *could*," Torren said.

<br/>

Mark decided he would never, as long as he lived, know whether Torren was joking. He found, somewhat to his surprise, that he was entirely at peace with this.

---

There was one final, unexpected development in the new workshop.

<br/>

On the first morning after the walls came down, Mark arrived to find that the corner of the old shed's doorframe was empty.

<br/>

His heart lurched. "Where's Legacy?"

<br/>

He searched the workshop with growing alarm. Under the benches. Behind the vise. Along the rafters. Lyra joined in. Then Torren.

<br/>

It was Lyra who found her.

<br/>

Legacy had relocated, entirely on her own initiative, to the upper corner of the *new* front doorframe, directly beside the carved fish. She had already spun a large, elaborate, and clearly permanent web. She turned all eight eyes toward Mark as he approached.

<br/>

Mark looked up at her for a long moment.

<br/>

"She approves," he said quietly. "I'm counting it as a vote of confidence."

<br/>

"She's guarding the entrance," Torren said, with deep respect. "Like a dragon."

<br/>

"Like a *security asset*," Mark corrected. "Enforcers are afraid of her."

<br/>

"Margaret is very brave," Torren agreed.

<br/>

Legacy turned very deliberately to face away from Torren.

---

### The Third Dinner: *The Deep Hearth*

<br/>

Torren's pick was in the lower town, down a flight of stone steps carved into the hillside, behind a heavy iron-banded door with no sign at all.

<br/>

"No sign?" Mark asked.

<br/>

"Everyone who needs to know where it is," Torren said, "knows where it is."

<br/>

He pushed open the door.

<br/>

*The Deep Hearth* was a single long room carved straight into the bedrock, lit by a roaring fire pit in the center and lined with long stone tables. It was loud, hot, smoky, and crowded with broad-shouldered men and women, a remarkable number of whom had braided beards. They looked up as the door opened. Then they saw Torren and roared a greeting that shook the dust from the ceiling.

<br/>

"*TORREN!*"

<br/>

"Cousin!" Torren roared back, and was immediately engulfed by a woman nearly as wide as he was, with a beard braided in no fewer than six plaits and forearms that made the *Pine & Anvil* cook look delicate.

<br/>

"This is my cousin Brunhild," Torren announced. "She runs the place."

<br/>

"You're the scrap boy," Brunhild said, looking down at Mark. "The one who fixed the blowers."

<br/>

"That's me."

<br/>

"And named the ballast block."

<br/>

"*Torren* named the ballast block. Lyra named the war machine."

<br/>

Brunhild nodded, as if this explained everything, and shoved them toward a stone table by the fire.

<br/>

The food arrived without anyone ordering it. There were no menus at *The Deep Hearth*; there was simply whatever Brunhild was cooking. Tonight, that was a loaf of dark bread so dense and hard that when Lyra tried to tear it, nothing happened; a cauldron of stew so hot it was still visibly bubbling in the bowl; and four tankards of ale, each made of solid pewter and approximately the size and weight of a small child.

<br/>

Lyra tried to lift hers. She managed about two inches before she had to put it down.

<br/>

"Use both hands, lass," Torren advised.

<br/>

Lyra used both hands. She managed four inches. Mark considered offering her his wristband for the extra kinetic lift, then decided that would be cheating, and then decided that he didn't care, and then found that Lyra had already flicked her own cuff and was drinking her ale with an expression of smug triumph.

<br/>

The stew was magnificent. It was also, Mark estimated, roughly the temperature of the sun.

<br/>

He took one spoonful, burned the roof of his mouth, and set the spoon down, eyes watering.

<br/>

"*Good*," Torren said approvingly. "Now tell her."

<br/>

"Tell her what?"

<br/>

"That it's terrible."

<br/>

Mark stared at him. "It's *wonderful*."

<br/>

"Aye, and that's why you have to tell her it's terrible," Torren said, as though explaining the obvious to a small child. "It's custom. The worse the insult, the better the compliment. If you just say it's good, she'll think you didn't like it."

<br/>

Mark looked at Brunhild, who was standing by the fire with her arms crossed, watching their table with an expression of fierce expectation.

<br/>

"*Brunhild!*" Torren bellowed across the room. "This stew tastes like a *boot*!"

<br/>

A roar of approval went up from the nearby tables. Brunhild beamed with pride.

<br/>

"*Your mother's* boot!" Torren added, warming up. "The *left* one! It's *older* than *Gerald*!"

<br/>

Brunhild placed a hand on her heart, deeply moved.

<br/>

Vespera was watching all this with an expression of total horror. "This is *barbaric*."

<br/>

Lyra was watching it with an expression of total delight. "This is *wonderful*."

<br/>

Mark took another, more careful spoonful of stew, swallowed, and found that he loved it immediately.

<br/>

"It's code review," he explained to Vespera. "But for food. You say the harshest thing you can think of, everybody knows it's out of respect, and nobody's feelings get hurt." He turned toward the fire and raised his voice. "*Brunhild! This stew has the architecture of a collapsed mine shaft!*"

<br/>

The room paused. A few heads turned, puzzled.

<br/>

Brunhild frowned at him for a long moment, clearly trying to parse it.

<br/>

"...I *think* that's a good one?" she ventured.

<br/>

"It's an excellent one," Torren assured her.

<br/>

Brunhild beamed and sent over more bread.

---

Then, near the end of the meal, the cousin brought out a special dish.

<br/>

She set it down in the center of the table with great ceremony. It was a slab of meatloaf, dark and dense and enormous, molded into the shape of a perfect rectangular block.

<br/>

"On the house," Brunhild announced. "For the boy who named the war machine. I call it *Gerald's Stew.*"

<br/>

"That's a meatloaf," Mark said.

<br/>

"It's a *stew*," Brunhild said firmly, "that's been *made solid*."

<br/>

Torren looked at the block of meatloaf. His eyes grew suspiciously bright. He took off his cap and held it over his heart.

<br/>

"He'd have loved this," Torren whispered.

<br/>

"He was a ballast block, Torren," Vespera said gently.

<br/>

"He was a *good* ballast block," Torren said, and wiped his eyes.

<br/>

They ate Gerald's Stew in respectful silence. It was, everyone privately agreed, the best meatloaf any of them had ever had. Mark told Brunhild it tasted "like an avalanche in a tax office," and she was so pleased that she hugged him, which hurt his forearms considerably.

---

It was after the meatloaf, with the ale working and the fire roaring, that the arm-wrestling started.

<br/>

Nobody was entirely sure, afterward, how it began. Mark suspected it had something to do with Lyra mentioning, very casually, that her wristband drew a third of the thermal bleed of anyone else's at the table. And Vespera mentioning, equally casually, that a bracelet was hardly a fair test of a *real* mage's capacity.

<br/>

One moment they were talking. The next, Lyra and Vespera were facing each other across the stone table, elbows planted, hands clasped, their wristbands glowing faintly blue.

<br/>

"This is not a contest," Vespera said.

<br/>

"Of course not," Lyra said.

<br/>

"This is a *demonstration*."

<br/>

"Of *course*."

<br/>

"On three," Torren said, delighted. "One. Two—"

<br/>

Neither of them waited for three.

<br/>

There was no visible strain. Both of them sat perfectly upright, perfectly still, hands locked together over the center of the table. But Mark could feel it, a faint, rising hum in the air. He could see the two crystals brightening by degrees. Lyra's cuff, with its long spiral heatsink, barely glowed at all. Vespera's bracelet, with its ring of silver leaves, began to shimmer like moonlight on water.

<br/>

The pewter tankards on the table began to rattle.

<br/>

The stone table began to hum.

<br/>

A thin, hairline crack appeared in the stone directly beneath their elbows and began to spread.

<br/>

"*Ladies*," Torren said, with a hint of alarm, "Brunhild *made* that table—"

<br/>

Mark, eating the last of the meatloaf, observed all this with calm professional interest.

<br/>

"Informal benchmark," he noted, through a mouthful. "Lyra's design has better thermal efficiency, but Vespera's has thirty years of channeling experience behind it. Very close. Ooh—Vespera's starting to chant."

<br/>

"I am *not*," Vespera hissed. Her lips stopped moving.

<br/>

"Lyra's getting a nosebleed."

<br/>

"I am *fine*," Lyra growled.

<br/>

The crack in the stone reached the edge of the table.

<br/>

"*Enough!*" Brunhild roared from across the room.

<br/>

Both of them let go at exactly the same instant. The hum vanished. The tankards stopped rattling. The two of them sat back in their chairs, breathing hard, staring at each other across the cracked stone table.

<br/>

There was a long pause.

<br/>

"A draw," Mark announced.

<br/>

"A *draw*," Vespera agreed, a little breathlessly.

<br/>

"*For now*," Lyra said, wiping her nose.

<br/>

Then, to Mark's enormous surprise, Vespera smiled. Not her usual thin, polite smile. A real one, warm and a little wild. And Lyra grinned back at her across the cracked table, a little wild herself, and for the first time since he'd introduced them in his shed, Mark had the strong impression that the two of them actually liked each other.

<br/>

*Team building,* he thought. *It works.*

<br/>

Brunhild came over and inspected the cracked table. She looked at the crack for a long time. Then she looked at the two of them.

<br/>

"That," she said, "is the *worst thing* anyone's ever done to my furniture."

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"I think that's a compliment," Lyra whispered.

<br/>

"It's the *best* compliment," Torren whispered back.

---

### The Fourth Dinner: A Noodle Stall by the River

<br/>

Lyra's pick was not a restaurant at all.

<br/>

"This is where workshop people actually eat," she said, leading them down a narrow set of steps to the riverbank below the Chapterhouse workshop district.

<br/>

It was a single wooden stall under a canvas awning, with a steaming copper cauldron, a few rickety stools, and a hand-painted icon on a board above it showing a bowl and a pair of sticks. A cluster of tired-looking engravers, polishers, and apprentices sat around it on upturned crates, slurping noodles out of clay bowls in the twilight.

<br/>

An old man with a long white mustache stood behind the cauldron. He looked up, saw Lyra, and grinned with no teeth at all.

<br/>

"*Lyra!* The usual?"

<br/>

"The usual, Hamm. And three more."

<br/>

"Three more *usual*?"

<br/>

Lyra looked at Mark, Vespera, and Torren. "...Three more *regular*."

<br/>

The noodles arrived in steaming clay bowls, thick and hand-cut, swimming in a rich broth, topped with green onions and slices of grilled river fish.

<br/>

Mark lifted a slice of the fish with his chopsticks.

<br/>

Then he stopped. Lowered it. Raised it again. Turned it over. Held it up to the dimming light.

<br/>

The fish was glowing.

<br/>

Faintly. Very faintly. A soft, pale, unmistakable blue luminescence, shimmering along the grain of the flesh like moonlight caught in water.

<br/>

Mark looked down into the river. In the dark water, here and there, faint blue shapes were moving: slow, gentle, glimmering fish, swimming lazy circles beneath the surface like drifting lanterns.

<br/>

"Lyra," he said slowly. "Why is the fish glowing?"

<br/>

"Oh," Lyra said, slurping. "That's the river fish. They all glow a little. Have for years."

<br/>

"*Why* do they glow?"

<br/>

Lyra paused with her noodles halfway to her mouth. Mark watched her work it out. Then she slowly turned to look up the bank, at the long stone chute that ran down from the back of the Chapterhouse workshop and emptied directly into the river, the chute she'd been dumping "dead" mana-crystals down every single evening for four years.

<br/>

"...Oh," she said.

<br/>

"Decades," Mark said, "of the entire Chapterhouse dumping charged mana-crystals into this river."

<br/>

"They weren't charged," Lyra said weakly. "They were *dead*."

<br/>

"They were at zero percent," Mark said. "And then they sat in a river full of ambient Aether for years. Some of them probably trickle-charged themselves. And the fish ate them. Or swam around them. Or—" He held up the faintly glowing slice of fish. "I don't actually know. That's the problem."

<br/>

"Is it *safe*?" Vespera asked, holding her own slice of glowing fish at arm's length.

<br/>

"People have been eating it for years," Lyra said. "It's a local delicacy. Hamm calls it 'Lantern Fish.'"

<br/>

"Has anyone had any... side effects?"

<br/>

Lyra considered. "Old Pell says he can see in the dark now. But Old Pell says a lot of things."

<br/>

Mark looked at the glowing fish. He looked at the glowing river.

<br/>

"Bioluminescent fish," he said, half to himself. "In a fantasy river. Caused by industrial crystal runoff. I'm going to need to talk to someone about environmental compliance."

<br/>

Torren, meanwhile, had eaten his entire bowl of noodles, including all of the fish, in about forty seconds.

<br/>

"Delicious," he said. "Can I have another?"

<br/>

They all looked at him. Torren looked back. In the gathering dusk, Mark could have sworn—though he was willing to put it down to the lighting—that Torren's beard was glowing just very, very slightly.

<br/>

"What?" Torren asked.

<br/>

"Nothing," the other three said in unison.

<br/>

And Lyra, when Hamm came back around with the pot, ordered herself a second bowl of cheese noodles, with extra cheese. Nobody asked.

---

They were scraping the bottoms of their bowls when a small, fast figure came pelting down the riverbank steps out of the dark, skidded to a stop in front of the stall, and doubled over, gasping.

<br/>

"Pip?" Mark said.

<br/>

Pip straightened up, breathing hard, freckled face flushed with exertion. He looked around the table—at Mark, Lyra, Vespera, Torren, at the steaming noodle bowls, at the faintly glowing fish—and then drew himself up with tremendous self-importance.

<br/>

"*Message bus*," Pip announced, "*delivering.*"

<br/>

"Pip," Mark said. "It's good to see you. How did you even know we were here?"

<br/>

"Errand boys hear everything," Pip said, with the air of someone quoting an expert. "You *told* me that."

<br/>

"I did," Mark admitted. "What's the message?"

<br/>

Pip glanced around at the tired workshop apprentices on their crates, then leaned in close and dropped his voice to a dramatic whisper.

<br/>

"The Ironhead Syndicate," he breathed. "They want to talk to you."

<br/>

The table went quiet.

<br/>

"Garek's lot?" Torren rumbled. "What do they want?"

<br/>

"I don't know exactly," Pip whispered. "But Garek said to tell you it's important. And it's urgent. And it's secret." He paused, frowning, as if trying to remember the precise wording. "And he said to tell you it's... about the goats."

<br/>

There was a long silence around the noodle stall.

<br/>

"The *goats*," Vespera repeated.

<br/>

"The *goats*," Pip confirmed solemnly.

<br/>

Mark set down his chopsticks very slowly.

<br/>

*"The goats,"* he thought. *"I knew it. I always knew it was the goats."*

---

It was nearly midnight by the time Mark got back to his narrow dorm room in the West Wing. He'd had to beg an hour's leave from vault duty for the noodle stall—a request Mott had granted by asking if he was one of the new ones and then going back to sleep.

<br/>

The lentil spider was still in its web in the corner of the ceiling. Mark nodded to it.

<br/>

"Good news," he told it. "There's an opening at the new workshop. Doorframe position. Excellent benefits. You'd be reporting to Legacy."

<br/>

The lentil spider did not respond. Mark decided to take that as *I'll think about it.*

<br/>

He lit his capacitor lamp, opened his ledger, picked up his charcoal stick, and wrote:

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 13: Team Onboarding & Infrastructure Expansion*
> *Team Status:*
> *1. Lyra's wristband (Prototype L-1) deployed. Uses First Era-style continuous-stroke traces and spiral heatsink. Thermal bleed ~1/3 of Prototype 2.0. Lead Engineer has surpassed Project Lead. (She knows.)*
> *2. Vespera's wristband (Prototype V-1, disguised as bracelet) deployed. Initial issues: involuntary chanting, phantom-staff reflex. RESOLVED: first fully silent, staffless cast achieved at The Silver Ladle, in response to cold soup. Root cause of breakthrough: mild irritation. Recommend more irritation.*
> *3. Informal benchmark (The Deep Hearth): Lyra vs. Vespera. Result: draw. Damage: one (1) stone table. Team cohesion: significantly improved.*
> *Infrastructure:*
> *- Workshop expanded. New vise, casting bench, small forge. New blower: Edna.*
> *- Funding secured via repair contracts and recharged crystal sales. Recharging conducted in low-draw Alpha bursts to avoid Thorne detection.*
> *- New signage: fish holding a hammer. Meaning: 'We fix things. And fish.' Unclear if joke.*
> *- Legacy has relocated to the new front door. Promoted to Head of Security.*
> *Vendor Reviews:*
> *- The Pine & Anvil: Reliable. Legacy infrastructure. Hearth-lighter still requires two smacks. Five stars.*
> *- The Silver Ladle: Overpriced. Microservices architecture: too many tiny components, none of them filling. Excellent bread (free). Two stars.*
> *- The Deep Hearth: Hostile UX, robust backend. Customers expected to insult the product. Five stars. Would insult again.*
> *- River noodle stall: Outstanding. Fish glow. Environmental compliance concerns. Investigate. Five stars.*
> *Open Items:*
> *- Valerius dines alone and reads at dinner. V. turned pink. Continue to watch.*
> *- Garin's boot: still under the humidity cover story. Maintain.*
> *- Torren's beard may be glowing slightly. Monitor.*
> *- Ironhead Syndicate requests a meeting. Topic: goats. (Finally.)*

<br/>

He sat back and read the entry over.

<br/>

Then, after a moment, he picked up the charcoal again and added a final line:

<br/>

> *Team morale: high. Do not tell the team.*

<br/>

He looked at that line for a long moment. In Raleigh, he had sat through two hundred team-building dinners in chain steakhouses and tapas bars and, once, an escape room with a buffet. He had never once enjoyed a single one.

<br/>

He thought about Torren in his cousin's too-small coat, staring at a single slice of radish. Vespera raising her glass to a bowl of soup. Lyra and Vespera grinning at each other over a cracked stone table. Garin's boot. Gerald's Stew. Glowing noodles under the moons.

<br/>

Mark closed the ledger and put out the lamp.

<br/>

*"Huh,"* he thought, lying back in the dark. *"So that's what they were supposed to feel like."*
