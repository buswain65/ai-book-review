## Chapter 19: The Security Sweep

<br/>

Mark spent the entire day waiting for the other shoe to drop.

<br/>

It didn't. The Academy carried on exactly as it always had. Bells rang. Students in velvet robes hurried between lectures. Archmage Ignis delivered ninety minutes on "The Ennobling Agony of Thermal Bleed" and spat on the front row twice. Nobody mentioned a low chime in the night, a red glow below the third landing, or anything at all unusual about the vaults.

<br/>

*"No incident report,"* Mark thought, sitting through his afternoon lecture with his back carefully to the wall. *"No alerts. No pages. Which means either nothing happened..."*

<br/>

He didn't finish the thought. Forty years in operations had taught him how the other half of that sentence usually went.

<br/>

Twice that day, he caught Julian Thorne watching him.

<br/>

The first time was in the dining hall. Julian sat at the head of the long noble table, surrounded by his usual court of admirers, but he wasn't eating. He was looking down the hall at Mark with an expression Mark couldn't quite read. It wasn't the contempt from the lecture hall. It was something cooler and more careful, like a man examining a crack in a wall and estimating how long it would hold.

<br/>

The second time was in the corridor outside the archive stairs, just before eight o'clock. Julian was leaning against a pillar, alone for once, turning a heavy silver signet ring around and around on his finger.

<br/>

"Vance," he said, as Mark passed.

<br/>

"Thorne."

<br/>

"Enjoying the vaults?"

<br/>

"Very much," Mark said. "Great filing system. Lovely archivist. Excellent ambiance."

<br/>

Julian's mouth curved in something that wasn't quite a smile. "Find anything interesting?"

<br/>

Mark kept his face perfectly neutral. He'd sat across from enough opposing counsel in licensing disputes to recognize a fishing question when he heard one. "Plates," he said. "Various."

<br/>

Julian held his gaze a moment longer. Then he pushed off the pillar and walked away without another word, still turning the ring.

<br/>

Mark watched him go. *"That,"* he thought, *"was not small talk."*

<br/>

---

<br/>

Lyra was waiting for him at the first landing, tool roll across her chest, apron pockets bulging. She took one look at his face and narrowed her eyes.

<br/>

"What happened?"

<br/>

"Julian Thorne asked me if I'd found anything interesting."

<br/>

"What did you tell him?"

<br/>

"'Plates. Various.'"

<br/>

Lyra considered this. "Good answer."

<br/>

"I thought so."

<br/>

She began unloading her pockets onto the worktable: calipers, rouge, graver, a coil of silver wire, and a fresh wedge of hard yellow cheese wrapped in cloth.

<br/>

Mark looked at the cheese.

<br/>

"Cheese the Third," Lyra said, before he could speak. "Don't ask about Cheese the Second."

<br/>

"What happened to Cheese the Second?"

<br/>

"I said don't ask."

<br/>

Mark produced his own contribution: two sausages wrapped in clean linen, which he set beside the cheese with the gravity of a quartermaster stocking a fort. They both looked at the little pile of provisions on the table and, without saying a word, privately agreed that neither of them was entirely sure why they had both brought so much food to a filing job.

<br/>

Archivist Mott shuffled out from behind his desk, wrapped in his usual layers of grey wool, and peered at them warmly.

<br/>

"Are you two new ones?"

<br/>

"Yes," they said in unison.

<br/>

"Lovely," Mott said. "I'm off for my nap. Lock the old cases." He shuffled toward the stairs, then paused and added, as if remembering something very faintly, "And don't make too much noise. It doesn't like noise."

<br/>

Mark and Lyra looked at each other.

<br/>

"What doesn't like noise?" Mark called after him.

<br/>

But Mott was already climbing, very slowly, one step at a time, and either didn't hear or had already forgotten.

<br/>

---

<br/>

They worked in tense silence for the first hour.

<br/>

Mark had intended to spend the night hunting for more Concord documents. Instead, he found himself cataloging plates and listening. Every creak of old stone made him look up. Every flicker of the wall lamps made Lyra's hand drift toward her graver.

<br/>

At nine-fifteen, far above them, they heard the heavy iron door at the top of the stairs slam shut.

<br/>

Then came a long, grinding scrape of metal, and the solid *clunk* of a crossbar dropping into its brackets.

<br/>

Mark was on his feet and halfway up the first flight before the echo faded. He reached the top landing and put his shoulder into the door.

<br/>

It didn't budge.

<br/>

Through the narrow grate at eye level, Mark could see the torchlit corridor beyond—and Julian Thorne, standing a few paces back from the door, calmly drawing a pair of fine leather gloves onto his hands.

<br/>

"Thorne," Mark said evenly. "Open the door."

<br/>

"No," Julian said.

<br/>

"This is a disciplinary shift. Mott will be back—"

<br/>

"Mott will sleep until dawn. He always does." Julian finished with his gloves and looked up at the grate. In the torchlight, his face was perfectly composed. "You ran one of the old plates last night, didn't you?"

<br/>

Mark said nothing.

<br/>

"You did," Julian said. "I felt it. Everyone in my family can feel it, if they know what to listen for." He held up his hand, and the silver signet ring caught the light. Deep in its setting, a tiny stone was glowing a faint, sullen red. "You shouldn't have done that, Vance. There are rules about clean casting. Very old rules. And there are things below the third landing whose entire purpose is to enforce them."

<br/>

Behind Mark, Lyra had come up the stairs. She stopped dead.

<br/>

"You *know*," she breathed. "You know what's down there."

<br/>

"House Thorne has always known," Julian said. "We are the Keepers of the Measure. We have kept it for five hundred years." He paused. "Some of us more eagerly than others. My father would have reported you. I find this more efficient."

<br/>

"You're going to let something *kill* us," Lyra said, her voice rising, "because he lifted an anvil *too well*?"

<br/>

Julian looked at her for the first time. Something flickered in his face—just for an instant—then smoothed over again.

<br/>

"I'm not going to let anything happen," he said quietly. "I'm simply going to go to bed. What happens below the third landing is not my responsibility. It never has been. That's rather the point of it."

<br/>

He turned and walked away down the corridor. His footsteps faded. A distant door closed.

<br/>

Mark stood at the grate, very still.

<br/>

"He just," Lyra said slowly, "locked us in the basement. With the thing. On purpose."

<br/>

"He did," Mark said.

<br/>

"And he used the word *efficient*."

<br/>

"I noticed that."

<br/>

Lyra reached into her apron, pulled out her brass clipboard, and pressed it hard against the door. She held it there for a long moment.

<br/>

The door remained locked.

<br/>

"Worth a try," Lyra said grimly, and put it away.

<br/>

Mark turned and looked down the long, spiraling staircase toward the archive, and the darkness beneath it, and the third landing below that.

<br/>

Forty years in tech, and he had finally found the one situation that never got covered in the incident response runbook: locked in the server room on the night shift, no on-call rotation, no escalation path, and the monitoring system itself coming up the stairs to get you.

<br/>

"Okay," he said. "New plan."

<br/>

---

<br/>

They had been back in the archive for six minutes when the lamps began to go out.

<br/>

It started at the bottom of the room, near the stairwell that led down to the lower landings. The farthest blue wall lamp dimmed, flickered, and died. Then the next. Then the next. One by one, steadily, from the bottom up, as if someone were walking slowly through the dark and pinching them out as they went.

<br/>

"It's pulling power," Mark murmured, watching. "It's drawing Aether out of the lamps to feed itself. Power-saving mode in reverse."

<br/>

A breath of cold air drifted up from the lower stairwell. Frost began to form on the bottom steps—a thin white skin creeping upward, one stair at a time, glittering in the dying light.

<br/>

Then the chime came. Low and slow and deep, from somewhere very close.

<br/>

Lyra had her graver in one hand and Cheese the Third in the other. Her knuckles were white on both.

<br/>

"Tell me again," she whispered, "how it's probably nothing."

<br/>

"I'm revising my estimate," Mark said.

<br/>

The last lamp near the lower stairwell went out.

<br/>

And something came up the stairs.

<br/>

---

<br/>

It was taller than Mark had imagined. Seven feet, at least. It moved in total silence, its long silver-white limbs unfolding with smooth, eerie precision as it stepped up out of the stairwell and onto the archive floor. Dust still clung to it in pale grey drifts, sliding from its shoulders with each step. Its face was a smooth, featureless plate.

<br/>

In the center of its chest, a fist-sized crystal pulsed a deep, steady crimson.

<br/>

It did not look at them. It stopped at the edge of the archive and stood motionless while a thin red line of light swept out from its faceplate, scanning across the room—over the shelves, the stacks, the tables, the leaning tower of plates—like a lighthouse beam made of a single, precise thread.

<br/>

The red line passed over Lyra, crouched behind a crate. It didn't pause.

<br/>

It passed over Mark, behind a shelf. It didn't pause.

<br/>

It passed over the back row of the room—and stopped on the old cases.

<br/>

The construct turned and walked toward them.

<br/>

It didn't hurry. It walked straight down the central aisle, ignoring everything else, reached the old iron-bound cases, and tore the lid of the first one off its hinges with a single, effortless motion.

<br/>

It reached inside and lifted out a First Era plate. The red scanning line flickered over it once.

<br/>

Then it closed its broad, flat hand and crushed the plate into a lump of silvery scrap, as easily as Mark might crumple a sheet of paper.

<br/>

"No," Lyra whispered, horror in her voice. "No, no, no—"

<br/>

The construct dropped the ruined plate, reached into the case, and lifted out another.

<br/>

Mark stared at it, his mind running at full speed.

<br/>

*"It's not a guard,"* he realized. *"Guards protect things. This isn't protecting anything. It's scanning, identifying, and deleting. It found unsanctioned code running on the network and traced it back to the source files."*

<br/>

"It's antivirus," he said aloud. "It's quarantining the source code. It's going to destroy every original plate in this room."

<br/>

The construct crushed the second plate.

<br/>

There were maybe forty left in the three cases. Forty pieces of evidence. The only proof that Guild magic had ever worked any other way.

<br/>

Mark didn't think. He stepped out from behind the shelf, sank into his Bow stance, thumbed his wristband's switch to Alpha, and fired an Impact Macro straight at the construct's back.

<br/>

*Pop!*

<br/>

The invisible pulse hit the construct square between the shoulder blades.

<br/>

It did nothing. It didn't scorch the metal. It didn't even raise the dust. The pulse simply dispersed across the silver-white armor like a wave breaking on a sea wall.

<br/>

But the construct stopped.

<br/>

Very slowly, it straightened. The red scanning line swept back across the room—and this time, when it passed over Mark, it locked. The line pinned itself to his right wrist, to the faint blue glow leaking from Prototype 2.0 beneath his sleeve.

<br/>

For the first time, the construct turned to face him.

<br/>

*"Ah,"* Mark thought. *"So that's how it sees."*

<br/>

It came at him.

<br/>

It was fast. Faster than anything that big had any right to be. It crossed twenty feet of archive floor in three long, silent strides and brought one flat hand down in a crushing overhead blow.

<br/>

Mark stepped *in*, the way he had against Garek. He brought both forearms up in a stacked cross-block, dropped his rear heel, and drove the impact straight down through his skeleton into the stone floor.

<br/>

The flagstones cracked under his boots. His forearms went numb to the elbow. But the blow didn't crush him. The root held.

<br/>

For one fraction of a second, Mark thought he had it. Against Garek, this was the moment the bigger man reeled backward, off-balance, his strike bounced back into his own frame.

<br/>

The construct didn't reel. It didn't lose its balance at all. It simply stepped through, as smoothly as if the block had never happened, and backhanded him with its other hand.

<br/>

Mark flew sideways, crashed over the top of a worktable, and landed in a heap behind it in a clattering avalanche of bronze discs.

<br/>

He lay there for a moment, staring up at the ceiling through a haze of dust, trying to remember how lungs worked.

<br/>

"*Mark!*" Lyra hissed from somewhere across the room.

<br/>

"Structural analysis," Mark wheezed, from under a pile of plates. "It does *not*... have bad balance."

<br/>

The red scanning line swept over the top of the table, searching.

<br/>

Mark rolled, scrambled on hands and knees between two shelving units, and ran.

<br/>

---

<br/>

The next ten minutes were a game of hide-and-seek in which losing meant being folded in half.

<br/>

The construct stalked the aisles of the archive in total silence, its red scanning line sweeping back and forth ahead of it in long, slow arcs. It moved methodically, aisle by aisle, the way a careful engineer works through a stack trace. It never rushed. It didn't need to.

<br/>

Mark kept ahead of it by keeping shelves between them, ducking, doubling back, moving whenever the scanning line swept away. Twice it came within a few feet of him. Both times he only escaped by throwing himself flat and crawling.

<br/>

On the third pass, he found himself crammed into a narrow gap between two shelves. Lyra was already squeezed in on the other side, clutching a modern Guild plate to her chest like a breastplate.

<br/>

"It's following you," Lyra whispered fiercely. "Not me. *You.*"

<br/>

"I noticed."

<br/>

"No—listen. It walked *right past me*, Mark. Twice. It was two feet away. I was holding this—" she held up the plate in her hands, a scratched, ordinary kinetic-lift template with the usual pinched return channel "—and the scanning light went straight over me like I wasn't there."

<br/>

Mark looked at the Guild plate. He looked at his own wrist, where the wristband's twin crystals still glowed faintly through the linen.

<br/>

The pieces fell into place with a cold, nasty click.

<br/>

"It can't see dirty circuits," he said slowly. "Only clean ones. Every Guild plate in this room has the Restriction cut into it. Noisy. Lossy. Rate-limited. It's all *sanctioned* traffic. It's invisible to the scanner." He held up his right arm. "But this? Zero-loss. Grounded. Clean. To that thing, my wristband is a lighthouse."

<br/>

Lyra stared at him. "So your hardware is—"

<br/>

"My entire tech stack," Mark said bleakly, "is a tracking device."

<br/>

"And the Guild's terrible magic—"

<br/>

"—is the only thing in this room that's safe. Yes."

<br/>

Lyra closed her eyes. "Five hundred years of bad magic," she whispered, "and it turns out it was *camouflage*."

<br/>

"I'm going to need some time to process that," Mark agreed. "Later. Assuming there is a later."

<br/>

Somewhere behind them, the red scanning line swept closer.

<br/>

Mark thought fast. If it hunted clean signals, then a clean signal was also bait.

<br/>

"I need a First Era plate," he whispered. "Just one."

<br/>

Lyra gave him a look of pure horror. "You want to *wave one at it*?"

<br/>

"I want to set a trap. A honeypot. A fake target that looks more interesting than we do." He nodded toward the far end of the room, across from the stairs. "We put a plate on a table over there and run a trickle through it. It goes for the plate. We go for the door."

<br/>

"The door is *barred*."

<br/>

"Bars are made of iron," Mark said. "Iron conducts. If I get enough current into it, I can heat the bracket until the bolts give."

<br/>

"You've *done* that before?"

<br/>

"I've done something kind of like that before," Mark said, "to a boot."

<br/>

Lyra looked at him for one long second. Then she reached into her apron, pulled out a First Era plate, and pressed it into his hand.

<br/>

Mark stared at it. "You *took* one?"

<br/>

"Last night," Lyra said. "When we were locking up. I wanted to study the cut." She glared at him defiantly. "Don't judge me. You put mana-crystals in a cheese."

<br/>

"No judgment," Mark said. "Genuinely. You're amazing."

<br/>

---

<br/>

The honeypot almost worked.

<br/>

Mark crept to the far end of the archive, set the First Era plate on a stone table, wired it to the end of a long silver lead, and trailed the wire back behind three rows of shelving. When the construct's scanning line swung the other way, he touched the wire to his cuff and fed the plate the gentlest trickle he could manage.

<br/>

Across the room, the ancient plate lit up silver-white, soft and clean and beautiful.

<br/>

The construct's head snapped toward it instantly. It abandoned its search and strode straight down the aisle toward the light.

<br/>

"*Go,*" Mark hissed.

<br/>

They ran. Up the spiral stairs, two at a time, past the first landing, up to the iron door at the top.

<br/>

Mark pressed his palm flat against the door's crossbar bracket, felt for the bolts, and fired his heat macro straight into the iron.

<br/>

The bracket started to glow. Dull red. Then brighter. The smell of hot metal filled the narrow stairwell.

<br/>

Below them, there was a crunch, the sound of a silver-white hand closing on a thousand-year-old plate.

<br/>

And then, silence.

<br/>

Mark kept pouring heat into the iron. The bracket was glowing orange now. One of the bolts was starting to sag in its socket.

<br/>

"Mark," Lyra said quietly.

<br/>

"Almost—"

<br/>

"*Mark.*"

<br/>

He looked down.

<br/>

At the bottom of the spiral staircase, a red scanning line was sweeping slowly up the stone steps toward them.

<br/>

It had found the bait, crushed it, and looked up for the next nearest clean signal. And Mark was standing on a staircase, pouring a clean, grounded heat macro into an iron bar.

<br/>

"Ah," Mark said.

<br/>

The first silver-white foot appeared on the bottom stair.

<br/>

"Okay," Mark said, letting go of the glowing bracket. "Plan A has failed. Moving on to Plan B."

<br/>

"What's Plan B?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

"I'm working on it," Mark said. "Run."

<br/>

---

<br/>

They didn't have far to run. There was only one place to go: back down into the archive, past the construct, as it turned on the narrow staircase to follow them.

<br/>

Mark went first, vaulting the banister at the first landing and dropping six feet onto the archive floor. Lyra came right behind him, swinging down off the railing with the agility of someone who had spent years climbing workshop scaffolding. The construct pivoted on the stairs above them, its scanning line sweeping, its long limbs folding as it began to descend.

<br/>

They ducked behind the leaning tower of plates in the back corner—the enormous, precariously stacked column of brass and bronze, taller than Mark, held upright by what appeared to be a broom and optimism.

<br/>

Mark pressed his back against the shelf beside it, breathing hard, and forced himself to think.

<br/>

He couldn't outrun it. He couldn't out-hit it. He couldn't hide from it as long as he wore the wristband, and without the wristband, he couldn't do anything at all.

<br/>

He looked at the red crystal pulsing in the construct's chest as it reached the bottom of the stairs.

<br/>

And something clicked.

<br/>

"It's a mana-crystal," he breathed.

<br/>

Lyra looked at him. "What?"

<br/>

"Its core. It's a mana-crystal. The same lattice as Torren's dead glass, just much bigger." He was talking fast now, the way he always did when a production outage finally gave up its root cause. "It has to be. It's been dormant for five hundred years, Lyra. *Grey.* Dead. Then the chime woke it up, and it started pulling Aether out of the lamps to charge itself back up. It's not magic. It's a battery."

<br/>

"So?"

<br/>

"So I can charge a dead crystal," Mark said. "I've done it a dozen times. Which means I know exactly how to do the opposite." He held up his right hand. "Reverse the trickle. Reverse the polarity. Instead of pushing current *into* the lattice, I pull it all out. Every drop. Down to zero."

<br/>

Lyra stared at him. "You want to *drain* it."

<br/>

"I want to drain it."

<br/>

"How?"

<br/>

"That's the part I'm working on," Mark admitted. "I need two things. First, a conductive path straight into the core. I can't reach it through the armor; I'd need a wire touching the crystal directly." He swallowed. "Second, a ground big enough to dump five hundred years of stored charge into without it coming back through me."

<br/>

Lyra was quiet for a moment.

<br/>

Then she reached for her calipers.

<br/>

She leaned around the edge of the shelf, peering at the construct as it stood at the foot of the stairs, its scanning line sweeping slowly across the room. Then she snapped the calipers open, held them up at arm's length, and sighted along them at the construct's chest, the way a surveyor sights along a level.

<br/>

"What are you doing?"

<br/>

"Measuring," Lyra said.

<br/>

"From *here*?"

<br/>

"Hush." She squinted. Adjusted. Squinted again. "There's a seam in its chest plate. Running vertically, right beside the crystal mount. The armor's in two halves and they don't quite meet." She lowered the calipers. "The gap's two-tenths of a millimeter. Maybe two and a half."

<br/>

Mark stared at her.

<br/>

"Two-tenths of a millimeter," Lyra repeated, a strange, fierce little smile tugging at her mouth. "Same as your trace on Torren's blower disc. The one I yelled at you about. Off by two-tenths of a millimeter." She tucked the calipers away. "I can get a graver into a two-tenths gap. And if I can get a graver in, I can thread a silver wire in behind it, right onto the face of the crystal."

<br/>

"That's your conductive path," Mark said slowly.

<br/>

"That's your conductive path," Lyra agreed. "What about your ground?"

<br/>

Mark looked up.

<br/>

Above them loomed the leaning tower: thousands upon thousands of brass, bronze, silver, and copper plates. Hundreds of pounds of conductive metal in a single precarious pile, held upright by a broom.

<br/>

"Eleven thousand metal plates," he said softly. "The best ground in the building."

<br/>

Lyra followed his gaze up the tower. Then down to the broom at its base. Then across the room, to the construct.

<br/>

Mark watched her put the plan together in her head. He could actually see her doing it.

<br/>

"So," Lyra said, very calmly. "Let me make sure I understand. I climb the shelf. I kick out the broom. I drop a tower of eleven thousand plates on a seven-foot murder statue. Then I climb on top of the murder statue, jam a graver into its chest, and thread a wire onto its heart. And while I am doing that, you stand somewhere very obvious, glowing, so it chases you to exactly the right spot."

<br/>

"Correct."

<br/>

"And then you stand still. And hope."

<br/>

"Correct."

<br/>

Lyra looked at him.

<br/>

"When you say it like that," Mark admitted, "it sounds bad."

<br/>

"When you say it *any* way," Lyra said, "it sounds bad."

<br/>

The construct's scanning line swept across the shelf beside them. It hesitated. Then it began to turn toward the corner.

<br/>

Lyra took a deep breath, shoved Cheese the Third into her apron, and started to climb.

<br/>

---

<br/>

Mark stepped out into the open.

<br/>

He walked to the center of the floor, directly in front of the leaning tower, and stopped. Then he deliberately pushed his sleeve up, exposing Prototype 2.0, and thumbed the switch to Alpha.

<br/>

The twin crystals flared bright, clean blue.

<br/>

The construct's head snapped toward him. The red scanning line locked onto his wrist like a hook.

<br/>

"Hey," Mark called. "Over here. Unsanctioned code. Running on your network. In plain sight."

<br/>

The construct came.

<br/>

It wasn't stalking now. It came straight at him, fast and silent, its long arms already rising. Mark sank into his root, dropped his heel, and met the first blow with a sweeping Kung Fu deflection, catching the descending forearm on the outside and guiding it past his shoulder, down, away.

<br/>

The force hammered through his frame and into the flagstones. The floor cracked. Mark's arms screamed.

<br/>

He gave one step back. Then another. Each step pulled the construct a little further forward, a little closer to the base of the tower.

<br/>

The second blow came sideways, a sweeping backhand. Mark ducked under it and felt it part his hair. The third came straight down. He caught it in a cross-block, rooted, and felt something in his left forearm creak ominously.

<br/>

*"Two more steps,"* he thought, through a haze of numbness. *"Just two more."*

<br/>

He took one back. The construct followed.

<br/>

He took another. The construct followed. It was standing right at the foot of the tower now, looming over him, its red core blazing, its hand drawing back for a blow that Mark wasn't sure his arms could take.

<br/>

"*Lyra!*" Mark shouted. "*NOW!*"

<br/>

High above, at the very top of the shelving unit, Lyra planted both boots against the broom handle and kicked.

<br/>

The broom snapped free.

<br/>

For one long, suspended heartbeat, the leaning tower of plates hung in the air, swaying gently, as if surprised to have lasted this long.

<br/>

Then gravity got involved.

<br/>

The entire tower came down in a roaring avalanche of brass, bronze, silver, and copper—eleven thousand plates pouring down onto the construct like a metal waterfall, burying its legs, its torso, its arms, the deafening crash of it echoing off the stone walls like the end of the world. When the clatter finally settled, the construct was pinned in a mountain of metal up to its chest, only its head, shoulders, and one arm still free.

<br/>

And the red core was still pulsing.

<br/>

The free arm began to push against the pile. Plates shifted and slid. The construct began, slowly and inexorably, to rise.

<br/>

Lyra didn't wait. She dropped from the top of the shelf straight onto the heap of plates, skidded down the shifting pile on her heels, and landed directly against the construct's chest.

<br/>

She had her graver out before her feet were set.

<br/>

She jammed the fine steel point into the seam beside the crystal mount. It slid in exactly as far as she said it would, two-tenths of a millimeter of clearance, no more, no less. She levered it, twisted, and with her other hand fed the end of the silver wire into the gap behind it, threading it in by feel, her face inches from the pulsing red core.

<br/>

The construct's free arm swung around toward her. Its broad, flat hand opened.

<br/>

"*Got it!*" Lyra shouted. "*Mark, it's on the crystal!*"

<br/>

The hand began to close toward her head.

<br/>

Mark grabbed the other end of the silver wire and slammed it against the copper plate of his wristband. He planted his feet on the edge of the metal pile so his boots were touching the plates, connecting himself to eleven thousand pieces of conductive ground.

<br/>

His thumb found the switch.

<br/>

Alpha wasn't enough. He knew it the way he knew the sound of a server about to fail. A single crystal didn't have the draw to pull five hundred years of charge out of a core that size. He needed both crystals. He needed Beta.

<br/>

And Beta meant another ping. Another frost. Another alarm on the Anchor, louder than the last.

<br/>

*"Sometimes,"* Mark thought, *"you have to take down production to save production."*

<br/>

He slid the switch to Beta.

<br/>

*Click.*

<br/>

He reversed the trickle.

<br/>

---

<br/>

Everything went white.

<br/>

The air in the archive froze in a single instant. Frost exploded outward from the construct's chest in a ring of white crystal that raced across the metal pile, across the floor, up the shelves, and across the ceiling. Mark's breath turned to ice in front of his face. Every lamp left in the room blazed briefly blue, then went out.

<br/>

The chime came. Not low this time. The high, piercing fourteen-kilohertz ring from his shed, shrieking through the stone, through his skull, through his teeth.

<br/>

And the current flowed—*backward*. It poured out of the construct's core, through Lyra's wire, through his wristband's silver traces, past his rooted feet, and down into eleven thousand plates, which hissed and crackled and steamed with frost as they swallowed it whole.

<br/>

Mark watched the red core in the construct's chest.

<br/>

It drained. Not all at once. In stages, the way Mark had watched a thousand battery icons drain on a thousand dying phones in airport departure lounges. A deep, blazing crimson. Then a dimmer red. Then a dull rust color. Then a weak, flickering pink.

<br/>

The construct's hand was still closing toward Lyra. Slower now. Much slower. Its fingers trembled.

<br/>

Lyra didn't move. She stood pressed against the construct's chest with her graver still wedged in the seam, staring up at the hand closing toward her face. She didn't flinch. She didn't let go.

<br/>

The core faded to a flicker. Then a faint, hesitant glow.

<br/>

Then nothing.

<br/>

Grey. Dull, opaque, slate grey.

<br/>

The construct's hand stopped an inch from Lyra's face.

<br/>

It did not move again.

<br/>

The chime faded into silence. The frost began, slowly, to drip. In the darkness, there was only the soft crackle of thawing metal, the slow drip of melting ice, and two people breathing very hard.

<br/>

Mark let go of the wire. His legs gave out, and he sat down abruptly on the edge of the metal pile.

<br/>

Lyra stayed exactly where she was for several long seconds, staring at the frozen hand an inch from her nose. Then, very carefully, she ducked out from under it, slid down the heap, and sat down heavily beside him.

<br/>

For a while, neither of them said anything.

<br/>

Then Mark looked up at the construct, towering silent and dark above them, its outstretched hand frozen forever in a reach that hadn't quite made it.

<br/>

He leaned over and patted it gently on the arm.

<br/>

"Have you tried turning it off," he said, "and on again?"

<br/>

Lyra turned and stared at him.

<br/>

Mark considered the construct for a moment longer.

<br/>

"Actually," he said, "we'll skip the 'on again.'"

<br/>

Lyra started laughing. It was the high, slightly hysterical laugh of someone who had nearly been crushed by a thousand-year-old automaton and was only just beginning to process it. Mark started laughing too, which hurt his ribs, which made him laugh harder, which hurt more. They sat there together in the dark on a pile of eleven thousand frosted metal plates, laughing until they couldn't breathe.

<br/>

---

<br/>

Eventually, the laughter subsided into exhausted silence.

<br/>

Lyra fumbled in her apron, pulled out Cheese the Third, which was slightly frozen but otherwise intact, broke it in half, and handed one piece to Mark.

<br/>

Mark unwrapped both sausages, handed one to her, and ate the other in four bites.

<br/>

"Power budget," he said, chewing.

<br/>

"Power budget," Lyra agreed, chewing.

<br/>

Lyra looked up at the construct. "I'm naming it," she said.

<br/>

"Please don't name it."

<br/>

"Gerald," Lyra said firmly.

<br/>

Mark stared at her. "Gerald's dead."

<br/>

"*Exactly,*" Lyra said, with deep satisfaction.

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth to argue, then closed it. He found, on reflection, that he had no counterargument whatsoever.

<br/>

He looked down at his forearms. They were bruised black and purple from wrist to elbow, and the left one throbbed every time he moved it. Lyra's palm was bleeding where the graver had slipped. They were both covered in frost, dust, and a fine layer of metallic grit.

<br/>

"Thank you," Mark said quietly. "You saved my life. That measurement—two-tenths of a millimeter, from across a room, under pressure—I couldn't have done that. Nobody I've ever worked with could have done that."

<br/>

Lyra looked at her bleeding hand for a moment. Then she shrugged with elaborate casualness.

<br/>

"You'd have figured something out," she said. "Probably something to do with a boot."

<br/>

---

<br/>

At a quarter past midnight, a scraping sound echoed down the stairwell. A crossbar lifted from its brackets. A hinge creaked.

<br/>

Mark and Lyra looked up, tensing.

<br/>

Archivist Mott shuffled slowly down the stairs, holding a candle. He was wrapped in his grey wool layers and wearing an expression of mild, sleepy irritation.

<br/>

"Forgot my scarf," he announced to no one in particular.

<br/>

He reached the bottom of the stairs and stopped.

<br/>

His candle lit the archive in a small, wavering circle: the crushed and broken shelves, the cracked flagstones, the frost dripping from the ceiling, the mountain of eleven thousand scattered plates. And looming at the center of it all, the seven-foot, silver-white ancient automaton, frozen mid-reach, its dead grey heart dark in its chest.

<br/>

Mott took all of this in very slowly. He raised his candle higher. He squinted through his thick spectacles at the construct's blank, featureless faceplate.

<br/>

"Ah," Mott said warmly. "Are you one of the new ones?"

<br/>

The construct did not answer.

<br/>

"Lovely," Mott said. He shuffled past it, retrieved his scarf from the back of his chair, wound it carefully around his neck, and shuffled back toward the stairs. On the first step, he paused and glanced back over his shoulder at the wreckage.

<br/>

"Bit loud tonight," he remarked mildly. "Mind you lock the old cases."

<br/>

He climbed slowly out of sight.

<br/>

Mark and Lyra sat in silence for a long moment.

<br/>

"The door," Lyra said. "He opened the door."

<br/>

"He opened the door," Mark agreed.

<br/>

"Julian barred it, and Mott just..." She waved a hand.

<br/>

"He came back for his scarf."

<br/>

"We nearly *died*," Lyra said, with growing outrage, "and we could have just waited for Mott to *forget his scarf*."

<br/>

---

<br/>

On their way out, Mark stopped at the top of the stairs and examined the iron crossbar where it leaned against the wall.

<br/>

Caught on the rough edge of one bracket was a single silver thread, fine and expensive, the kind used in noble embroidery. And pressed into the soft iron near the latch, where someone had leaned on it hard while dropping the bar, was a faint, small indentation.

<br/>

The imprint of a signet ring.

<br/>

Mark held the lamp close.

<br/>

The impression showed a circle, bisected by a single horizontal line.

<br/>

Lyra saw it too. Her face went pale.

<br/>

"That's the seal," she whispered. "From the decree. The Concord's seal."

<br/>

"It is," Mark said quietly.

<br/>

He thought of Julian in the corridor, turning his ring over and over, its stone glowing red. *We are the Keepers of the Measure. We have kept it for five hundred years.*

<br/>

"Julian isn't just a spoiled rich kid," Mark said. "He's a stakeholder."

<br/>

---

<br/>

It was nearly two in the morning when Mark finally sat at the desk in his narrow dorm room, his forearms wrapped in strips of torn linen, his back very firmly against the wall.

<br/>

In the corner of the ceiling, the lentil spider was still on its web. Mark looked up at it.

<br/>

"You'd have been useless tonight," he told it.

<br/>

The spider did not respond.

<br/>

"That's fine," Mark said. "So was I, mostly. Lyra did all the hard parts."

<br/>

He opened his leather-bound ledger, picked up his charcoal stick in fingers that still didn't quite want to close, and wrote in slow, slightly shaky English cursive:

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 12: Security Incident — Hostile Automated Sanitation Unit*
> *Incident Summary:*
> *Locked in Archive Level 1 by Julian Thorne at 21:15. Hostile automated construct (designation: Gerald II, per Lyra) ascended from below the third landing, drained wall lamps to power itself, and began destroying First Era source plates. Approx. 3 of 40 originals lost (2 crushed in cases, 1 used as decoy).*
> *Root Cause:*
> *Unsanctioned execution of an original First Era template (Log 11). Construct appears to be an automated enforcement process, an antivirus for the Measure.*
> *Key Technical Findings:*
> *1. Construct detects clean (zero-loss) circuits only. Guild-standard 'restricted' magic is invisible to it. The Restriction is not just a throttle. It's camouflage.*
> *2. Construct is powered by an oversized mana-crystal core. Same lattice as standard crystals.*
> *Resolution:*
> *Lyra measured a 0.2mm seam in the chest armor from across the room (!!), inserted graver and silver lead onto the core. Fallen plate tower used as ground. Reverse-polarity trickle drain via Prototype 2.0 (Beta mode). Core drained to 0%. Construct offline.*
> *Side Effects:*
> *Beta mode used. 14 kHz ping and thermal vacuum triggered. Anchor alert almost certain. Expect company by morning.*
> *Damage:*
> *My forearms. Lyra's hand. Archive floor. Archive shelving. The broom. Mott's sense of order (unaffected; possibly immune).*
> *Stakeholder Update:*
> *- Julian Thorne: Member of House Thorne, self-described 'Keepers of the Measure.' Ring bears the Concord seal (circle bisected by a line). Knew the construct existed. Locked us in deliberately. Upgrade status from 'rival' to 'threat actor.'*
> *- The Concord: Not Probably Legal. Probably Thorne.*
> *Lessons Learned:*
> *1. Lyra is better at this than me.*
> *2. Do not tell Lyra.*
> *3. Do not, under any circumstances, recharge Gerald.*
> *4. Always bring snacks.*

<br/>

He read it over once. Then, after a moment's thought, he went back to Lesson Learned #2, crossed it out, and wrote beside it: *Too late. She definitely knows.*

<br/>

Mark closed the ledger, unbuckled his wristband with careful, aching fingers, and lay back on his bed, staring at the dark ceiling.

<br/>

Somewhere above him, the Academy slept. Somewhere below him, a seven-foot silver statue stood frozen in a ruined archive, its heart gone grey. And somewhere further down still, in a vault nobody had opened in five hundred years, something had just noticed that one of its processes had stopped responding.

<br/>

*"Root cause identified,"* Mark thought, closing his eyes. *"Threat actor identified. Incident contained."*

<br/>

He paused.

<br/>

*"For now."*

<br/>

### *Stinger: Deep Vault 3 — Oakhaven Chapterhouse*

<br/>

Fifty feet beneath the Grand Amphitheater, the obsidian pillar of the Aether Anchor pulsed red in the darkness.

<br/>

The red glyph that had flared to life only days ago was still burning steadily on its face. Beneath it, three new lines of First Era script etched themselves into the stone, one after another:

<br/>

`[SANITATION UNIT 04-B: NO RESPONSE]`

<br/>

`[SANITATION UNIT 04-B: OFFLINE]`

<br/>

`[ESCALATING...]`

<br/>

The script faded. For a long moment, the pillar was still.

<br/>

Then, for the first time in five hundred years, a second glyph beside the first flickered, sputtered, and slowly began to glow.

<br/>

It was not red.

<br/>

It was gold.
