## Chapter 25: Undocumented Dependencies

<br/>

On the Monday after the tournament, Mark discovered that he had become famous, and that he hated it.

<br/>

It began at the Academy gate, where two first-years he had never met held the door for him and then fled before he could thank them. It continued in the corridor outside the Grand Amphitheater, where conversations stopped when he walked past and started again, louder, the moment he'd gone. By the time he reached his seat in the top corner of the highest row, someone had carved a small, careful picture into the bench beside it: a fish, holding a hammer.

<br/>

*"Brand recognition,"* Mark thought grimly, sitting down. *"Unsolicited. Uncontrolled. Exactly the kind of visibility you never want when a regulator is watching."*

<br/>

Archmage Ignis lectured for an hour on the Eleven Sacred Cadences of Thermal Binding. He did not look at the top row once. Mark had the distinct impression that this took a great deal of effort.

<br/>

But it wasn't the attention that bothered Mark most that morning.

<br/>

It was the empty seat in the front row.

<br/>

Julian's place, at the end of the bench nearest the lectern, where he had sat every day since Mark arrived, straight-backed and immaculate, his polished silver staff resting against his knee, was empty. Nobody had taken it. Nobody had even put a satchel on it. The noble students sat around the gap the way people stand around a hole in a road.

<br/>

When the bell rang, Mark took the long way out, past the practice yard. Tobin Pell was waiting for him behind a pillar, as Mark had suspected he would be.

<br/>

"I'm reporting," Tobin whispered, looking over both shoulders at once. "That's what I'm supposed to do now, isn't it? Pip said I report to you. I've never reported anything in my life. Am I doing it right?"

<br/>

"You're doing it exactly right," Mark said. "What have you got?"

<br/>

Tobin swallowed. "Julian's gone."

<br/>

"Gone where?"

<br/>

"The night of the tournament. After the—" Tobin made a small gesture that was meant to suggest a silver staff exploding in a public arena. "A carriage came to the West Wing gate. After midnight. Black, with the circle-and-line on the door. Two men got out. Julian came down the stairs between them." He hesitated. "He wasn't carrying anything. No trunk. No satchel. Not even his cloak."

<br/>

Mark said nothing for a moment.

<br/>

"Did he look hurt?"

<br/>

"He looked," Tobin said, searching for the right word, "*collected*."

<br/>

That evening, Hollis Grey confirmed it. He found Mark at the water pump in the lower courtyard and stood beside him, filling a bucket he didn't need.

<br/>

"His room's still full," Hollis said quietly. "Books. Clothes. That stupid silk handkerchief collection. His mother sends him one every month." He turned off the pump. "Nobody packed. You don't leave for the term without packing. Not even Julian."

<br/>

"Unless you didn't know you were leaving," Mark said.

<br/>

Hollis looked at him. Then he picked up his bucket and walked away without another word, and Mark stood alone at the pump for a long time, thinking about a young man lying on the scorched sand beside a dead staff, trying very hard not to cry in front of the entire Academy.

<br/>

*"He brought them proof,"* Mark thought. *"Against himself."*

<br/>

He took out his clipboard and wrote, under *Threats*: *Julian — status unknown.*

<br/>

Then, after a moment, he crossed out *Threats* and wrote *Concerns*.

---

"Mott," Mark said that night, and wrote the name at the top of a fresh page.

<br/>

They were gathered in the back room of the Fish & Hammer: Mark at the workbench with his clipboard, Lyra perched on her stool with a lens screwed into one eye, Vespera in the good chair with a cup of tea, and Torren leaning in the doorway, where Legacy, from her web in the corner of the frame, appeared to be supervising him. Edna the blower hummed contentedly in the corner. On the bench sat Cheese the Third, with one wedge removed, for power.

<br/>

Under Mott's name, Mark drew a line down the middle of the page and labeled the two columns.

<br/>

*SENILE.* *NOT SENILE.*

<br/>

"We have two weeks until the carriage comes," he said. "Aldric Thorne wants to meet me. He's had five hundred years of a head start on us, and I'd like to walk into that room knowing at least one thing he doesn't think I know." He tapped the page. "And the only person in this entire city who reacted when we said those words out loud was a hundred-and-four-year-old man in a grey scarf."

<br/>

"*Supposedly* a hundred and four," Lyra said.

<br/>

"Supposedly," Mark agreed. "Corwin's number. Which raises the question of where Corwin got it." He picked up his charcoal. "Evidence. Column by column."

<br/>

Under *SENILE*, he wrote:

<br/>

*- Asks if everyone is one of the new ones. Every time. Including people he's seen fifty times. Including Ignis. Including a seven-foot automaton.*

<br/>

*- Eleven years to produce an index that says 'PLATES (various).'*

<br/>

*- Forgets his scarf.*

<br/>

"Strong evidence," Vespera murmured.

<br/>

"Very strong," Mark agreed, and moved to the other column.

<br/>

*- 'Mind you lock the old cases.' (Two days before we found the old cases.)*

<br/>

*- 'It doesn't like noise.' (Two weeks before something that didn't like noise came up the stairs.)*

<br/>

*- Opened a door Julian had barred, to fetch a scarf, walked past a seven-foot construct, and was not attacked.*

<br/>

*- 'Bit of a mystery who did it. Wasn't me.' (Nobody asked him.)*

<br/>

*- Heard 'Advenai… Exori.' Stopped writing. Hands shook for the rest of the shift. Did not ask if we were new.*

<br/>

He set down the charcoal. The left column had three entries. The right column had five, and they were longer.

<br/>

"So it's not senile," said Torren.

<br/>

"It's not *only* senile," Mark said. "That's a different finding. In my experience, the most dangerous component in any old system is the one everyone's agreed is harmless. Nobody audits it. Nobody documents it. It's been there so long it's part of the furniture." He looked around at them. "Mott isn't a person in that archive. He's an undocumented dependency. And I'd like to know what's depending on him."

<br/>

"So we ask him," said Torren.

<br/>

"We tried that," said Lyra. "He asks if we're new."

<br/>

"So we ask him *harder*," Torren said, and cracked his knuckles.

<br/>

"We are not," said Vespera, "tackling a hundred-and-four-year-old archivist."

<br/>

"*Supposedly* a hundred and four," Torren said, a little sulkily.

<br/>

Mark held up a hand. "We do it the way we did the inscription. Triangulation. Several independent sources, each using what they're good at, and we see where the lines cross." He started writing assignments. "I take the records. Every institution writes down who it pays. Lyra, you take his ledger. He writes in it for four hours every night, and none of us has ever looked at a page."

<br/>

Lyra's eyes lit up behind her lens.

<br/>

"Vespera, you know High Imperial better than any of us. I want you to listen to him. Really listen. How he talks, what words he uses, anything that sounds wrong for a man his age." He turned the page. "And Pip's bus watches where he goes when he says he's going for a nap. Because it isn't to bed. I've never once seen him go up past the first landing."

<br/>

"And me?" said Torren.

<br/>

Mark considered him.

<br/>

"You," he said, "can think about the problem from a completely different angle."

<br/>

Torren nodded slowly, with great seriousness. Mark could see the idea arrive behind his eyes like a cart turning into a lane, and he realized, a second too late, that he was going to regret this.

<br/>

"*Goats*," said Torren.

---

The Academy Bursar's office was on the ground floor of the administrative wing, behind a door with three locks, a brass plaque, and a clerk named Fennimore who had worked there for twenty-two years and had never once been asked for anything interesting.

<br/>

Mark walked in at ten o'clock the next morning carrying a brass clipboard.

<br/>

He did not explain himself. He did not produce a writ. He simply stood in front of Fennimore's desk with the clipboard held at a slight angle, the way he'd once held a tablet during a SOC 2 audit, and said, in a voice of quiet, weary authority:

<br/>

"Personnel records. Archives division. Full history. I'll need the payroll ledgers going back as far as you keep them."

<br/>

Fennimore looked at the clipboard.

<br/>

Nobody, in Mark's experience, had ever questioned a brass clipboard. It was the single most reliable security exploit he had discovered in this world, and he had discovered quite a few.

<br/>

"...Of course," said Fennimore, and went to get the key.

<br/>

The payroll ledgers lived in a long, narrow room behind the Bursar's office, stacked floor to ceiling in fat leather volumes, one per year. Mark started with the current one and worked backward.

<br/>

*Archivist, Lower Vaults: Mott. Quarterly stipend: four silver. Status: Unclaimed.*

<br/>

He pulled the volume from the year before. Then the one before that.

<br/>

*Archivist, Lower Vaults: Mott. Quarterly stipend: four silver. Status: Unclaimed.*

<br/>

He went back ten years. Then twenty. Then fifty. The bindings got darker and the ink got browner and the clerks' handwriting changed a dozen times, from neat to cramped to florid and back again, and the line never changed at all.

<br/>

*Archivist, Lower Vaults: Mott. Four silver. Unclaimed.*

<br/>

Ninety years back, the ink had faded to the color of weak tea. A hundred years back, the pages were spotted with damp. A hundred and twenty years back, Mark had to hold the page up to the window to read it.

<br/>

*Archivist, Lower Vaults: Mott.*

<br/>

At a hundred and sixty-one years, the shelves stopped. Fennimore, hovering anxiously in the doorway, explained that there had been a fire. Everything older had burned.

<br/>

"Has anybody ever noticed," Mark asked carefully, "that the same man has been on the payroll for a hundred and sixty years?"

<br/>

Fennimore blinked. "It's a family post, surely," he said. "Mott's a family name. Down in the vaults. Father to son. That sort of thing." He looked uncertain. "Isn't it?"

<br/>

"Has a different Mott ever collected the stipend?"

<br/>

Fennimore looked down at the ledger. He looked at the column. *Unclaimed. Unclaimed. Unclaimed.*

<br/>

"No," he said slowly. "I don't believe anyone ever has."

<br/>

Mark did the arithmetic in his head, the way he used to estimate a vendor's back-billing. Four silver a quarter. Sixteen a year. A hundred and sixty-one years, at minimum. The Provincial Chapterhouse Academy owed its archivist something in the region of two thousand five hundred silver pieces, and had owed it to him, or to someone with his name, since before anyone in the building had been born.

<br/>

He thanked Fennimore and walked out into the corridor and stood there for a while, holding the clipboard against his chest.

<br/>

*"Option one,"* he thought. *"A family of identically named archivists, none of whom have ever needed money, going back at least a hundred and sixty years.*

<br/>

*Option two: one man who doesn't."*

<br/>

He didn't like either answer. He wrote them both down anyway.

---

Lyra's part of the investigation required Mott to leave his desk, which, it turned out, he did every night at almost exactly midnight.

<br/>

"Lovely work," he said, rising slowly from his chair and picking up his candle. He peered at the two of them across the archive. "I'm off for my nap. Lock the old cases."

<br/>

"There aren't any old cases anymore," Mark said. "They were taken."

<br/>

"Lovely," said Mott, and shuffled toward the stairs.

<br/>

Lyra waited until the last scrape of his slippers had faded up the stairwell. Then she counted to sixty under her breath, very fast, and crossed the archive to his desk.

<br/>

The ledger lay open where he'd left it, beside the stub of his quill. It was enormous: a fat, ancient, leather-bound volume, so thick that the spine had been rebound at least twice with mismatched thread. Mott's last line was still faintly wet.

<br/>

Mark looked over her shoulder and read it.

<br/>

*Tuesday. Soup, adequate. Weather mild, by report. Two of the new ones in. Lovely.*

<br/>

He turned back a page.

<br/>

*Monday. Soup, adequate. Weather unremarkable. One of the new ones in, twice. Lovely.*

<br/>

And back another.

<br/>

*Sunday. Soup. Cold upstairs, by report. Nobody new. Quiet.*

<br/>

Mark flipped back twenty pages, then fifty. Soup. Weather. New ones. Lovely. Page after page after page of it, in the same small, cramped hand, as far back as the book went.

<br/>

"It's a diary," Lyra said, deflated. "It's an old man's *soup diary*."

<br/>

"Maybe," Mark said. He reached into his satchel and took out a small wooden box. "Let's check the source."

<br/>

Inside the box were the anti-reading spectacles: thin wire frames, two discs of carefully frosted glass, a pair of hinges Lyra had machined herself. They blurred the edges of any written glyph just enough that the translation layer in Mark's head failed to parse it, while leaving the overall shapes visible. He'd worn them for a week while they decoded the gate inscription. They were also, technically, glasses, and he had not stopped being quietly pleased about that.

<br/>

He put them on and looked down at the page.

<br/>

The soup was gone.

<br/>

In its place was a dense, tightly packed column of First Era script: the same angular, vertically stacked glyphs that covered the oldest plates and the Academy gate. Not a single line of it resembled anything he'd just read. The lines were short and evenly spaced. Each one began with the same small cluster of marks, slightly different each time, and then ran on for three or four glyphs before stopping.

<br/>

Mark took the spectacles off. *Tuesday. Soup, adequate.*

<br/>

He put them back on. Columns of dead script.

<br/>

He took them off again and stood very still beside the desk.

<br/>

"Mark?" Lyra said.

<br/>

"It's not a diary," Mark said quietly. "It's a log file."

<br/>

"A what?"

<br/>

"Look at the structure." He pointed, careful not to touch the page. "Every line starts with a short cluster that changes a little each time. That's a timestamp. A date, or a count. Then a short entry. Then the next line. Same format, every line, for hundreds of pages. That isn't how people write down what they had for lunch. That's how a system writes down what happened."

<br/>

"And the soup?"

<br/>

"The soup," Mark said, "is what my translation layer decided I should see." He looked at the page through the frosted glass, and something cold settled into the pit of his stomach. "Last week we caught it deleting three words out of a gate inscription. This is a whole book. Every line. It's rewriting an entire *ledger*, Lyra. Live. While I watch. And it's making it as boring as it possibly can, so that I'll never look twice."

<br/>

Lyra stared at the ledger, then at him.

<br/>

Then she took a fresh sheet of parchment out of the third pocket of her apron, sat down on the floor beside Mott's desk, and began copying the open page, glyph by glyph, without understanding a single one of them.

<br/>

She worked fast and perfectly. She'd spent four years cutting templates she'd never been allowed to understand; she could copy a shape she couldn't read better than anyone alive. By the time Mark heard the faint, slow scuff of slippers returning down the stairwell, she had two full pages, and the ledger was lying exactly as Mott had left it, open to the same page, the quill beside it at the same angle.

<br/>

Mott shuffled in, set down his candle, and lowered himself into his chair.

<br/>

He looked at his ledger for a long moment.

<br/>

Then he looked at Lyra, standing very innocently by the near shelves with a plate in each hand.

<br/>

"Lovely," he said. And he picked up his quill and wrote one more line.

<br/>

When they left at midnight, Mark looked at it on the way past, without the spectacles.

<br/>

*Tuesday, later. Young lady very interested in soup.*

---

The bus report came in three days later, delivered by Wren in person, because she had decided it was too important for the usual runners.

<br/>

She stood in the middle of the Fish & Hammer workshop, eleven years old, filthy from the knees down, and gave her report with the crisp gravity of a field officer.

<br/>

"Subject leaves the archive at midnight," she said. "Every night. Takes a candle. Goes up the stairs. Doesn't go to the West Wing. Doesn't go to the staff quarters. Doesn't go to bed." She paused for effect, the way she'd seen Pip do. "Goes to the main gate."

<br/>

The workshop went quiet.

<br/>

"He sits on the steps," Wren said. "Right under the big carving. Under the writing. Sits there till about three. Then he goes back down to the archive."

<br/>

"What does he do," asked Vespera slowly, "for three hours?"

<br/>

Wren frowned, trying to get it exactly right.

<br/>

"Mostly nothing," she said. "He looks up at it. At the writing. Sometimes he hums. One night it rained and he didn't even move." She hesitated. "And when he gets up to go, he says something. To the carving. Every night."

<br/>

"What does he say?"

<br/>

"Goodnight," said Wren.

<br/>

Nobody spoke.

<br/>

"That's the report," Wren said. "Message bus, out." She saluted. Torren saluted back without meaning to, then looked down at his own hand in mild betrayal.

<br/>

When she'd gone, Mark sat looking at the clipboard. *Main gate. Under the inscription. Midnight to three. Says goodnight.*

<br/>

The same gate where Julian had stood for an hour in the dark, turning his ring. The same three glyphs that his translation layer refused to let him read.

<br/>

He went to find Corwin, who was exactly where he always was, lying flat on his back on a sun-warmed bench in the Academy courtyard with his arms folded over his chest.

<br/>

"The gate steps," Mark said. "At night. You said it's your second-favorite spot."

<br/>

Corwin didn't open his eyes. "It is."

<br/>

"You've seen Mott there."

<br/>

"Every night for three years." Corwin yawned. "He was there first. Long before me. Ages before me. He's the reason I picked it, honestly. I thought, if an old man finds it comfortable enough to sit on all night, the stone must be very good." He considered. "It is very good."

<br/>

"Has he ever said anything to you?"

<br/>

"Once," said Corwin. "My first week. I lay down on the step beside him, and he looked at me for a long time, and he asked if I was one of the new ones." He was quiet for a moment. "I said yes. I was a first-year. And he said, 'No, you're not.' And he went back to looking at the gate."

<br/>

Mark stood very still.

<br/>

"Did that strike you as odd?"

<br/>

"Everything about Mott strikes me as odd," Corwin said. "I find it restful." He opened one eye and looked at Mark for a long, unhurried moment. "He's never told *you* that, has he?"

<br/>

"No," said Mark.

<br/>

Corwin closed the eye again.

<br/>

"Mm," he said, and that was all.

---

Torren's part of the investigation arrived at the Academy's rear service gate on a Thursday afternoon, on a length of rope.

<br/>

"No," said Mark.

<br/>

"Hear me out," said Torren.

<br/>

"Torren, that's a goat."

<br/>

"That," Torren said, with dignity, "is *Duchess*."

<br/>

Duchess regarded Mark with her yellow, horizontal eyes and chewed something that was not, as far as Mark could see, food. She was no longer charged. She had not floated in nearly three weeks. But there was still, Mark thought, something faintly electric about her personality.

<br/>

"The night the archive was cleaned," Torren said patiently, "the driverless wagon came out of this gate. And the goat driver said his goats wouldn't go near it. Wouldn't go within twenty feet. Pulled at their ropes. Lay down in the road." He patted Duchess's bony head. "Goats know things. Everyone knows that. So." He spread his hands, as if presenting an elegant mathematical proof. "If Mott's part of whatever drove that wagon, Duchess won't go near him. Simple. Goat test."

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth to explain the dozen methodological problems with the goat test, beginning with sample size and ending with the goat.

<br/>

Then he closed it again.

<br/>

He'd seen a lot of diagnostics in his career that were worse than a goat.

<br/>

"Fine," he said. "Goat test."

<br/>

Smuggling a goat down three flights of stairs into a restricted First Era archive turned out to be easier than Mark expected, chiefly because nobody they passed could think of a single regulation that applied. A porter on the second landing opened his mouth, looked at the goat, looked at Mark's brass clipboard, and closed it again.

<br/>

They reached the archive door. Mott sat at his desk, writing slowly.

<br/>

Torren crouched down beside Duchess and unclipped her rope.

<br/>

"Go on, girl," he murmured. "Tell us the truth."

<br/>

Duchess looked around the archive. She looked at the shelves, the stacks of plates, the leaning tower held up by its new broom.

<br/>

Then she trotted straight across the room to Mott's desk, without the slightest hesitation, and stopped beside his chair.

<br/>

Mott looked up. He peered down at her through his thick spectacles for a long, thoughtful moment.

<br/>

"Ah," he said warmly. "Are you one of the new ones?"

<br/>

Duchess considered the question. Then she reached up, took the trailing end of Mott's grey wool scarf delicately between her teeth, and began, slowly and contentedly, to eat it.

<br/>

Mott watched her do this with an expression of great tenderness.

<br/>

"Lovely," he said, and scratched her between the horns.

<br/>

In the doorway, Torren let out a long, slow breath.

<br/>

"Well," he said. "That's that, then."

<br/>

"That's *what*?" said Mark.

<br/>

Torren thought about it, very seriously.

<br/>

"Inconclusive," he admitted. "Goat ate the scarf."

---

By the end of the first week, the clipboard page had grown a third column.

<br/>

Mark had labeled it, after some thought, *OTHER*.

<br/>

*- On the payroll for at least 161 years. Never collected a copper.*

<br/>

*- Keeps a log in First Era script. Translation layer rewrites it as soup.*

<br/>

*- Sits under the gate inscription every night. Says goodnight to it.*

<br/>

*- Told Corwin, 'No, you're not.'*

<br/>

*- Goat: inconclusive.*

<br/>

"We've learned a great deal," Vespera said, studying it, "and none of it is an answer."

<br/>

"That's most investigations," said Mark. "You gather data until the shape of the question changes." He rubbed his eyes. "The trouble is, every method we've used so far treats him like a system. Audit the records. Read the logs. Monitor the traffic. Run a diagnostic." He glanced at Duchess, who had been returned to the Syndicate stables in disgrace and a new scarf. "Sort of."

<br/>

"He *is* a system," said Lyra. "Apparently."

<br/>

"Maybe," said Vespera quietly. "But he's also an old man who sits alone on cold stone in the rain."

<br/>

They both looked at her.

<br/>

She was turning her teacup slowly in her hands, looking at nothing.

<br/>

"I've spent every afternoon since the Infirmary opened in that line in Torren's yard," she said. "Enforcers. Dockhands. Goat handlers. Men who'd hit other men with chairs. Not one of them would have told me anything if I'd asked. But when you take away someone's pain, even for an afternoon, they talk. They can't help it. Brann told me about every chair." She set the cup down. "We've been investigating Mott for a week. Has any of us noticed his hands?"

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth. Then he shut it.

<br/>

He had noticed. That was the thing. He'd been noticing for weeks, without letting himself think about it.

---

Mott's hands were the worst Mark had ever seen.

<br/>

He looked at them properly that night, sitting at the long table in the archive with a plate he wasn't really indexing. The old man's knuckles were swollen into hard, knotted lumps, the fingers bent sideways at the middle joints, like the branches of an old apple tree. He held his quill not between his fingers but wedged into the crook of his palm, gripping it with his whole fist. Every few lines he stopped writing and slowly, carefully, opened and closed his hand, and Mark could see from across the room what it cost him.

<br/>

Mark knew that motion.

<br/>

He had made it himself, every morning for ten years, sitting at his desk in Raleigh before his first cup of coffee, flexing the stiffness out of his knuckles so he could type. He had felt the heat-pad on his wrists at night and the ache in the joints when the rain came in off the pines. He had heard his own fingers clicking against the keys like dry twigs.

<br/>

He looked down at his own hands now: smooth, quick, fourteen years old, and entirely without pain.

<br/>

He hadn't thought about the ache in weeks. He realized, with a small, unpleasant jolt, that he'd forgotten what it felt like.

<br/>

Mott hadn't.

<br/>

"Archivist Mott," Vespera said, crossing the room to his desk. She said it gently, the way she spoke to the patients in the line. "Your hands are cold."

<br/>

Mott looked up at her. He blinked slowly behind his thick spectacles.

<br/>

"Always cold down here," he said. "It's the stone." He considered this. "It wasn't, once."

<br/>

"May I?"

<br/>

He looked at her for a long, uncertain moment. Then, slowly, he set down his quill and held his hands out on the desk, palms up, the way a child holds out its hands to be inspected.

<br/>

Vespera knelt beside his chair. She took both his twisted hands in hers, closed her eyes for a single breath, and her silver bracelet glowed faintly, softly blue.

<br/>

She didn't chant. She didn't reach for her staff. Low draw. Alpha. Gentle, steady, perfectly controlled. Mark felt nothing at all in the air; not even a flicker.

<br/>

The warmth went into Mott's hands slowly, the way it went into the stiff backs in Torren's yard. Mark watched the old man's face change. The faint, permanent tightness around his eyes and mouth, which Mark had always assumed was just age, eased a fraction. Then a little more. His shoulders came down from somewhere near his ears.

<br/>

"*Oh*," Mott said softly.

<br/>

Lyra came over and set something on the desk beside him: one of her grip trainers, the smallest she'd made, a soft roll of oiled leather wrapped around a springy core of ash.

<br/>

"For the mornings," she said. "Squeeze it ten times before you write. It helps. Ask Garek."

<br/>

Mott looked down at it. He picked it up in his warming hand and squeezed it, very slowly, once.

<br/>

"Lovely," he said. And for the first time Mark could remember, the word didn't sound like a habit. It sounded like he meant it.

<br/>

They stayed like that for a while. Vespera, kneeling, holding his hands. Lyra, sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the desk. Mark, at the table, not indexing anything. The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above them, very faintly, a bell rang the hour.

<br/>

"You've been here a long time," Vespera said at last, softly.

<br/>

"Mm," said Mott. His eyes had drifted half-closed.

<br/>

"Longer than anyone."

<br/>

"Mm."

<br/>

"Then you must know the old words," Vespera said, and Mark, at the table, went very still. "Better than anyone. There's a phrase on the gate. Three words. We know two of them." She paused, and said it as gently as she'd ever said anything: "*Advenai*… something… *Exori*."

<br/>

Mott's hands didn't tense in hers. His eyes didn't open.

<br/>

But his lips moved.

<br/>

"*Ulteri*," he murmured.

<br/>

Nobody breathed.

<br/>

"*Advenai Ulteri Exori*," said Mott drowsily, with the faint, fussy irritation of a teacher who has corrected the same mistake a thousand times. "Nobody says it properly anymore. You're all dropping the stress. It's ad-*VEN*-ai. *UL*-teri. You say it like you're reading a laundry list." He sniffed. "Dreadful."

<br/>

Vespera's grip on his hands didn't change. Her voice didn't change. Mark had never admired anyone more.

<br/>

"What does it mean?" she asked. "*Ulteri*?"

<br/>

There was a long pause.

<br/>

Mott opened his eyes. He looked at her, and for just a moment, Mark saw the same thing he'd seen across the archive in the week of the inscription: something sharp and old and focused, deep behind the bottle-bottom glass.

<br/>

"It means," Mott said, "mind you lock the old cases."

<br/>

He drew his hands gently out of hers. He picked up his quill and wedged it into his warm palm.

<br/>

<br/>

He went back to his ledger.

<br/>

Lyra looked at Vespera. Vespera looked at Mark. Mark, very slowly, picked up his charcoal and wrote the word on the corner of his index card, in neat English capitals, and then sat staring at it.

<br/>

*ULTERI.*

<br/>

Vespera got up from the floor. She went back to the table and sat down, and Mark could see her running through every root she knew, every lexicon she'd ever memorized, every scrap of High Imperial grammar drilled into her since she was twelve.

<br/>

After a long while, she shook her head, very slightly.

<br/>

*Nothing.*

<br/>

Not in any dictionary. Not in any spell. Not in any root. Removed from the language so thoroughly that the most educated mage in Oakhaven couldn't find a trace of it.

<br/>

And a hundred-and-sixty-one-year-old archivist had just corrected her pronunciation.

---

They left at midnight, as they always did.

<br/>

Mott was writing slowly, his new grip trainer resting beside the ledger. As Mark passed the desk, the old man looked up and smiled at him, warm and vague.

<br/>

"Are you one of the new ones?"

<br/>

"Yes," said Mark, as he always did.

<br/>

"Lovely," said Mott.

<br/>

Behind Mark, Vespera stopped walking.

<br/>

She didn't say anything. Not on the stairs, not on the first landing, not in the long dark corridor of the West Wing. She walked beside them with her face very pale and her eyes fixed on nothing, and when they reached the outer door and the cold night air, she stopped and took hold of Mark's sleeve.

<br/>

"Say it again," she said. "What he asks you. Every night. Exactly as you hear it."

<br/>

"'Are you one of the new ones?'" said Mark.

<br/>

Vespera closed her eyes.

<br/>

"He has never once," she said quietly, "said the word *new*."

<br/>

Mark stared at her.

<br/>

"I've heard it fifty times," Vespera said. "We all have. He mumbles it, and it sounds like *aduenai*. The courtesy. It's what the Academy calls first-years at their matriculation, 'honored guests,' and it's in the commencement speech too. When an old man in a scarf mumbles it at you in a doorway, everyone hears it as 'new students.' So everyone thinks he's asking if you're new. That's how I heard it. That's how *everyone* has heard it, for a hundred years."

<br/>

She opened her eyes.

<br/>

<br/>

"He's not asking if we're honored guests," said Lyra slowly.

<br/>

"No," said Vespera. "He's asking if we're *Advenai*. 'Those who arrive.' He's been standing at the door of that archive for a hundred and sixty years, asking every single person who walks through it whether they arrived from outside."

<br/>

The night was very quiet. Above them, the two moons hung over the dark spires of the Academy, one green, one silver.

<br/>

"And my translation layer," Mark said, at last, "has been telling me he says 'new ones.'"

<br/>

"Yes."

<br/>

"Since my first night."

<br/>

"Yes."

<br/>

Mark found that he had raised his hand to push his glasses up his nose. There was nothing there. He lowered it.

"It doesn't mean anything," Mark said the next evening.

<br/>

<br/>

The "come alone" problem was debated on the Saturday before the carriage, in the back room of the Fish & Hammer, and it went about as well as every meeting Mark had ever attended in which everyone in the room cared very much about the outcome and nobody was in charge.

From the long bench under the window, where he had been lying with his eyes closed for the past hour, Corwin spoke without moving.

<br/>

Nan Brask stood on the threshold with her knitting bag over one arm and Garek looming behind her like a very large, slightly limping shadow.

<br/>

<br/>

<br/>

"Why?" Mark asked.

<br/>

Nan Brask stopped knitting.

<br/>

She looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment, with the same flat, assessing look she'd given him the day they met, across her knitted organizational chart with the goat on it.

<br/>

She resumed knitting. "Also, you're my business partner. Forty-five percent of you is mine. I protect my investments."

<br/>

<br/>

"No," said Lyra.

<br/>

"Garek wants to come too," Nan added. "I told him no. He's sulking."

<br/>

"I'm not *sulking*," said Garek, from the doorway. He looked at Mark. "I could, though. Come. If you wanted. I'm good in a doorway."

<br/>

Garek frowned.

<br/>

The Gerald core." He nodded toward the locked iron box on the top shelf, the one with *DO NOT CHARGE* painted on the lid in Lyra's neatest capitals. "I need someone in the doorway here. <br/>

"Exactly like that."

Garek nodded, satisfied. Then, a moment later, quietly: "Should I keep doing the leg lifts while I'm standing guard? For the knee?"

The bus-factor problem occurred to Mark on the ninth night, at two in the morning, and once it had occurred to him he couldn't make it go away.

<br/>

He'd been lying in the dark in his narrow dorm room in the West Wing, running through the meeting with Aldric the way he used to run through a hard negotiation the night before, branch by branch, and somewhere in the middle of the worst branch, the one where he didn't come back from the capital at all, he'd thought: *And then what?*

<br/>

He'd sat up.

<br/>

*Then Lyra charges crystals. She can do that. Vespera keeps the Infirmary going. She can do that too. Torren keeps fixing blowers.*

<br/>

*And everything else is in a book nobody in this world can read.*

<br/>

The grounding theory. The trace ratios. The reverse-drain technique. The Concord decree, which now existed nowhere on earth except as Log 11, in English cursive. The full reasoning behind every prototype. What the frost meant. What the chime meant. Why, under no circumstances, was anyone ever to charge Gerald's core.

<br/>

All of it in his ledger. All of it in a language that had died with him, in Raleigh, at 11:42 on a Thursday night.

<br/>

In his old life, they'd had a name for this. The *bus factor*: how many people on a team could be hit by a bus before a project became impossible to continue. A healthy team had a bus factor of four, or five. A dangerous one had a bus factor of two.

<br/>

Project Root Access had a bus factor of one.

<br/>

And Mark knew, better than almost anyone alive in either world, what happened when the one person who understood the system disappeared in the middle of the night without handing anything over. Somewhere in North Carolina, someone had sat down at his desk the following morning, looked at an OAuth pipeline that he alone understood, and tried to explain to Brad why the build had failed.

<br/>

He had never written that handover document. He'd always meant to. There'd always been a deadline first.

<br/>

He got up, lit the lamp, and went to find Lyra.

---

They worked every night for four nights, after his vault shift, in the back room of the Fish & Hammer, with Edna humming in the corner and the lamp turned low.

<br/>

Mark talked. Lyra wrote.

<br/>

He'd tried, at first, to write it himself, and discovered something he hadn't known: the translation layer would let him *read* the local script effortlessly, but when he picked up a quill to write it, his hand produced English every time. So Lyra became the scribe, the way she'd been the logger during the triangulation, setting down everything he said in clean, careful local script, asking questions whenever an idea didn't translate, which was often.

<br/>

"What's a 'circuit'?"

<br/>

"A loop. A path the Aether takes from the source and back again. Every working spell is a loop. Every broken one has a gap in it, or a short across it."

<br/>

"A *loop*," Lyra repeated, writing. "Fine. What's a 'heatsink'?"

<br/>

"Something that drinks heat so you don't have to. Your copper fins. Torren's quench bucket. Vespera, in a pinch."

<br/>

"I'm not writing *Vespera* down as a heatsink."

<br/>

"Footnote, then."

<br/>

Section by section, the document grew. *Grounding: Why and How.* *The Pinched Return Channel (Sacred Restriction): What It Does and Why It Fails.* *Recharging Dead Glass.* *Alpha and Beta Modes: When Each Is Safe (Beta: Never, Unless You Want Company).* *Signs of Construct Activity.* *The Fish & Hammer Infirmary: Standard Protocols (Heat, Grip, Posture, Leg Lifts).* *Message Bus: Codes and Runners.*

<br/>

And, on its own page, at the very end, in letters Lyra underlined twice:

<br/>

*Gerald's Core: Why You Must Not Charge It, Ever, Under Any Circumstances, Even If It Seems Like a Good Idea, Especially If It Seems Like a Good Idea.*

<br/>

On the fourth night, near two in the morning, Lyra set down her pen and flexed her ink-stained fingers and looked at the stack of pages in front of her. Then she looked at him.

<br/>

"You're writing this," she said, "in case you don't come back."

<br/>

Mark didn't answer straight away.

<br/>

"I'm writing it," he said, "because I should always have written it." He looked at the lamp. "In my old job, I was the only one who knew how half the systems worked. For years. I used to think that made me valuable. Indispensable." He shook his head slowly. "It doesn't. It just means that when you leave, nobody else can fix anything. And they have to find out the hard way, in the middle of the night, with everything on fire."

<br/>

Lyra was quiet for a moment.

<br/>

"Did you?" she asked. "Leave?"

<br/>

Mark thought of his desk. The second monitor. The rain on the loblolly pines. The backspace key, held down under the weight of his own forehead.

<br/>

"Not on purpose," he said.

<br/>

Lyra looked at him for a long time. Then she picked up her pen again and, at the top of the first page, above all of it, wrote a title.

<br/>

Mark leaned over to read it.

<br/>

*HOW EVERYTHING WORKS (MARK).*

<br/>

"I was going to call it a runbook," he said.

<br/>

"I don't know what that is," said Lyra. "So I'm not calling it that." She squared the stack of pages and tied them with a strip of leather. "And Torren doesn't get to name it either. I already checked."

His last vault shift before the carriage was very quiet.

<br/>

He indexed eleven plates. He didn't learn anything. He didn't try to. Mostly he sat at the long table in the lamplight and listened to the slow scratch of Mott's quill, and the soft, rhythmic creak, every few lines, of a small leather grip trainer being squeezed ten times.

<br/>

At midnight, he packed his satchel and stood.

<br/>

"Goodnight, Archivist Mott."

<br/>

Mott didn't look up from his ledger.

<br/>

"Mind you come back," he said.

<br/>

Mark stopped in the doorway.

<br/>

He hadn't told Mott about the invitation. Nobody had. He was almost certain of it.

<br/>

He turned around.

<br/>

Mott had set down his quill. He was looking at Mark across the dim archive, over the top of his bottle-bottom spectacles, and his eyes were clear and old and very sharp, and they were not vague in the slightest.

<br/>

"Are you one of the new ones?" he asked.

<br/>

And then, for the first time in all the weeks Mark had known him, he didn't go back to his ledger. He waited.

<br/>

<br/>

He thought about Corwin, lying on the cold gate steps in his first week. *No, you're not.*

<br/>

"...Yes," Mark said.

<br/>

The same answer he'd given every night since the first.

<br/>

<br/>

<br/>

Then he picked up his quill, wedged it into his warm palm, and went back to his ledger.

<br/>

Mark climbed the stairs very slowly, one step at a time.

---

The carriage came at dawn.

<br/>

They were all in the armory yard to see it: Torren, in his good apron; Lyra, with the leather-tied bundle of *HOW EVERYTHING WORKS (MARK)* clutched against her chest; Vespera, hooded, very still; Garek, already planted in the workshop doorway with his shoulders touching the frame; Corwin, who had arrived at some point in the night and was lying on the coal pile, and claimed it was very comfortable. Pip and Wren and half the message bus sat along the top of the yard wall like a row of sparrows.

<br/>

Out on the North Gate road, Mark knew, a convoy of wool goats was already moving, and on one of the wagons sat an errand boy who could run very fast.

<br/>

They heard the carriage before they saw it, and then realized they hadn't heard it at all. That was what was wrong. A carriage made noise. Hooves, harness, iron tires on cobbles, a driver clicking his tongue.

<br/>

This one came up the lane in silence.

<br/>

It was tall and black, lacquered to a mirror shine, with high wheels and dark glass windows. On its door, in silver, was a circle cut by a single line.

<br/>

There were no horses.

<br/>

There was no driver.

<br/>

The bench at the front was empty, its reins neatly coiled and tied off, as if out of politeness. Nothing pulled it. Nothing pushed it. It simply rolled up the lane and through the yard gate and came to a smooth, perfect stop in front of Mark, exactly where a footman would have stood, with a soft hiss like a sigh.

<br/>

In the yard, every animal went quiet. The two Syndicate goats tethered by the Infirmary line, which had been placidly eating a patient's hat, folded their legs and lay down flat in the dirt and would not look at it. In the doorframe of the workshop, Legacy drew all eight legs in tight and backed into the deepest corner of her web.

<br/>

On the top of the yard wall, Pip leaned over to the runner beside him and whispered, "*Gerald*."

<br/>

And the word went down the wall, runner to runner, whispered, *Gerald, Gerald, Gerald*, until it ran out of wall and jumped down into the lane and was gone into the waking city, carried away at the speed of small, bare feet.

<br/>

"That," said Torren hoarsely, "is a very big Gerald."

<br/>

Mark looked at the carriage. At the empty bench. At the silver seal.

<br/>

*Night of the archive cleanup,* he thought. *Driverless wagon. Left the rear gate at three in the morning, riding heavy. Goats refused to approach.*

<br/>

*Well. Now we know whose wagons those are.*

<br/>

Or at least, he corrected himself, which side of the dam they were parked on.

<br/>

The door swung open by itself.

<br/>

Lyra stepped forward. She didn't hug him. She took a small, waxed-paper parcel out of the twelfth pocket of her apron, the one nobody was allowed to ask about, and pressed it into his hand.

<br/>

It was a wedge of cheese.

<br/>

"In case they don't feed you," she said fiercely. "And so you remember to come back and tell me *everything*."

<br/>

"Cheese," said Mark gravely, tucking it into his satchel. "Understood."

<br/>

Vespera put her hand on his shoulder, briefly, and said nothing at all, which from Vespera said a great deal.

<br/>

Torren cleared his throat several times, and then gave up, and saluted.

<br/>

Mark climbed into the carriage. Inside, it was upholstered in dark grey velvet and smelled faintly of cold stone, like the archive. There was no one else in it. On the seat opposite lay a folded blanket, a crystal water carafe, and a small card in the same heavy cream paper as the invitation, bearing two words in an elegant hand:

<br/>

*Do settle.*

<br/>

The door closed by itself with a soft, expensive click.

<br/>

Through the dark glass, he saw them all standing in the yard in the pale dawn: the smith, the engraver, the mage, the enforcer in the doorway, the boy on the coal pile, the sparrows on the wall. His team. Bigger than it had been a month ago. Bigger than any team he'd had in forty years.

<br/>

He raised his hand against the glass.

<br/>

"Message bus," he said quietly, to no one. "Out."

<br/>

The carriage began to move.

<br/>

He closed the ledger and sat back against the grey velvet.

<br/>

Outside the dark glass, the fields were going gold in the early light, and the road ran on ahead of him, very straight, toward the capital. The carriage made no sound at all. There was nothing to hear but his own breathing, and, if he listened very carefully, the faint, distant, crystalline hum of something old moving the wheels.

<br/>

He found that he had raised his hand to push his glasses up his nose.

<br/>

This time, he didn't lower it right away. He sat there in the silent carriage with his fingertips resting on bare skin, where the glasses had been for forty years, and let himself think, just for a moment, the thought he'd been refusing to finish for three nights running.

<br/>

*He asks everyone.*

<br/>

*But he only ever waited for me.*

<br/>

Then he lowered his hand, folded it over the ledger in his lap, and watched the road.
