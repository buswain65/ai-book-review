## Chapter 24: The Practical Exam

<br/>

The notice went up on the Academy's great board on a grey Monday morning, and within an hour, every student in the building had read it twice.

<br/>

*TERM-END PRACTICAL EXAMINATION. Single-elimination dueling tournament. All Advanced Track students to compete. Sanctioned foci only. Bouts conclude by forfeit, ring-out, or the judgment of the presiding Archmage. Faculty, families, and invited guests welcome in the stands.*

<br/>

*Presiding: Archmage Ignis.*

<br/>

Mark read it standing in the crowd in front of the board, his satchel over his shoulder. Then he read it again, slowly, the way he used to read a contract clause that a vendor's lawyer had been suspiciously eager for him to sign.

<br/>

*Sanctioned foci only.*

<br/>

*Invited guests welcome in the stands.*

<br/>

"Oh," Mark said quietly. "That's bad."

<br/>

Beside him, a lanky first-year with a pale, anxious face and a mop of dark hair happened to glance over, saw who he was standing next to, and went white.

<br/>

"*Please don't do the thing*," the first-year whispered, and fled.

---

"It's a trap," Mark said that evening, standing in the Fish & Hammer workshop with his clipboard. "It's a beautifully designed trap. I have to compete. If I don't, it's a failed exam, and Ignis gets to have me expelled. The Statute 402 transfer can't cover me if I fail the course on paper." He tapped the clipboard. "And if I do compete, I'll be in a public arena, in front of an audience. And I'll bet my entire Facilities budget that some of that audience will be wearing rings."

<br/>

"Gold ones," said Lyra.

<br/>

"Gold ones. Watching for one clean cast. One. That's all they need. One zero-loss execution in front of witnesses, and they've got everything they've been looking for."

<br/>

"Then don't cast clean," Torren said reasonably.

<br/>

"That's the plan," Mark said. "The problem is that if I don't cast clean, I'm a fourteen-year-old with no staff fighting the best students in the Academy using the worst magic in the Empire. And I can't even use Prototype 2.0. Sanctioned foci only."

<br/>

There was a short silence.

<br/>

"Then you need a sanctioned focus," said Vespera slowly. "One that passes inspection."

<br/>

They all turned and looked at Lyra.

<br/>

Lyra looked back at them. Then she understood what they were asking, and her face fell.

<br/>

"No," she said.

<br/>

"Lyra—"

<br/>

"You want me to make you a *Guild-standard bracelet*," she said, with mounting horror. "With the *Restriction* cut into it. The *pinched return channel*. On *purpose*."

<br/>

"It has to be convincingly bad," Mark said apologetically. "It has to pass a detection rod. It has to look and feel exactly like every other sanctioned focus in that arena."

<br/>

Lyra stared at him the way a master chef might stare at someone who'd asked her to burn the toast. On purpose. As art.

<br/>

"I have spent *weeks*," she said, "learning to cut lines like the First Era masters. Long strokes. Clean channels. Perfect grounding. And you want me to go back to cutting *broken* ones."

<br/>

"Just this once."

<br/>

"I'll *know*," Lyra said darkly. "I'll know it's broken. Every time I look at it."

<br/>

"I know," Mark said. "So will I. For the first time in my entire life, I'm deliberately shipping bad code." He paused. "And I need it to be *really* convincingly bad."

---

It took Lyra one evening. She worked in grim, tight-lipped silence, cutting the Sacred Restriction into a plain silver bracelet with exactly the specified forty-percent narrowing, exactly to Guild template, with the precision of someone performing an operation she found morally objectionable. Every so often she muttered, "This is *wrong*," under her breath. Twice she put the graver down and walked once around the workshop before she could pick it up again.

<br/>

When she was finished, she set it on the bench and pushed it toward Mark without looking at it.

<br/>

"There," she said. "It's terrible. I hope you're happy."

<br/>

Mark picked it up and turned it over. It was flawless. Flawlessly, perfectly, devastatingly bad: a textbook Guild-standard focus, indistinguishable from the thousands of others in the Empire.

<br/>

"It's beautiful," he said sincerely.

<br/>

"Don't," said Lyra.

<br/>

"It's *beautifully* terrible."

<br/>

"I'm going to go and cut something *correctly*," said Lyra, "to cleanse myself."

---

Then came strategy.

<br/>

They sat around the workbench that night, the four of them, with the bracelet in the center and the clipboard beside it.

<br/>

"Here's the thing about this bracelet," Mark said. "It has the same flaw as every Guild focus. Every time I cast through it, the heat builds up in the silver, and the pinched return channel traps it. Too much casting, too fast, and the silver melts and shorts. Exactly like Torren's blowers."

<br/>

"Exactly like Bessie," Torren said, a little mournfully.

<br/>

"Exactly like the *old* Bessie," Mark corrected gently. "Before we fixed her." He tapped the bracelet. "So I have a heat budget. A small one. If I cast too much in a single bout, or too often across the whole tournament, I'll burn it out. Then I'm fighting with nothing."

<br/>

"How many casts do you have?" Vespera asked.

<br/>

"I don't know exactly. Not many. That means I have to fight mostly with Kung Fu and save the magic for when I really need it. And between rounds, I have to cool it down."

<br/>

"How?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

Mark thought about it for a moment. Then he pointed at the water bucket Torren kept by the small forge for quenching hot iron.

<br/>

"Liquid cooling," he said.

<br/>

"*Liquid cooling*," Torren repeated, impressed. He didn't know what it meant, but it sounded expensive.

<br/>

"Very high-end," Mark said. "Everyone serious about performance does it."

<br/>

He turned to the next page of the clipboard.

<br/>

"Now. Julian." He looked around at them. "Julian isn't trying to beat me. If he just wanted to win, he could. He's better at Guild magic than anyone else in the Academy. But Corwin says he said 'Father won't wait much longer.' I think he wants something else. He wants to push me so hard that I break. That I panic and cast clean, right in front of his father's men. That gets them their proof, and he gets to end this before Aldric arrives and does something much worse."

<br/>

The workshop was quiet.

<br/>

"So how do we beat him?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

"We don't," Mark said. "His staff does."

<br/>

He picked up the dirty bracelet and held it up.

<br/>

"Julian's staff is silver and ironwood. Guild standard. It has the exact same flaw as this bracelet and every blower in Torren's scrap bin. Every time he casts, he pumps heat into the silver core, and it stays there. Julian's style is all power and noise. Long chants, maximum force, no rest." Mark set the bracelet down. "So I don't attack. I dodge. I make him miss. I make him try harder. Every attack that misses, he tries harder and casts bigger. And every time he does, the heat in that staff climbs."

<br/>

"Until it melts," said Lyra slowly.

<br/>

"Until it melts," Mark agreed. "In my old job, we had a name for that. A *retry storm*. When a system fails, so it tries again harder, which makes it fail worse, which makes it try harder still. Until the whole thing collapses under its own load." He looked at them. "I don't need to beat Julian. I just need to stay standing long enough for his own staff to give up."

<br/>

"And how will you know when it's about to?" asked Vespera.

<br/>

Mark looked at Lyra.

<br/>

Lyra frowned for a moment, then understood.

<br/>

"The color," she said. "Of the silver. When silver gets hot, it changes. Dull red first. Then cherry. Then orange. I can see it from the stands. I've been reading metal in forges for years."

<br/>

"And when it's about to go?"

<br/>

Lyra thought about it. Then a slow, wicked grin spread across her face.

<br/>

"I'll yell," she said, "*cheese*."

---

The arena had been built into the Academy's eastern grounds centuries ago: a broad, round sand pit, ringed with a low stone wall and ward-lines, surrounded by tiered stone benches that rose steeply on all sides. By the morning of the tournament, every bench was full.

<br/>

Mark walked out of the competitors' tunnel at eight o'clock and stopped for a moment to take in the crowd.

<br/>

The students and faculty filled most of the stands, a bright sea of velvet robes and silver-capped staves. The noble families sat in a private gallery under an awning, sipping wine. Archmage Ignis stood at the judges' table on the edge of the sand in his crimson robes, beard freshly oiled, looking happier than Mark had ever seen him.

<br/>

And then Mark looked up into the stands to his left and nearly laughed out loud.

<br/>

Torren had come. He was sitting in the third row, unmissable, holding a large canvas banner painted, with great enthusiasm and very little skill, with a fish holding a hammer.

<br/>

Around him, filling nearly half a section, sat the patients of the Fish & Hammer Infirmary. Enforcers. Dockhands. Goat handlers. The tannery worker with the bad elbow. Brann, the enforcer with the folded ear. The door guard from the Syndicate warehouse, who could now turn his head to the left again and was doing so constantly, out of pure joy. And in the center of them, Garek, cheering at full volume with both fists in the air and visibly trying not to put any weight on his left knee.

<br/>

Nan Brask sat in the front row of the section, knitting. She did not look up. But Mark had the strong impression she knew exactly where he was.

<br/>

Wren and Pip and three other errand runners were scattered through the crowd, each perched where they could see a different part of the arena.

<br/>

And in the front row of the main student section sat Lyra and Vespera, side by side. Lyra had her lens out already. Vespera was scanning the stands, her face very still.

<br/>

Mark caught her eye. She held up four fingers, very discreetly, against her knee.

<br/>

Four gold rings. Four men in the crowd, watching him.

<br/>

*"Right,"* Mark thought. *"Let's begin."*

---

### Round One: Tobin Pell

<br/>

Mark's first opponent was the lanky, pale first-year from the notice board.

<br/>

He walked out onto the sand with the posture of a man walking to his own execution. When he reached his starting mark and finally looked up at Mark, his face went from white to faintly green.

<br/>

"Competitors ready," Ignis announced. "Begin—"

<br/>

"I forfeit," said the first-year.

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"You haven't *started*," Ignis said.

<br/>

"I know," said the first-year. "I'd like to forfeit before I start. If that's allowed."

<br/>

"It's allowed," said Ignis, through his teeth.

<br/>

The crowd laughed. The first-year turned and walked back toward the tunnel. But as he passed Mark, he paused, fumbled at his belt, and pressed something into Mark's hand.

<br/>

It was a leather water flask.

<br/>

"You'll need it," the first-year whispered. "Everyone's saying Julian's been training for *you*. All week. Before dawn, every day. In the practice yard." He glanced nervously over his shoulder. "I'm Tobin. Tobin Pell. Good luck. *Please* don't do the thing."

<br/>

He was gone before Mark could answer.

<br/>

Mark looked down at the flask in his hand. Then he looked at the tunnel where Tobin had disappeared, and thought: *Kind. Observant. Terrified of everything.* He made a small note on his clipboard.

<br/>

*Tobin Pell: first-year. Notices things. Bring water to strangers. Possible bus recruit. Academy node #2.*

---

### Round Two: Tessa Fenn

<br/>

The second opponent was a different matter.

<br/>

She was a slight, sharp-featured girl of about sixteen, with straw-colored hair cropped short and a plain, patched student robe. In her right hand she carried not a staff but a short, scuffed wooden rod tipped with a chipped and cloudy crystal. It was clearly secondhand, possibly thirdhand.

<br/>

"Tessa Fenn," she said, inclining her head politely. "Scholarship track."

<br/>

"Mark Vance," said Mark. "Coal pit."

<br/>

Something that was almost a smile flickered across her face.

<br/>

"Begin," said Ignis.

<br/>

Tessa didn't chant for two minutes. She didn't scream. She didn't plant her feet and raise her focus overhead in a grand, dramatic stance like the other students.

<br/>

She simply flicked the battered wooden rod toward Mark and spoke three syllables, short and sharp.

<br/>

A bolt of kinetic force cracked across the sand.

<br/>

Mark only barely sidestepped it. It struck the stone wall behind him with a crack and left a pale scuff. Before he'd even recovered, she flicked again. Three more syllables. Another bolt, a little to the left, where he'd been about to step.

<br/>

Mark ducked under it, rolled, and came up in a low stance, his mind suddenly racing.

<br/>

*"She's fast,"* he thought. *"She's very fast. And she's not wasting anything."*

<br/>

Every other student he'd seen cast spent most of their energy on noise: light, sparks, wind, long incantations, dramatic gestures. Tessa didn't. Her casts were short and tight and direct, just enough force to do the job and not a scrap more. She waited a beat between each one to let her rod cool, and he realized she'd *learned* that, through hard experience.

<br/>

*"Her focus is cheap,"* he realized. *"It overheats fast. So she taught herself to cast short and save heat. She's working out efficient casting all on her own. Nobody taught her. She just couldn't afford to do it any other way."*

<br/>

Another bolt. Mark dodged it, but only just, and this time it clipped his sleeve.

<br/>

He couldn't outrun her. She was too fast, too precise, reading his movement and casting where he was going to be. He could use his bracelet, but every cast would cost him heat he'd need for the rest of the tournament.

<br/>

So instead, he started watching her rhythm.

<br/>

Three syllables. Flick. Pause. Three syllables. Flick. Pause. Short, regular, efficient. Each pause the exact same length, as her focus cooled.

<br/>

*"She's predictable,"* Mark thought. *"Because she's efficient. She has to wait the same amount of time between every cast, or her rod overheats."*

<br/>

He waited for the next flick. Dodged left. And the instant the bolt passed him, in the pause, before she could cast again, he moved.

<br/>

He crossed the sand in four quick steps, inside her reach, and caught her wrist in a light, controlled Kung Fu redirection. He didn't twist and he didn't strike. He simply turned her own forward momentum aside, so that her next bolt, already half-spoken, discharged harmlessly into the sand at their feet, and her balance tipped gently over the edge of the ring.

<br/>

Her foot touched the line.

<br/>

"Ring-out," said Ignis, with very poor grace. "Vance."

<br/>

Tessa stood at the edge of the sand for a moment, breathing hard, staring at Mark.

<br/>

Then she laughed.

<br/>

"You waited for my cooldown," she said.

<br/>

"You have a very good cooldown," said Mark sincerely. "Very consistent. That was the problem."

<br/>

"My rod overheats if I don't." She looked down at the cheap, chipped wooden focus in her hand. "I can't afford a proper one. A proper staff costs more than my family makes in a year." She held up the rod, a little self-consciously. "So I learned to make this one last."

<br/>

"You learned to cast efficiently," Mark said. "Without anybody teaching you. That's—" He paused, looking for the right word. "That's *rare*."

<br/>

Tessa flushed slightly, then shrugged. "I send half my stipend home," she said simply. "My mother's got four more at the farm. I can't afford to burn out a focus. So." She tucked the rod into her belt. "Good bout. I nearly singed you, I think. Sorry about your sleeve."

<br/>

"Don't be," Mark said. "It was a good shot."

<br/>

She smiled—a real one, quick and surprised—and walked off toward the tunnel.

<br/>

Mark watched her go, then took out his clipboard and wrote, very carefully:

<br/>

*Tessa Fenn: scholarship. Taught herself efficient casting out of necessity. Natural engineer. Has never heard the word 'engineer.' Watch. Possibly recruit.*

<br/>

Then he went to the bucket at the edge of the sand and quietly dipped his bracelet into the water. It hissed gently.

<br/>

He had used no magic at all in that bout. Not one cast.

<br/>

*"Good,"* he thought. *"Budget intact."*

---

### Round Three: Percival Ashdown

<br/>

The third bout lasted roughly four seconds.

<br/>

Mark's opponent was a tall, broad-shouldered noble student in a scarlet cape, with a staff so enormous and ornate that it looked less like a magical focus and more like a decorative lamppost. He swept onto the sand to loud applause from the noble gallery, struck a heroic pose, and announced, to the entire arena:

<br/>

"*Percival Ashdown*, of House Ashdown, heir to the Western March!"

<br/>

"Mark," said Mark. "Coal pit."

<br/>

"*Begin*," said Ignis.

<br/>

Percival lowered his enormous staff, roared a short incantation, and charged straight across the sand, riding a massive kinetic blast that propelled him forward like a battering ram. The air in front of him blurred with force. The noise was tremendous.

<br/>

Mark stepped two feet to the left.

<br/>

Percival thundered past him at full speed, carried entirely by his own spell. He saw too late that Mark was no longer in front of him. He tried to stop. He couldn't. The spell had all the steering of a runaway cart.

<br/>

He shot straight across the arena, over the ring line, and over the low stone wall, and landed upside down in the front row of the student section, in a tangle of cape, staff, and two very surprised first-years.

<br/>

There was a long, ringing silence.

<br/>

"Ring-out," said Mark helpfully.

<br/>

Ignis's face went through several shades of red.

<br/>

"That—that does *not* count—" he began.

<br/>

"He left the ring, Archmage," called someone from the stands. Mark recognized Hollis Grey, a senior, sitting with his arms folded. "Entirely of his own accord."

<br/>

The crowd started to laugh. Up in the noble gallery, Mark noticed, even some of Julian's friends were laughing. Julian wasn't. He sat at the end of the row, very still, his eyes fixed on Mark.

<br/>

"...Ring-out," Ignis said at last, through his teeth. "Vance."

<br/>

From the front row, upside down, came Percival's muffled voice: "I *meant* to do that."

<br/>

Mark walked back to the water bucket and dipped his bracelet. It was still perfectly cool.

<br/>

*"Bad balance,"* he thought. *"Every universe."*

---

### Round Four: Hollis Grey

<br/>

Hollis Grey walked onto the sand like a man walking onto a field he'd plowed every day of his life.

<br/>

He was a broad, steady senior, perhaps eighteen, with a miller's shoulders, a plain ironwood staff, and a face that looked as if it had never learned how to sneer. He stopped at his mark, looked across the sand at Mark, and bowed. A real bow. Formal and respectful, the kind Mark had only ever seen exchanged between martial artists who took their training seriously.

<br/>

Mark returned it.

<br/>

"Begin," said Ignis.

<br/>

Hollis didn't throw anything at Mark. He drove the butt of his staff into the sand and spoke a low, rolling incantation.

<br/>

And the ground beneath Mark's feet *moved*.

<br/>

It wasn't an earthquake. It was a smooth, rolling heave, like a wave passing under a boat, lifting and tilting the sand under his left foot while dropping it away under his right. Mark felt his root—the deep, settled connection to the earth that had saved him against Garek and the construct—simply slide out from under him.

<br/>

He stumbled. Caught himself. Sank, reached for the ground, re-rooted.

<br/>

The sand rolled again. Under the right foot this time.

<br/>

Mark stumbled again.

<br/>

*"Oh,"* he thought, with a cold jolt. *"Oh, that's clever."*

<br/>

Every one of his techniques depended on a stable foundation. Every block, every deflection, every grounding pass. Hollis had seen him fight—they'd all seen the first three rounds—and Hollis had understood exactly where his strength came from.

<br/>

And he'd attacked the ground.

<br/>

Mark re-rooted. The sand heaved. He re-rooted again. The sand heaved again, from a different angle. Each time, it was a little harder to find his footing. Each time, Hollis drove his staff into the sand a little faster, with steady, patient force.

<br/>

The crowd had gone quiet. They could see what was happening. The coal-pit prodigy who'd breezed through three rounds was being slowly, methodically taken apart.

<br/>

Mark gritted his teeth.

<br/>

There was an easy answer, and he knew exactly what it was. One clean, zero-loss grounding pass would lock his root to the deep bedrock below the sand, so firmly that no surface magic in the world could shift it. Barely a flicker of effort. He'd done it a hundred times.

<br/>

And four men in the stands would see it.

<br/>

Mark felt Vespera's eyes on him from the front row. He didn't need to look to know she was holding very still.

<br/>

*"No,"* he thought. *"Not here. Not ever, here."*

<br/>

The sand rolled. He stumbled.

<br/>

*"Think,"* Mark told himself. *"Every attack has a pattern. Every system has a flaw. What's his?"*

<br/>

He watched the next wave.

<br/>

Hollis drove the staff into the sand. A heartbeat later, the ground under Mark rolled. And in that heartbeat, in the half-second between the staff striking and the wave arriving, Mark saw a faint ripple travel outward across the surface of the sand, from the butt of Hollis's staff toward him, like the ripple from a stone dropped in a pond.

<br/>

*"It doesn't start under me,"* Mark realized. *"It starts at his staff. He's pushing a wave through the ground from his end. And waves move in straight lines."*

<br/>

He watched the next one. The ripple. The heave. The ripple. The heave.

<br/>

Then he stopped trying to root against it.

<br/>

The next time the ripple came, Mark didn't brace. He moved *with* it. He let the wave lift him and stepped forward along its crest the way he'd once ridden the pillow waves on the Green River in his kayak, reading the water, flowing with the current instead of fighting it. The wave lifted him, and he used it, like a surfer, like a kayaker catching a fast eddy line, to carry himself in a single long, gliding stride halfway across the sand.

<br/>

Hollis's eyes widened.

<br/>

He drove his staff down again, fast, sending another wave. Mark read the ripple and rode it in too, closing the rest of the distance, right up to the point where the wave began, at the butt of Hollis's staff.

<br/>

There, at the source, the ground was perfectly still.

<br/>

Mark planted his feet, finally, in the one place in the arena where the sand wasn't moving. He rooted. He raised his bracelet, and for the first time in the tournament, he cast.

<br/>

A single, small, sputtering dirty push. Guild-standard. Pinched return, forty percent thermal loss, sparks and all. Not enough force to knock Hollis over. Just enough to knock the butt of his staff out of the sand, mid-incantation.

<br/>

Hollis's next wave, half-formed, collapsed back into his own feet. The sand bucked under *him*. He stumbled sideways, staggered, and his boot crossed the ring line.

<br/>

"Ring-out," Ignis said. "Vance."

<br/>

Mark stood on the sand, breathing hard. His bracelet was hot. Not dangerously, but hot enough that he could feel it through his sleeve, the heat trapped in the silver, going nowhere.

<br/>

*"One cast,"* he thought. *"One small cast. And it's already this warm."*

<br/>

Across the sand, Hollis Grey had straightened up. He looked at the ring line under his boot. Then he looked at Mark. And then, to Mark's surprise, he smiled, a wide, honest, delighted grin.

<br/>

"You rode it," he said. "The ground wave. Like water."

<br/>

"It moved in a straight line," Mark said. "From your staff. Like a ripple."

<br/>

"*Nobody's* ever seen that," said Hollis, shaking his head. "Not in four years." He walked across the sand and held out his hand.

<br/>

Mark shook it.

<br/>

Hollis held on a moment longer than necessary, and lowered his voice so it wouldn't carry to the judges' table.

<br/>

"My family runs the mill on the east bank," he said quietly. "Our grinding wheel runs on Aether crystals. The Chapterhouse ones always burned out every few months. We couldn't afford to keep buying them." He paused. "For the past month, we've been running on crystals from a smith in the south armory yard. Cheap. Good. Never burn out." His eyes met Mark's. "My father says they're the best thing that's happened to the mill in twenty years."

<br/>

Mark said nothing. But he nodded once.

<br/>

Hollis glanced up toward the noble gallery, where Julian sat at the end of the row, alone, very still.

<br/>

"I used to be his friend," Hollis said quietly. "Julian's. I was one of the ones who stopped sitting with him." He hesitated. "He wasn't always like this. Last year, he was... he was *funny*. Kind, even. He'd help the first-years with their chants." He shook his head slowly. "Then his father took him to the capital last winter. Two weeks. And when he came back, he wasn't the same. Wouldn't talk about it. Just... closed up. Started wearing that ring."

<br/>

"What happened in the capital?" Mark asked.

<br/>

"I don't know," Hollis said. "Nobody does. But whatever it was, it frightened him." He let go of Mark's hand. "Be careful in the final. He's been training for you every morning. And I don't think he's trying to win."

<br/>

He walked off the sand.

<br/>

Mark stood still for a moment, watching him go. Then he walked over to the water bucket and plunged his bracelet in. It hissed loudly and sent up a little curl of steam.

<br/>

He took out his clipboard.

<br/>

*Hollis Grey: senior, miller's son. Honorable. Fights fair. Customer. Former friend of Julian. Julian 'changed after two weeks in the capital last winter.' Started wearing the ring then. Find out more. Ally.*

<br/>

He looked at the steam rising off the bucket and added, underneath: *Bracelet heat: rising.*

---

### Round Five: Corwin

<br/>

Mark's semifinal opponent was asleep.

<br/>

He had been asleep, as far as anybody could tell, for the entire tournament. In the first round, his opponent had walked into the ring, found Corwin lying flat on his back in the center of the sand with his arms folded over his chest, and been unable to bring himself to attack an unconscious person. He'd forfeited out of sheer awkwardness. The same thing had happened in the second round. And the third. And the fourth.

<br/>

Corwin had reached the semifinal of the Term-End Practical Examination without opening his eyes.

<br/>

"Begin," said Ignis, in the tone of a man who had stopped believing in anything.

<br/>

Corwin lay in the center of the sand, motionless, snoring very faintly.

<br/>

Mark stood at his mark and looked down at him.

<br/>

"Corwin," he said.

<br/>

"Mm," said Corwin.

<br/>

"It's the semifinal."

<br/>

"Mm."

<br/>

"Are you going to forfeit?"

<br/>

There was a long pause.

<br/>

Then Corwin opened one eye. He looked up at Mark for a long, thoughtful moment. Then he opened the other eye too.

<br/>

And, to the total, shocked silence of the entire arena, he sat up.

<br/>

Then he stood up.

<br/>

A low murmur rolled through the stands. In three years at the Academy, as far as anybody knew, Corwin had never once stood up in public for longer than it took to walk between benches. Up in the noble gallery, several people actually rose to their feet to see. Even Ignis leaned forward at the judges' table.

<br/>

Corwin stretched, cracked his neck, and looked across the sand at Mark with an expression of mild, sleepy curiosity.

<br/>

"I've always wanted to see," he said, "what you'd do."

<br/>

Then he raised one hand.

<br/>

Corwin didn't carry a staff. He wore a battered, ancient bracelet, clearly Guild-standard, so old and scuffed that the engraving had almost worn away. He didn't chant. He didn't plant his feet or shout or gesture. He simply flicked one finger, almost lazily, toward the sand at Mark's feet, and murmured a single, quiet syllable.

<br/>

A small, precise kinetic push snapped the sand out from under Mark's leading foot.

<br/>

Mark caught himself. Barely.

<br/>

Corwin flicked another finger. Another syllable. Another precise push, at Mark's other foot, just as he shifted his weight onto it.

<br/>

Mark stumbled.

<br/>

*"He's good,"* Mark realized, with a jolt of genuine shock. *"He's really, really good."*

<br/>

Corwin didn't waste a thing. Not a spark. Not a breath. Every cast was the smallest possible force, aimed at exactly the right place, at exactly the right moment, with perfect, lazy economy. He wasn't trying to overpower Mark. He was trying to do the *least* amount of work necessary to knock him over.

<br/>

And it was working.

<br/>

"How," Mark asked, sidestepping a third tiny push, "are you this *good*?"

<br/>

"Three years of doing as little as possible," Corwin said, without changing expression, flicking a fourth. "Turns out, if you only ever do the minimum, you get very, very good at the minimum." He yawned in the middle of the next cast and somehow didn't lose his aim. "You learn exactly how much force a thing actually needs. And it's always much less than people think."

<br/>

He pushed again, from two directions at once, and Mark's feet went out from under him.

<br/>

Mark rolled across the sand, came up in a crouch, and found himself backed into the edge of the ring, inches from the line.

<br/>

Corwin raised his hand for one more push. Mark saw it coming and knew, with cold certainty, that it would be the last one. He had no room left to dodge. His bracelet was still warm from Hollis, and one more dirty cast would push it close to the edge.

<br/>

And there it was, right in front of him: the easy answer.

<br/>

One clean cast. One zero-loss grounding pass, deep into the bedrock under the sand. Perfectly rooted, unshakeable, the work of an instant. Corwin's tiny, precise pushes would slide off him like rain off stone.

<br/>

He felt it, tingling at the edge of his fingertips. Felt how simple it would be.

<br/>

He looked up into the stands.

<br/>

Vespera, in the front row, was not breathing. Up in the noble gallery, a well-dressed man in a dark coat had leaned forward slightly, his right hand resting on the railing. On his finger, a silver ring caught the morning light. The stone set in it was gold.

<br/>

Mark clenched his fist and let the tingle drain away.

<br/>

*"No,"* he thought.

<br/>

He couldn't dodge. He couldn't root against Corwin's pushes without magic. And he couldn't use the magic that would save him.

<br/>

So he did the only thing left. He didn't move at all.

<br/>

He sank down into the deepest, lowest, most relaxed horse stance he could manage, the stance he'd drilled ten thousand times in a decade of Kung Fu forms. He let his weight drop all the way down into his legs, and he waited, absolutely still, like a stone sitting in a riverbed.

<br/>

Corwin's next push arrived.

<br/>

It was perfect, as all of Corwin's pushes were. The exact minimum force needed to knock a fourteen-year-old boy off-balance and over the line.

<br/>

But it was calculated for a fourteen-year-old boy who was moving. Not one who had sunk every ounce of himself into a stance designed to absorb exactly this kind of force.

<br/>

The push hit. Mark's body rocked. Swayed. His heels slid half an inch in the sand.

<br/>

And he held.

<br/>

Corwin blinked.

<br/>

He looked at Mark, crouched motionless at the edge of the ring. He considered his own outstretched hand. He seemed, Mark thought, to be doing some quick arithmetic, recalculating exactly how much more force it would take to move a boy who refused to move.

<br/>

Mark watched him do the math.

<br/>

Then Corwin slowly lowered his hand.

<br/>

And yawned.

<br/>

"I've been vertical," he announced to the arena, "for four minutes. That's my limit." He turned toward Ignis's table. "I forfeit."

<br/>

He walked back across the sand to the edge of the ring, lay down flat on his back, folded his arms over his chest, and closed his eyes.

<br/>

There was a long, stunned silence.

<br/>

"...Forfeit," Ignis said faintly. "Vance."

<br/>

The arena erupted. Not for Mark, this time. For Corwin. Students who'd walked past that sleeping figure on his bench for three long years stood up in the stands and cheered for him, astonished.

<br/>

Corwin didn't open his eyes. But the corner of his mouth twitched up, just slightly.

<br/>

Mark walked over to him, still breathing hard.

<br/>

"You could have won," he said quietly.

<br/>

"Probably," said Corwin. "But then I'd have to fight Julian. And that sounds exhausting." He cracked one eye open and looked up at Mark. "Besides. I got what I wanted."

<br/>

"Which was?"

<br/>

"I wanted to see if you'd do it," Corwin said. "Whatever it is you *aren't* doing. The thing everyone's watching for." He glanced, very slightly, toward the noble gallery, toward the man with the gold ring. "You didn't."

<br/>

He closed the eye again.

<br/>

"Good," said Corwin. "Don't."

<br/>

Mark stared at him.

<br/>

Then he walked to the water bucket and plunged his bracelet in. It hissed sharply. A small cloud of steam rose and drifted away across the sand.

<br/>

He pulled out his clipboard. His hand was shaking very slightly.

<br/>

*Corwin: Not just horizontal. Extremely good. Three years of practicing 'the minimum' = best force economy I've ever seen. Knows I'm hiding something. Knows they're watching. Told me not to. Asset. Much more than I thought.*

<br/>

He added, at the bottom: *Bracelet: hot. One cast left, maybe two.*

---

### Round Six: The Final

<br/>

There was an hour's interval before the final. Mark spent it sitting on the edge of the competitors' tunnel with his bracelet submerged in the water bucket, watching the steam rise, while Lyra crouched beside him and rapidly swapped its crystal for a freshly charged one.

<br/>

"How hot is it?" he asked.

<br/>

Lyra pulled it out of the water and held it up to the light. Her face was grim.

<br/>

"It's cooled," she said. "But not all the way. The silver's been heated too many times today. It's tired. It won't take much more." She hesitated. "One cast, Mark. Maybe one and a bit. Then it'll go."

<br/>

"One cast," Mark repeated. "Okay."

<br/>

"*Mark*."

<br/>

"I don't need any more than that," Mark said. "If this works, I won't need to cast at all."

<br/>

Lyra looked at him. Then she looked across the arena, at the far tunnel, where Julian Thorne had just emerged into the light.

<br/>

"And if it doesn't work?"

<br/>

Mark didn't answer.

<br/>

Lyra pressed the bracelet into his hand, folded his fingers around it, and gripped his fist tightly with both of hers for a moment.

<br/>

"Cheese," she said quietly. "When I see it, I'll yell cheese."

<br/>

Then she was gone, back up into the stands.

---

Julian walked to the center of the sand.

<br/>

He looked, Mark thought, terrible. He was dressed immaculately as always, in silver-embroidered robes, his polished silver staff gleaming in his hand. But beneath the polish, his face was pale and drawn. There were dark circles under his eyes. He hadn't been sleeping. Corwin had been right.

<br/>

He stopped at his mark and looked at Mark across the sand. He didn't sneer. He didn't smile. He just looked.

<br/>

"Vance," he said.

<br/>

"Thorne."

<br/>

"You haven't cast clean once today," Julian said quietly, so that only Mark could hear. "Not once. Not even against Grey, when the ground was moving. Not even against Corwin, when you were backed into the line." He paused. "That was very disciplined."

<br/>

"Thank you."

<br/>

"It won't save you," Julian said. "I'm going to make you." His knuckles whitened on his staff. "And when you do, it'll be over. For everyone. Before my father gets here and makes it so much worse." He took a breath. "Please believe me. I'm doing you a kindness."

<br/>

Mark looked at him. At the dark circles under his eyes. At the tremor in the hand holding the staff.

<br/>

"I believe you think so," Mark said quietly.

<br/>

"*Competitors*," said Ignis, rising from the judges' table, his voice amplified by the acoustic rune and dripping with anticipation. "The final bout of the Term-End Practical Examination. Julian of House Thorne." A roar of applause from the noble gallery. "And... Apprentice Vance." A smaller roar, entirely from one section of enforcers, dockhands, and goat handlers, and one large, painted banner of a fish holding a hammer.

<br/>

"*Begin*."

---

Julian opened with fire.

<br/>

He raised the silver staff over his head with both hands and *screamed* a long, rolling incantation in High Imperial. The crystal at the staff's tip blazed, and a torrent of white-hot flame roared across the sand toward Mark like the breath of a furnace.

<br/>

Mark dodged.

<br/>

He threw himself sideways, rolled through the sand, and came up in a crouch as the flame scorched the ground where he'd been standing, turning the sand to glittering glass.

<br/>

Julian didn't pause. He swept the staff around and chanted again, louder. A kinetic blast tore across the arena. Mark ducked under it. It struck the stone wall behind him and cracked it.

<br/>

Fire again. Mark sidestepped.

<br/>

Kinetic again. Mark dove.

<br/>

The crowd was roaring. This was what they'd come to see: a Thorne battle-mage at full power, light and noise and fury, the arena shaking with every cast. Up in the noble gallery, the families were on their feet. Ignis stood at the judges' table, fists clenched, eyes shining with delight.

<br/>

And Mark didn't fight back.

<br/>

He didn't cast. He didn't attack. He moved. He sidestepped, ducked, rolled, redirected the edges of blasts into the sand with quick Kung Fu deflections and the thinnest touch of his root. He never stood still and he never closed in. He just kept making Julian *miss*.

<br/>

"*Fight back!*" Julian shouted, between chants. "*Cast, damn you!*"

<br/>

"That one was very loud," Mark called back, sliding under a fireball. "And very slow. You telegraphed it."

<br/>

Julian's face flushed. He chanted again, harder. Bigger fire. Mark dodged.

<br/>

"Your stance is very narrow," Mark observed politely, rolling clear of a kinetic blast. "You're leaning forward. You're leading with your weight."

<br/>

"*Shut up!*"

<br/>

Another blast. Bigger. Longer chant. Louder. Mark slid aside.

<br/>

"It's not your fault," Mark called. "It's the architecture. You're working with a very poorly designed system."

<br/>

Julian *screamed*.

<br/>

It was a sound of pure, raw fury, and he poured every ounce of it into his next cast, the longest chant yet, a full minute of rising, roaring High Imperial. The silver staff blazed so brightly that the front rows shielded their eyes, and the flame that came out of it was not a jet but a *wall*: a towering, rolling wave of white fire that swept across the entire width of the arena.

<br/>

There was nowhere to dodge.

<br/>

Mark dropped flat to the sand and covered his head, and the wave of fire roared over him, so close that he felt it singe the back of his tunic, so hot that the sand beneath him turned briefly to molten glass and then cooled into a glittering crust around his body.

<br/>

Then it was gone.

<br/>

Mark lifted his head, coughing, and looked across the arena at Julian.

<br/>

And at Julian's staff.

<br/>

The polished silver had changed color. It wasn't gleaming anymore. Along the full length of the shaft, beneath the ironwood, a faint, dull red glow had begun to show through the grain, like coals seen through a wooden grate.

<br/>

*"There,"* Mark thought.

<br/>

Julian hadn't noticed. He was too angry. He was already raising the staff again, already chanting.

<br/>

Mark stood up and backed away. He let Julian come.

---

The next three minutes were the longest of Mark's life.

<br/>

Julian chased him across the sand, casting again and again and again. Fire. Force. Fire. Force. Each cast a little longer, a little louder, a little more desperate. Each miss drove him to cast the next one bigger. He was sweating now, pouring sweat, his hair plastered to his forehead, his chanting ragged.

<br/>

And with every cast, the color in the staff deepened.

<br/>

Dull red.

<br/>

Cherry red.

<br/>

Mark kept moving. Ducking, sliding, rolling. His muscles burned. His lungs ached. He'd been fighting for six rounds. The singed back of his tunic stung. He kept moving.

<br/>

Bright cherry. Then the first faint touch of *orange*, creeping up the silver core.

<br/>

Julian's palms were blistering. Mark could see it from across the sand: angry red welts rising on the young man's hands where they gripped the ironwood, the thermal bleed burning straight through the insulation. Julian didn't seem to feel them. Or he felt them and didn't care.

<br/>

He chanted again. The longest yet. The staff screamed with heat.

<br/>

And from the front row of the stands, clear and sharp over the roar of the crowd, came Lyra's voice:

<br/>

"*CHEESE!*"

<br/>

In the enforcers' section, a dozen people who had no idea what was happening leaped to their feet and cheered.

<br/>

"*CHEESE!*" roared Garek, both fists in the air.

<br/>

"*CHEEEEESE!*" bellowed Torren, waving the fish banner.

<br/>

"*Cheese*," murmured Nan Brask in the front row, without looking up from her knitting.

<br/>

Ignis turned and stared at the stands in total bewilderment.

<br/>

But Julian didn't notice the shouting. He'd felt it. Mark saw the exact moment it happened. Julian's chant faltered. He looked down at his own hands, at the staff, at the silver core glowing orange through the wood. At the blisters on his palms.

<br/>

And his face changed.

<br/>

It wasn't anger now. It was fear.

<br/>

He knew. Every Guild-trained mage knew, the way every smith knew about overheated iron. *When the silver glows, the staff is failing.*

<br/>

He looked across the sand at Mark.

<br/>

Mark was standing completely still, in the center of the arena, his hands at his sides. He hadn't cast once.

<br/>

And Julian, exhausted and burned and terrified, with his staff failing in his hands and his one chance slipping away and his father's men watching him from the gallery, made a decision.

<br/>

Mark saw it in his eyes a fraction of a second before it happened.

<br/>

*"No,"* Mark thought. *"No, Julian, don't—"*

<br/>

Julian closed his eyes. He stopped chanting. His lips stopped moving. His grip on the staff shifted. And Mark felt, from across the arena, the faint, unmistakable change in the air, the clean, cold, silent tug of a spell being gathered *without resistance*, with no loss, flowing smooth and frictionless through the—

<br/>

Up in the stands, all four gold rings flared.

<br/>

Mark saw them. Four bright points of golden light, all at once, scattered through the crowd: in the noble gallery, in the student section, near the eastern tunnel, in the back row. Four men leaped to their feet together, their faces turning not toward Mark, but toward Julian.

<br/>

*His own father's men.*

<br/>

Julian's eyes snapped open. He'd felt them too. He'd felt the rings react to him. The color drained from his face completely as he understood exactly what he'd just done, in front of exactly whom.

<br/>

He tried to stop. He wrenched the spell back, trying to abort the clean cast halfway through.

<br/>

But a spell that was half clean and half dirty, forced back through a silver staff already glowing orange-hot, was more than any Guild-standard architecture had ever been designed to survive.

<br/>

The silver core shorted out.

<br/>

It went with a bang like a thunderclap. A blinding burst of blue-white sparks exploded from the staff's tip and ran down the length of the shaft in a crackling cascade, exactly like Torren's old blower, exactly like every melted regulator in every scrap bin Mark had ever pulled apart. The backlash hit Julian full in the chest and threw him off his feet. He flew backward across the sand and landed hard on his back, the staff tumbling from his blistered hands.

<br/>

The staff hit the sand beside him, still sparking. Still glowing. Still discharging, crackling and spitting in great erratic bursts, and the ground around it began to smoke.

<br/>

It was rolling, Mark saw, slowly, sparking and spitting, toward Julian's head.

---

Mark didn't think.

<br/>

He was running before the staff stopped rolling. Across the sand, eight long strides, and as he ran he reached into his pocket and pulled out a short coil of plain copper wire, the kind Torren kept in a dozen bins around the workshop. Mark had stuffed a length of it into his pocket that morning, the same way he'd once kept a spare charger in every bag he owned in Raleigh. Just in case.

<br/>

He dropped to his knees beside the sparking staff, whipped the wire twice around the silver tip, and drove the other end straight down into the sand, as deep as he could reach, into the damp earth under the arena floor.

<br/>

It took no magic at all. Just a wire and the ground.

<br/>

The staff *hissed*, a long, sustained sound like a forge-iron plunged into a quenching barrel. The sparks stuttered, flickered, and died. The orange glow faded from the silver core, down through cherry, down through dull red, and then went dark.

<br/>

Silence fell over the arena.

<br/>

Julian lay on his back in the sand beside the dead staff, staring up at the sky. His robes were scorched. His palms were badly blistered. His breath came in short, shocked gasps. But he was alive, and he was unhurt.

<br/>

Mark sat back on his heels beside him, breathing hard, his hand still resting on the copper wire.

<br/>

For a long moment, neither of them spoke.

<br/>

Up in the stands, the four men with gold rings were still on their feet. They were no longer watching Mark at all. Every one of them was staring at Julian.

---

Archmage Ignis rose slowly from the judges' table.

<br/>

He looked at Julian, lying on the sand beside his ruined staff. He looked at Mark, kneeling beside him with a length of common blacksmith's wire in his hand. He looked at the glittering crust of melted sand, the scorched walls, the cracked stone, all of it the work of Julian's staff and not one mark of it from Mark's.

<br/>

He looked as if he'd swallowed something enormously bitter.

<br/>

"The bout," Ignis said at last, in a strangled voice, "is concluded. Julian of House Thorne is... unable to continue." He paused. It clearly cost him a great deal. "The victor of the Term-End Practical Examination is... Apprentice Vance."

<br/>

The enforcers' section exploded.

<br/>

Garek leaped to his feet with a roar, forgot about his knee entirely, and immediately sat back down again, wincing. Torren waved the fish banner so hard that the pole snapped. Brann the folded-eared enforcer hugged the goat handler next to him, who hugged him back. Tessa Fenn, in the student section, was on her feet, applauding. So was Hollis Grey. So was Tobin Pell, though he was doing it from slightly behind a pillar.

<br/>

In the front row, Lyra had both hands pressed over her mouth. Beside her, Vespera let out a long breath she seemed to have been holding for the last ten minutes.

<br/>

And at the edge of the sand, Corwin, still lying flat on his back with his eyes closed, slowly raised one hand in the air and gave a single, lazy thumbs-up.

---

Julian turned his head on the sand and looked at Mark.

<br/>

The crowd was roaring. Nobody could hear them.

<br/>

"Why," Julian said hoarsely, "did you help me?"

<br/>

Mark looked down at him. At the scorched robes, the blistered hands, the pale, exhausted face of someone who was barely older than the body Mark was wearing.

<br/>

"Because your staff was designed to fail," Mark said quietly. "Every staff in the Empire is. That's not your fault."

<br/>

Julian stared at him.

<br/>

"You could have let it burn me," he said. "You could have won without lifting a finger. You *did* win without lifting a finger."

<br/>

"I know."

<br/>

"Then why?"

<br/>

Mark considered how to answer. He thought about forty years of watching good engineers burn out on bad systems built by people who never had to use them. He thought about Hollis saying *he wasn't always like this*.

<br/>

"Because you're not the one who built it," he said at last.

<br/>

Julian said nothing. He closed his eyes. His jaw was clenched tight, and Mark realized, with a jolt, that he was trying very hard not to cry in front of the entire Academy.

<br/>

Mark stood up and stepped back, to give him a moment.

<br/>

When he looked up at the noble gallery, the man with the gold ring in the dark coat was gone.

---

They celebrated at *The Deep Hearth*.

<br/>

It was not, strictly, a team dinner. By the time they arrived, half the Fish & Hammer Infirmary had followed them down the stone steps, along with Garek, Brann, both of Nan's door guards, Pip and most of the message bus, and, to Mark's surprise, Hollis Grey and Tessa Fenn, who had been swept along in the crowd and seemed delighted to be there. Tobin Pell had come too, though he spent most of the evening standing very close to the door.

<br/>

Brunhild met them at the bottom of the steps with her arms folded and looked Mark up and down.

<br/>

"I heard," she said, "about your fight."

<br/>

"Ah," said Mark.

<br/>

Brunhild nodded slowly, gravely, and laid one huge hand on his shoulder.

<br/>

"That," she said, "was the *worst fight* I have *ever* heard of."

<br/>

The room roared its approval. Torren wiped his eyes.

<br/>

"You didn't even *hit* him," Brunhild went on, with deep feeling. "You won a *fight* without *hitting* anyone. It's *disgraceful*. It's an *insult* to fighting." She clapped him on the back hard enough to rattle his teeth. "*Best* thing I ever heard. Sit down. Gerald's Stew's on the house."

<br/>

They sat. The stew came. Garek insisted on buying a round for the entire hall, then quietly asked Mark, under the noise, whether jumping to his feet in the stands could have damaged his knee.

<br/>

"Probably not," said Mark. "But do the leg lifts tonight."

<br/>

"Twice?"

<br/>

"Twice."

<br/>

Garek nodded solemnly and drank his ale.

<br/>

Across the table, Lyra and Tessa Fenn had fallen into an intense conversation about focus overheating that had already filled three sheets of Lyra's parchment with diagrams. Hollis was telling Torren about his family's grinding wheel, and Torren was hanging on every word. Vespera was quietly warming Brann's bad shoulder under the table while he described, in great detail, every chair he'd ever hit a man with.

<br/>

Mark sat back and watched them for a while.

<br/>

*"Team building,"* he thought. *"It's growing."*

---

They left a little before midnight, spilling up the stone steps into the cool night air under the two moons, laughing and loud.

<br/>

At the top of the steps, a man was waiting.

<br/>

He was tall and well-dressed, in a plain dark coat. He stood very still beside the doorway. Mark recognized him at once: the man from the noble gallery, the one who'd leaned forward on the railing when Mark was backed against the line. On his right hand, a silver ring caught the moonlight.

<br/>

The stone in it was gold.

<br/>

The laughter around Mark died at once. Garek stepped forward, close at Mark's shoulder, and his right hand drifted toward his belt. Brann was suddenly beside him on the other side. Torren put himself very deliberately between the man and Lyra.

<br/>

The man didn't seem to notice any of them. He looked only at Mark.

<br/>

"Apprentice Vance," he said. His voice was quiet and courteous. "Congratulations on your victory."

<br/>

"Thank you," said Mark carefully.

<br/>

The man reached into his coat. Garek tensed.

<br/>

The man drew out a letter: thick, cream-colored parchment, folded and sealed with a disk of dark wax. He held it out.

<br/>

Mark took it. He looked down at the seal.

<br/>

Pressed into the wax was a circle, cut through by a single horizontal line.

<br/>

"Lord Aldric Thorne," the man said, "requests the pleasure of your company. Two weeks from today, at his residence in the city. A carriage will be sent." He paused. "My lord asks that you come alone."

<br/>

He inclined his head politely, turned, and walked away into the dark.

<br/>

Nobody spoke until his footsteps had faded entirely.

<br/>

"Well," said Torren at last, into the silence. "That's a Gerald."

---

It was nearly two in the morning when Mark sat at the desk in his narrow dorm room, his back to the wall, his clipboard beside his ledger, the sealed letter unopened on the desk in front of him.

<br/>

His tunic was scorched across the back. His legs ached from six rounds of dodging. The dirty bracelet sat beside the letter, its silver dull and tired. Lyra had said it would never be quite the same again, and that she would happily melt it down, "with *joy*."

<br/>

In the corner of the ceiling, the lentil spider had expanded its web again. It was, Mark noted, very definitely growing.

<br/>

He picked up his charcoal and wrote:

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 16: Term-End Practical Examination*
> *Result: Won.*
> *Attacks landed: zero.*
> *Clean casts: zero.*
> *Dirty casts: one (Round 4).*
> *Victory condition (Final): Opponent's hardware failed per its original design specification. Retry storm induced via dodging and polite feedback. Silver core reached thermal failure. I didn't beat Julian. The Guild did.*
> *Round Summary:*
> *1. Tobin Pell — forfeit before start. Gave me water. Kind, observant, terrified of everything. Academy node #2 candidate.*
> *2. Tessa Fenn — fast, efficient, short casts. Taught herself efficient casting because she couldn't afford a staff. Sends half her stipend home. Natural engineer. Recruit.*
> *3. Percival Ashdown — 4 seconds. Self-propelled ring-out. 'I meant to do that.' Bad balance, every universe.*
> *4. Hollis Grey — ground-wave magic attacking my root. Rode the waves like river current to the source. Honorable. Customer. Former friend of Julian. Julian changed after two weeks in the capital last winter. Ally.*
> *5. Corwin — stood up. Is extremely good. Three years of 'the minimum' = best force economy in the Academy. Knew I was hiding something. Told me not to. Forfeited after four vertical minutes. Much more than an asset.*
> *6. Julian Thorne — see above.*
> *Critical Event:*
> *Under pressure, Julian attempted a CLEAN cast in public. All four gold rings in the arena flared. Every Thorne agent turned to look at Julian, not me. Julian tried to abort. Half-clean, half-dirty spell + overheated staff = catastrophic short. I grounded the staff with copper wire (no magic). Julian unhurt.*
> *Julian asked why I helped him. I said: 'Because you're not the one who built it.' He nearly cried in front of the Academy.*
> *Assessment:*
> *Julian is in more danger now than I am. His father's own men saw him cast clean. He was trying to bring them proof against me. He brought them proof against himself.*
> *Incoming:*
> *Sealed invitation (Concord seal) from Lord Aldric Thorne. Two weeks. His residence. 'Come alone.'*
> *Gerald.*

<br/>

He put down the charcoal and looked at the letter for a long time.

<br/>

He didn't open it yet. He didn't need to. He already knew everything it would say that mattered.

<br/>

Instead, he picked up his clipboard and wrote out a new list, very neatly, under the heading *People I Fought Today*:

<br/>

*Tobin. Tessa. Percival. Hollis. Corwin. Julian.*

<br/>

He looked at it for a while. Then he crossed out *Fought* and wrote, above it: *Met*.

<br/>

He thought about Tessa's chipped wooden rod. Hollis's bow. Corwin's thumbs-up from the sand. Tobin's water flask. Even Percival, upside down in the front row, insisting he'd meant to do it.

<br/>

And Julian, lying on the scorched sand beside a dead silver staff, trying not to cry.

<br/>

*"Good people,"* Mark thought. *"Every one of them, stuck inside a bad system. Doing the best they can with tools designed to fail."*

<br/>

He put out the lamp and lay back in the dark.

<br/>

In forty years of corporate life, he'd seen it over and over. The system was almost never the fault of the people inside it. It was the fault of whoever designed it, and then made sure nobody could ever read the source.

<br/>

*"Two weeks,"* he thought, staring at the ceiling. *"And then I meet the architect."*
