### Chapter 7: The Bottleneck and the Guild

<br/>

Mark woke to a shaft of bright morning sunlight cutting across his face, accompanied by a headache that felt like a rusty nail driven behind his left eye.

<br/>

He sat up slowly, groaning as a dull, lingering numbness tingled down his right forearm. His brain felt heavy, sluggish, and thoroughly fried—the exact cognitive hangover he used to get after spending sixteen straight hours debugging asynchronous threading issues on a red-eye deployment. The only thing missing was the cold pizza.

<br/>

He looked across the room at the candle on the small table. The wick was still charred black from the night before.

<br/>

*"Output confirmed,"* Mark thought, rubbing his throbbing temples with both hands. *"Process cost: astronomical."*

<br/>

On the far side of the room, Garin was sitting on the edge of his cot, holding his left boot up to his nose with a deeply troubled expression. He sniffed it. He frowned. He sniffed it again, as though the second sniff might file an appeal against the first.

<br/>

"Something's wrong with my boot," Garin announced.

<br/>

"Hm," Mark said neutrally.

<br/>

"Smells like a wheel of cheese caught fire inside it." Garin turned the boot over and peered into it with one eye closed. "And it's warm. Why is it warm?"

<br/>

"Humidity," Mark said. "Very humid region."

<br/>

Garin stared at him for a long moment, then at the boot, then shrugged and jammed his foot into it with the resigned acceptance of a man who had smelled worse things on the road.

<br/>

*"Incident report,"* Mark thought. *"Root cause: user error. Impact: one boot. Resolution: deny everything. Ticket closed."*

<br/>

He swung his legs over the edge of the cot and rested his bare feet on the cold floorboards, taking slow, deep breaths to clear the brain fog.

<br/>

The experiment had proven his theory—magic didn't inherently require a staff or a spoken chant—but it had also exposed a glaring system bottleneck. His fourteen-year-old brain possessed the raw agility, but not the working memory bandwidth to calculate energy collection, thermal conversion, targeting, and safety cut-offs simultaneously without burning out his neural pathways. Last night had been like writing assembly code by hand while someone played the tuba in the corner of the room. Specifically, Garin.

<br/>

*"It's a compiler issue,"* Mark realized, staring at his palms. *"I tried to manually evaluate every single line of code in real-time. In martial arts, you don't consciously calculate hip rotation, weight distribution, and vector force every time you throw a jab or execute a hip escape. You drill the movement five thousand times until it becomes muscle memory. The subconscious handles the execution."*

<br/>

If he wanted to cast spells silently and staffless without giving himself an aneurysm, he couldn't rely on raw, conscious willpower. He needed to build **cognitive subroutines**—mental muscle memory that his subconscious could execute on command.

<br/>

*"And,"* he added, glancing at Garin's boot, *"better target validation."*

---

Downstairs in the common room, the morning rush was in full swing. The air smelled of fried salt pork, baked barley bread, and cheap ale—because apparently, in Oakhaven, ale counted as a breakfast beverage.

<br/>

Garin had claimed a corner table and was demolishing a mountain of eggs and ham the way a forest fire demolishes a forest: thoroughly, loudly, and with no regard for the surrounding ecosystem. Vespera sat across from him, sipping herbal tea, her indigo cloak neatly folded over her lap and her ironwood staff leaning against her chair. She had pulled her chair a careful eighteen inches away from Garin's elbow, which suggested she had shared breakfast tables with him before.

<br/>

"Look who finally crawled out of the grave," Garin boomed as Mark approached. "You look worse than you did yesterday, kid. What were you doing up there, fighting ghosts?"

<br/>

"Optimizing throughput," Mark muttered, dropping onto the bench. He pulled a fresh bread roll toward himself and took a large bite.

<br/>

Garin squinted at him. "Is that like the cake thing?"

<br/>

"Sort of," Mark said. "It's more like... figuring out how to carry more grain without the cart breaking."

<br/>

"Huh." Garin nodded slowly, chewing. "So, a bigger cart."

<br/>

"No, it's—" Mark stopped. Forty years of explaining technical debt to people who just wanted a bigger cart rose up inside him like a tide. "Yes. A bigger cart."

<br/>

"Knew it," Garin said, satisfied.

<br/>

Vespera's sharp eyes narrowed as she looked at Mark. She set her teacup down with a soft, deliberate *clink*.

<br/>

"There was an erratic fluctuation in the local Aether flux around midnight," Vespera said quietly, her voice dropping so the surrounding tables couldn't hear. "It was faint, but unrefined. Spiky. Like someone trying to strike flint underwater." She paused. "Twice. The second time was marginally less incompetent."

<br/>

"I'll take that as positive feedback," Mark said.

<br/>

"It was not feedback. It was an accusation." She leaned across the table. "Mark... what did you do in that room?"

<br/>

"Speaking of that room," Garin said helpfully, through a mouthful of ham, "something got into my boot last night."

<br/>

Vespera's gaze swiveled slowly from Garin to Mark, like a lighthouse beam finding a ship that had no business being where it was.

<br/>

Mark chewed his bread calmly, giving nothing away. "Rats, probably. Very warm rats."

<br/>

"Warm rats," Vespera repeated flatly.

<br/>

"Thermally active rats. It's a regional thing." Mark took another bite. "As for the flux, I was practicing my breathwork. I'm a very vigorous breather."

<br/>

Vespera stared at him, clearly unsatisfied with the answer and visibly composing a much longer interrogation. But before she could push further, Garin wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and stood up.

<br/>

"Well, whatever you were doing, break's over," the merchant announced. "I've got iron to unload at the smithies, and Vespera's got business at the local Mages' Chapterhouse. Mark, you're coming with me to the Guildhall."

<br/>

Mark raised an eyebrow. "Guildhall?"

<br/>

"You're a long way from home, kid, and you're broke," Garin said pragmatically. "I bought you dinner and a bed for helping with the cart, but today you're on your own dime. The Trades and Adventurers Guild handles short-term labor contracts for travelers—everything from hauling grain to guarding livestock. It's the best place to earn a few coppers if you don't mind sweating."

<br/>

*"Free trial expired,"* Mark thought. *"Time to upgrade to a paid plan."*

<br/>

He nodded. He needed local currency—not just for food and lodging, but to buy basic supplies. Notebooks, ink, maybe some raw copper wire or mineral samples to test as thermal conductors.

<br/>

"Lead the way," Mark said.

<br/>

Vespera rose as well, collecting her staff. As Mark passed her, she leaned down and murmured, just loud enough for him to hear: "I know what a thermally active rat is, Mark. It's a boy."

<br/>

"Noted," Mark murmured back.

---

The Oakhaven Guildhall was a massive, timber-framed building near the town square, buzzing with activity. Merchants, off-duty guards, armored mercenaries, and local laborers crowded around large wooden notice boards pinned with parchment contracts.

<br/>

Mark took in the scene—the shoving crowd, the desperate faces, the board plastered edge to edge with postings—and felt a profound wave of recognition.

<br/>

*"A job board,"* he thought. *"Of course. Different universe, same hellscape."* He half expected one of the postings to demand ten years of experience with a spell that had been invented three years ago.

<br/>

Garin led Mark past the crowd to a long oak counter where an overworked clerk with ink-stained fingers was furiously stamping papers. The man had the hollow, thousand-yard stare of someone who had processed every form in existence and had come to hate all of them equally.

<br/>

"Harlan!" Garin barked cheerfully. "Got a fresh hire for your labor roster."

<br/>

The clerk, Harlan, didn't look up from his ledger. "Name, origin, skill set."

<br/>

"Mark. Upriver," Mark answered directly. "General labor, physical coordination, mechanics, problem-solving. Forty years of—" He caught himself. "—of, uh. Enthusiasm."

<br/>

Harlan finally glanced up, squinting at Mark over a pair of wire-rimmed spectacles. He took in Mark's lean frame, smooth skin, and youth.

<br/>

"Forty years of enthusiasm," Harlan repeated.

<br/>

"I'm very enthusiastic."

<br/>

"Fourteen? Fifteen?" Harlan sighed, dipping his quill in ink. "You look like you'd snap under a sack of grain, boy. Can you lift?"

<br/>

"I understand leverage," Mark replied. "Put me where the work is heavy."

<br/>

Harlan scratched something onto the form. "I'll put you down for 'general labor.' We don't have a box for 'problem-solving.'"

<br/>

"What happens to people who solve problems?"

<br/>

"They get put in the general labor box," Harlan said. "Problems here get solved by hauling them somewhere else."

<br/>

*"Honestly,"* Mark thought, *"that's more self-aware than most of the org charts I've worked under."*

<br/>

Harlan scribbled Mark's name onto a parchment sheet. "Blacksmith's yard behind the south armory needs coal moved from the storage pit to the furnaces. Two copper bits for four hours of hauling."

<br/>

Mark did the math instantly. Half a copper an hour. He'd negotiated better rates as a summer intern in 1984. Then again, he'd also had student loans in 1984, so maybe it came out even.

<br/>

"Don't steal the iron, don't piss off the smith, and try not to break your back," Harlan continued.

<br/>

"Is that the whole employee handbook?"

<br/>

"There used to be a fourth rule." Harlan frowned at the ceiling. "Nobody remembers what it was. We think it was about goats."

<br/>

He slapped a small bronze token onto the counter. "Show that to Smith Master Torren."

<br/>

"Appreciate it," Mark said, scooping up the token.

<br/>

"Don't mention it," Harlan said, already stamping the next form. "Truly. Don't. I have a line."

---

An hour later, Mark was standing in the sweltering heat of the south armory forge yard, stripped down to his linen trousers, shovel in hand. A massive pile of black coal sat on one side of the yard; three roaring brick furnaces sat on the other.

<br/>

Around him, two other teenage laborers were already sweating profusely, groaning under the weight of oversized wooden wheelbarrows as they struggled across the rutted dirt. One was a lanky, sunburned boy named Tam. The other, a broad, bull-necked lad named Dobbs, was already bragging loudly about how many loads he'd move before noon.

<br/>

Dobbs proved his point by heaping his wheelbarrow so high with coal that it looked like a small black mountain range. He grabbed the handles, heaved, took three heroic steps—and the entire barrow rolled sideways and dumped its load across the yard in a cloud of black dust.

<br/>

Mark watched with professional sympathy.

<br/>

*"Overcommitted sprint,"* he thought. *"Looks heroic at stand-up. Ends in tears by Thursday."*

<br/>

"You might want to load it lower," Mark offered. "Keep the weight over the wheel, not the handles. It's basic center of gravity."

<br/>

Dobbs glared at him through a mask of coal dust. "Who asked you, twig?"

<br/>

"Nobody," Mark said cheerfully. "Nobody ever does. That's how I know I'm right."

<br/>

Mark didn't rush. He stepped up to his own wheelbarrow, checked the balance, and analyzed the movement.

<br/>

*"Physical labor is just dynamic breathwork,"* Mark thought, taking a deep breath through his nose.

<br/>

He didn't just haul with his arms. He dropped into a shallow Kung Fu stance, engaging his legs, hips, and core. He shoveled the coal with rhythmic, fluid efficiency, keeping his spine straight and his center of gravity low.

<br/>

While his body executed the physical labor on pure muscle memory, he opened up a secondary background thread in his mind.

<br/>

With every breath in, he pulled a tiny, controlled trickle of ambient Aether into his chest. With every breath out, he routed the energy down through his legs and into the dirt beneath his feet, maintaining a constant, subtle ground.

<br/>

*Inhale, pull Aether. Exhale, shovel coal, discharge to ground.*

<br/>

The first ten minutes did not go perfectly.

<br/>

On his sixth shovelful, Mark got the rhythm backward—inhaling on the swing and exhaling on the pause. Instead of flowing down into the dirt, the Aether found the nearest available exit, which happened to be the iron blade of his shovel.

<br/>

*Snap.*

<br/>

A tiny blue spark jumped from the shovel to the wheelbarrow. Every single hair on Mark's head stood straight up, as if he'd licked a nine-volt battery.

<br/>

Tam stopped in his tracks, staring at him. Dobbs, still scooping up his spilled coal by hand, slowly looked up.

<br/>

Mark smoothed his hair down with one palm, which accomplished nothing whatsoever.

<br/>

"Dry air," he explained.

<br/>

"It rained yesterday," Tam said.

<br/>

"Dry *today*," Mark said firmly, and went back to shoveling.

<br/>

He corrected the timing. Inhale on the pause, exhale on the swing. The prickling settled. The current found the ground. Within a few minutes, the rhythm locked in and the whole process became smooth, quiet, and almost automatic.

<br/>

The work was grueling, but to Mark, it was meditative. He wasn't just moving coal to earn two copper coins; he was building the physical and mental infrastructure for his new life. He was training his nervous system to handle energy flow automatically while his body performed complex physical tasks. It was, he reflected, the first time in his life he'd been paid to do on-the-job training that was actually useful.

<br/>

By the time the four hours were up, the other two laborers were collapsed against the stone wall, gasping for air, their arms trembling with exhaustion. Dobbs lay flat on his back in the dirt, staring at the sky like a man reconsidering every decision that had led him here.

<br/>

Mark stood beside his empty coal pile, wiping a streak of black dust from his forehead. His arms were sore and his skin was covered in soot, but his breathing was steady, his head was clear, and he felt a deep, satisfying hum of energy thrumming just beneath his skin. His hair, regrettably, was still sticking up on one side.

<br/>

Smith Master Torren—a burly dwarf-like man with a braided beard—walked out of the forge, looking from the empty coal pit to Mark, then to the two boys lying in the dirt, then back to Mark.

<br/>

"Well, be damned," Torren grunted.

<br/>

"He's a witch," Dobbs wheezed from the ground, without lifting his head. "His hair did a thing."

<br/>

"Dry air," Mark said.

<br/>

Torren looked at Mark's hair. He looked at the sky, which was full of fat, damp clouds. He decided, visibly, that he did not get paid enough to care.

<br/>

"You move like a machine, kid," Torren said, tossing two heavy copper coins to Mark, who caught them neatly. "You want work tomorrow?"

<br/>

Mark flipped the coins in the air, catching them with a grin.

<br/>

"Tomorrow," Mark said, "I'm going to need a notebook."
