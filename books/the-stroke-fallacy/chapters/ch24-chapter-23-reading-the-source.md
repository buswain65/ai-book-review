## Chapter 23: Reading the Source

<br/>

The first milestone of Project Root Access was, according to the charter, *Learn to read First Era script without the translation layer.*

<br/>

It had seemed like a perfectly reasonable goal when Mark wrote it down. It seemed considerably less reasonable on the morning he actually sat down to start.

<br/>

"The problem," he explained to the team, standing at the workbench in the Fish & Hammer workshop with his clipboard, "is that I can't *not* read it."

<br/>

Lyra, Vespera, and Torren looked at him.

<br/>

"Every time I look at First Era script, the translation layer kicks in automatically. I see the glyphs, and my brain hands me English. Instantly. I can't turn it off. There's no settings menu." He sighed. "I've checked."

<br/>

"So you can read the ancient language," Torren said slowly, "and that's a *problem*?"

<br/>

"It's a problem because I have no idea if it's reading it *right*," Mark said. "It's an unaudited dependency. I didn't install it, I don't know who wrote it, and I've been trusting its output for months without checking a single line." He tapped the clipboard. "In my old job, that would have gotten someone fired."

<br/>

"Who would they fire?" Lyra asked. "You?"

<br/>

"Whoever installed it," Mark said darkly. "If I ever find them."

---

The first obstacle was source material.

<br/>

The archive had been wiped clean. Every First Era plate, every iron-bound case, every scrap of the Concord's decree: gone, along with seven feet of construct, on a driverless wagon at three in the morning.

<br/>

"Fine," Mark said. "We work with what survived. Item one. My ledger." He opened it to Log 11, where he had painstakingly copied out the full text of the Concord decree. "We have a complete record of the decree right here."

<br/>

Lyra leaned over and looked at the page.

<br/>

"That's in English," she said.

<br/>

Mark looked at the page.

<br/>

It was, in fact, entirely in English. Every word of the decree, transcribed in his neat cursive, exactly as the translation layer had rendered it. Not a single original glyph.

<br/>

There was a long pause.

<br/>

"I documented the output," Mark said slowly, "and not the source."

<br/>

"Is that bad?"

<br/>

"It's a rookie mistake," Mark said. "It's the kind of mistake I used to write *very* stern code review comments about." He closed the ledger. "Item two."

---

Item two was Gerald.

<br/>

Lyra brought out the construct's core from her apron's twelfth pocket, wrapped in its cloth, still labeled in large letters: *DO NOT CHARGE GERALD.* She set it on the workbench under the brightest lamp.

<br/>

All four of them leaned in and looked at it.

<br/>

"Nobody charge it," Mark said.

<br/>

"Nobody's *going* to charge it," Lyra said.

<br/>

"I'm just saying it out loud. As a safety measure."

<br/>

"You say it every time we look at it."

<br/>

"Because every time we look at it, someone might be *thinking* about charging it."

<br/>

"Nobody," Torren said, with great dignity, "is thinking about charging Gerald."

<br/>

There was a pause.

<br/>

"I was thinking about it a *little*," Torren admitted. "Just to see if he'd wake up and say hello."

<br/>

"*Torren.*"

<br/>

"I said a *little*."

<br/>

Lyra pulled out her lens, angled it over the grey crystal, and went quiet for a long time. Then she drew in a slow breath.

<br/>

"There's writing," she said. "*Inside* it."

<br/>

Mark leaned in. Through the lens, he could see it: deep within the crystal's grey lattice, impossibly fine, a line of tiny glyphs etched into the structure itself, layered between the facets like letters inside a block of ice.

<br/>

The translation layer kicked in the instant his eyes touched them.

<br/>

`TARGET CLASS:`

<br/>

And then nothing. The line ended there, abruptly, like a sentence that had been cut off mid-thought.

<br/>

"It says 'Target Class,'" Mark reported, frowning. "And then it just... stops."

<br/>

"It doesn't stop," Lyra said, still peering through the lens. "There's more."

<br/>

"There isn't. It's blank after the colon."

<br/>

"Mark," Lyra said, very patiently, "there are three more glyphs after the colon. I'm looking at them right now."

<br/>

Mark looked again. *TARGET CLASS:* and then nothing. Clean, empty space.

<br/>

He looked at Lyra.

<br/>

Lyra looked at him.

<br/>

A cold feeling began to creep slowly up Mark's spine.

<br/>

"Okay," he said quietly. "That's not great."

---

Item three turned out to be the Academy's front gate.

<br/>

"There's an inscription over the main doors," Vespera said. "First Era script. It's been there since before the Academy was built. Everybody walks under it every day. Nobody can read it, of course. The official translation is the Academy motto." She recited it with the weary tone of someone who had heard it at a thousand ceremonies. "'Knowledge Through Discipline.'"

<br/>

"That's what's carved on the gate?" Mark asked.

<br/>

"That's what we *say* is carved on the gate."

<br/>

The four of them went to look at it that afternoon.

<br/>

The Academy's main doors stood open—Mark had timed it, and they had taken three full minutes to open that morning, as always—and above them, set into a long stone lintel, ran a single line of First Era glyphs, worn smooth by a thousand years of weather.

<br/>

Mark looked up at them.

<br/>

The translation layer kicked in.

<br/>

*Let knowledge be held here, and let the worthy pass within.*

<br/>

"Huh," he said. "That's not 'Knowledge Through Discipline.'"

<br/>

"What does it say?"

<br/>

He told them. Vespera frowned.

<br/>

"That's... close enough, I suppose," she said. "'Knowledge through discipline.' 'Let the worthy pass within.' Translations drift. Every scholar renders it a little differently."

<br/>

"Maybe," Mark said. He looked at the inscription for a long time. Then he took out his clipboard and handed it to Lyra. "Can you copy it? Exactly. Every stroke. As shapes, not words."

<br/>

"I can't read it."

<br/>

"That's why I'm asking *you*."

---

It took Lyra twenty minutes, perched on a ladder borrowed from an Academy groundskeeper who asked no questions after seeing the clipboard. She copied every glyph by hand with her finest stylus, stroke by stroke, the way she would copy a master template at the workshop. When she climbed down, she handed Mark the sheet.

<br/>

He looked at it.

<br/>

The layer kicked in at once. *Let knowledge be held here, and let the worthy pass within.* Eleven words.

<br/>

"Count them," he said. "The glyphs."

<br/>

Lyra counted. She frowned. She counted again.

<br/>

"Fourteen," she said.

<br/>

"I'm reading eleven words."

<br/>

"There are *fourteen glyphs*, Mark."

<br/>

"Maybe some glyphs combine," Vespera suggested. "High Imperial has compound forms. Two glyphs, one word."

<br/>

"Maybe," Mark said. "Lyra, count again."

<br/>

Lyra counted a third time, touching each glyph with the tip of her stylus.

<br/>

"Fourteen," she said. "I'm sure."

<br/>

Mark stared at the page. Then, slowly, he did something he'd done only once before: on his first week in Oakhaven, standing in front of a tax decree in the town plaza. He consciously disengaged his focus. He stopped trying to read. He let the glyphs go blurry and shapeless in front of him, until they stopped being words and became just lines and angles on paper.

<br/>

For a moment, he could see them as shapes. All fourteen.

<br/>

Then the headache hit.

<br/>

It was like an iron spike driven straight behind his left eye—worse than the cognitive hangover from his first failed candle-lighting, months ago. The glyphs swam back into focus, the layer snapped back on, and the eleven English words reasserted themselves.

<br/>

Mark sat down hard on the Academy steps, clutching his head.

<br/>

"Mark!" Lyra crouched beside him. "What happened?"

<br/>

"I can see them," Mark said through gritted teeth. "If I unfocus. I can see the shapes. But my brain *really* doesn't want me to."

<br/>

"Then stop doing that," Vespera said sharply.

<br/>

"I need to see the shapes," Mark said. "But I can't do *that* again. Not for hours at a time."

<br/>

Lyra looked at him for a long, thoughtful moment.

<br/>

"Give me two days," she said.

---

Two days later, she handed him a small wooden box.

<br/>

Inside, nestled in a scrap of cloth, sat a pair of spectacles.

<br/>

They were round, wire-rimmed, and plain, and the lenses had been ground by hand from good glass. When Mark held them up to the light, he could see that they had been ground very slightly, very precisely *wrong*: a faint, deliberate ripple running through each lens.

<br/>

"They blur fine detail," Lyra explained. "Just a little. Not enough that you can't see. But enough to smudge the edges of the strokes." She hesitated. "I thought, if the layer needs clean edges to lock onto the words, maybe it won't recognize them if they're a little fuzzy. But you'll still be able to see the shapes."

<br/>

Mark looked at the spectacles.

<br/>

He thought about the first morning he had woken up in this world, on the moss beside the river, reaching up to push his heavy reading glasses up his nose and finding only bare skin. *Twenty-twenty vision. No bifocals. No astigmatism. No smudges.* For forty years he had worn glasses, and for a few glorious months he'd been free of them.

<br/>

He put the spectacles on.

<br/>

He looked down at Lyra's copy of the gate inscription.

<br/>

The fourteen glyphs swam slightly, the fine edges of each stroke softened and blurred. And the translation layer, reaching for them, slid off. It didn't kick in. The glyphs stayed shapes. Fourteen of them. Clear, countable, unreadable.

<br/>

No headache.

<br/>

"It works," Mark said quietly.

<br/>

"*Yes!*" Lyra punched the air.

<br/>

Mark took the spectacles off. He looked at them in his hand.

<br/>

"I had *one* perk," he said. "One. Out of this entire situation. Rebuilt body, new world, magic system, and the single best part was not needing glasses anymore. And now I'm back in glasses."

<br/>

"You're welcome," said Lyra.

<br/>

"Thank you," said Mark, with deep feeling. "Truly. They're brilliant." He put them back on. "I hate them."

<br/>

He wore them back across the Academy courtyard that afternoon. Corwin, lying on his bench, opened one eye as Mark passed, studied the wire-rimmed spectacles in silence for a long moment, and then opened the other eye.

<br/>

"Did you lose a bet?" Corwin asked.

<br/>

"Research," said Mark.

<br/>

"Mm," said Corwin, and closed both eyes again. "Bad bet."

---

That evening, in the Fish & Hammer workshop, they triangulated.

<br/>

It had been Mark's idea, borrowed from every security audit he'd ever run. When you can't trust a single source, you use three independent ones and compare their outputs. Where they agree, you trust it. Where they disagree, you dig.

<br/>

The three sources sat around the workbench.

<br/>

**Source one** was Mark, wearing the anti-reading spectacles on his forehead. When he pulled them down, he saw shapes. When he pushed them up, the layer gave him English.

<br/>

**Source two** was Lyra, with her perfect hand copy of the gate inscription. She couldn't read a single glyph, which, as Mark pointed out, made her the most reliable source of all. "You're not interpreting anything. You're pure data. No bias. I'd hire you as a logging system."

<br/>

"Is that a compliment?"

<br/>

"It's the *highest* compliment."

<br/>

**Source three** was Vespera. She couldn't read First Era script either. Nobody alive could. But High Imperial, the language of every incantation in the Empire, was its descendant. Many of the roots had survived. She could look at a First Era glyph and, sometimes, recognize the ancestor of a word she had chanted ten thousand times.

<br/>

"Like reading very, very old handwriting," she explained, "from a great-grandmother who spelled everything slightly differently."

<br/>

They worked through the inscription glyph by glyph.

<br/>

Lyra pointed at each one with her stylus. Mark, glasses up, said what the layer told him. Vespera, squinting, said what she could make out from the roots. Torren, who had no linguistic skills of any kind, sat at the end of the bench and kept a tally on a board with a lump of chalk, very proudly.

<br/>

The first glyph. Mark: *Let.* Vespera: "A command form. Yes."

<br/>

The second. Mark: *knowledge.* Vespera: "*Scientia*-root. Yes."

<br/>

The third, fourth, fifth. *Be held here.* Vespera: "Yes—though 'held' is closer to 'kept back.' Or 'held behind.'"

<br/>

"Behind what?"

<br/>

Vespera pointed at the sixth glyph. "This one. I think it's 'wall.'"

<br/>

Mark pushed his glasses up and looked. The layer gave him: *and*.

<br/>

"The layer says 'and.'"

<br/>

"It's not 'and,'" Vespera said firmly. "It's a wall-root. It's in half the warding incantations I know."

<br/>

Torren made a careful chalk mark under *DISAGREE.*

<br/>

They kept going. The seventh glyph: Mark read *let*, and Vespera read something closer to "let none." The eighth: Mark read *the*.

<br/>

And then Lyra's stylus moved to the ninth glyph, and Mark, glasses up, said: "*Worthy.*"

<br/>

Lyra stopped.

<br/>

"No," she said slowly. "That's the twelfth glyph."

<br/>

"What?"

<br/>

"You just skipped three," Lyra said. "My stylus went from eight to nine, and you said 'worthy.' But 'worthy' matches glyph *twelve*." She tapped her copy. "Nine, ten, eleven. You didn't say anything for those. You just... jumped right over them."

<br/>

Mark pulled his glasses down.

<br/>

He looked at the paper. Glyphs nine, ten, and eleven sat there in a row, plain as day in Lyra's careful hand: three shapes, clustered close together, more ornate than their neighbors.

<br/>

He pushed his glasses up.

<br/>

*...let the worthy pass within.*

<br/>

Glyphs nine, ten, and eleven had vanished. Not blurred. Not garbled. Simply not there. His eyes jumped straight from *the* to *worthy* as smoothly as if the space between them had never existed. And the sentence flowed so naturally around the gap that he had never once, in all those months of reading, felt a seam.

<br/>

He pulled the glasses down. Three glyphs.

<br/>

He pushed them up. Nothing.

<br/>

Down. Three glyphs.

<br/>

Up. Nothing.

<br/>

"Mark," Lyra said softly. "Stop doing that. You're going to make yourself sick."

<br/>

Mark stopped. He took off the glasses, set them on the bench, and sat very still.

<br/>

"It's not mistranslating," he said slowly. "It's *deleting*. It's dropping three words, always together, and then smoothing the sentence over so I never notice the gap." He looked up at them. "And it's the *same three*. The same three glyphs that were missing inside Gerald's core. 'Target Class,' and then nothing. These three."

<br/>

He thought about it, the way he'd think about a production log with a suspicious hole in it.

<br/>

"It's not filtering words," he said. "It's filtering a *term*. A single, specific name, three parts long. A fully qualified identifier." He looked at the three glyphs on Lyra's copy. "And whatever's running inside my head has been configured to make sure I never, ever read it."

<br/>

Torren, at the end of the bench, made a very large chalk mark under *DISAGREE* and then, after a moment's thought, drew a frowning face beside it.

<br/>

"I've been running an unaudited dependency," Mark said quietly, "inside my own head. For *months*. And it's been silently editing my input the entire time."

<br/>

Nobody said anything for a while.

<br/>

"Is that," Torren ventured at last, "bad?"

<br/>

"Yes, Torren," Mark said. "That's bad."

---

They turned to the three glyphs themselves.

<br/>

Vespera bent over Lyra's copy, studying the first of them for a long time, her lips moving silently as she sounded out the roots.

<br/>

Then her face changed.

<br/>

"I know this one," she said. "It's in the graduation rite."

<br/>

"The what?"

<br/>

"The Academy's graduation rite. Every mage in the Empire has spoken it. At the end of the ceremony, when the new mages are welcomed into the Chapterhouse, the Archmage recites a passage in High Imperial." Her eyes were half-closed now, remembering. "'*Venite, venite, Aduenai*.' 'Come in, come in, honored guests.'" She tapped the first glyph. "That's it. *Aduenai*. It means 'the welcomed ones.' Honored guests."

<br/>

"So the first word is 'guests,'" Torren said, pleased, chalking it onto his board. "Easy."

<br/>

But Lyra was frowning.

<br/>

She had pulled her own copy closer. Then she pulled out a scrap of parchment from her apron, and with a few swift strokes she wrote *Aduenai* in High Imperial script, the way she had seen it engraved on a dozen ceremonial plaques at the workshop. She laid it directly beside the First Era glyph and stared at the two of them side by side.

<br/>

Then she reached for her lens.

<br/>

"It's not the same," she said.

<br/>

"It's *very* close," said Vespera.

<br/>

"Close isn't the same." Lyra pointed with her stylus. "Look. Here. The ceremonial spelling has this stroke, this curve, and this hook. The First Era glyph has all three. *And* it has this." She pointed at a single, short, angled stroke, cutting down through the middle of the glyph, so fine that Mark had to lean in to see it. "There's a stroke in the original that isn't in the ceremonial version. It's missing. Someone took it out."

<br/>

Vespera leaned in. She studied the angled stroke for a long moment.

<br/>

Then she went pale.

<br/>

"That stroke," she said slowly, "changes the vowel. Without it, it's a soft *u*—*Aduenai*. With it..." She swallowed. "It's a hard *v*. *Advenai*."

<br/>

"Does that change the meaning?" Mark asked.

<br/>

"Yes," Vespera said quietly. "*Aduenai* comes from a root that means 'to be welcomed in.' But *Advenai*..." She looked up at him. "It's the root for 'to arrive.' To come from somewhere else. Not as a guest. As a stranger. Those who arrive." She paused. "Uninvited."

<br/>

The workshop went very quiet.

<br/>

"Somebody," Mark said slowly, "changed one stroke."

<br/>

He thought about the transition plate in the archive. The original channel, filled with solder and recut narrower. One small, surgical change. Nothing else touched.

<br/>

"It's the same technique," he said. "Exactly the same. They took the original, kept everything that worked, and changed *one small thing* so that nobody would ever notice." He looked at Vespera. "They didn't just patch the magic. They patched the *language*. They took a word that meant 'strangers who arrive' and turned it into 'honored guests.' And then they put it in a ceremony and had every mage in the Empire recite it at graduation for five hundred years."

<br/>

Vespera put her hand over her mouth.

<br/>

"I've *said* that," she whispered. "At my own graduation. I *recited* it. 'Venite, Aduenai.' Come in, honored guests."

<br/>

"You were welcoming strangers," Lyra said slowly. "Without knowing it."

<br/>

"Five hundred years," Mark said, half to himself, "of commencement speeches. And nobody read the source."

---

The third glyph came next, and it was Vespera again who cracked it.

<br/>

She stared at it for a long time. Then she began to murmur under her breath. It wasn't the involuntary chanting of the past few weeks. This time she was doing it on purpose: running through incantation after incantation, testing roots against the shape.

<br/>

"*Flamma orietur*," she murmured. "*Elevatum orietur*... *orietur*... *ori*..." She stopped. She tapped the third glyph. "This ending. It's the same root. *Ori*. From *orietur*. 'It shall arise.' It's the end of half the spells I know." She frowned at the front of the glyph. "And this part is... a prefix. Out. From outside. *Ex*." She looked up. "*Exori*. 'Arisen from outside.' Or 'one who arises from without.' Something like that."

<br/>

Mark said the word aloud, tasting it. "*Exori*."

<br/>

There was a short pause.

<br/>

"It sounds like a prescription drug," he said.

<br/>

The other three looked at him.

<br/>

"'Ask your healer if Exori is right for you,'" Mark said. "'Side effects may include arising from outside.'"

<br/>

Nobody laughed. Nobody had any idea what he was talking about. Lyra wrote something down on a scrap of parchment, and Mark was fairly confident it was going on the *Strange Things Mark Says* list.

<br/>

"And the middle one?" Torren asked, chalk poised.

<br/>

They all looked at the second glyph.

<br/>

It sat between *Advenai* and *Exori*: a dense, angular, many-stroked shape, heavier than its neighbors. Vespera studied it for a long time. She tried root after root. She tried every incantation she knew.

<br/>

At last she shook her head.

<br/>

"I don't know it," she said. "I've never seen anything like it. It's not in any spell. Not in any rite. Not in High Imperial at all." She frowned. "It's as if it was taken out of the language completely."

<br/>

Torren chalked a large question mark onto his board.

<br/>

"So," Mark said, looking at Lyra's copy. "We have: *Advenai*. Something. *Exori*." He pulled the paper closer. "Those who arrive... *something*... arisen from outside."

<br/>

"And it's on the gate," Lyra said. "And it's inside Gerald. 'Target Class.'"

<br/>

"And the layer won't let me read it," Mark said. "At all."

---

It was Mark who built the theory, late that evening, pacing the workshop with his clipboard while the others watched.

<br/>

"Look at it from the Concord's perspective," he said. "Five hundred years ago, they deploy the Measure. They throttle the magic. They patch the templates. They patch the *language*. Why?"

<br/>

"To hide something," Lyra said.

<br/>

"To hide *this*." He tapped the three glyphs. "Julian said the Measure was a dam. That there's something on the other side of it. A threat. Something they're *holding back*." He tapped again. "Those who arrive. From beyond. Arisen from outside. That's the threat. That's what's on the other side of the dam. And the constructs—Gerald—were built to hunt it. 'Target Class.' Clean code is just a secondary trigger. A symptom. What they're really looking for is *this*."

<br/>

He turned to the board.

<br/>

"And the translation layer? I'll bet everyone who's ever tried to read First Era script has had this same filter. Every scholar. Every archivist. The Concord didn't just change one stroke in a word. They built a filter into the language itself. Into the Measure. Into the Aether grid. So that *nobody*—not the Academy, not the mages, not even an outsider like me—could ever read about *them*."

<br/>

He put the clipboard down.

<br/>

"It's the Measure," he concluded. "Censoring the script. It's not about me. It's about whatever's behind the wall."

<br/>

Lyra nodded slowly. Vespera nodded. Torren nodded very vigorously, because he could tell it was a good theory.

<br/>

It was a good theory. It was reasonable. It fit the evidence.

<br/>

None of them noticed that every single place the missing glyphs appeared—on the gate, inside the construct, in the old graduation rite—they appeared right beside words about crossing, about passing through, about *coming in*.

---

The Fish & Hammer Infirmary opened the next morning, entirely by accident.

<br/>

It started with Garek.

<br/>

He arrived at the workshop just after dawn, before the others, holding his right wrist out in front of him like an offering. He rotated it in a full circle, slowly, in both directions, with an expression of profound and slightly suspicious satisfaction.

<br/>

"Look at that," Garek said. "Look at it."

<br/>

"That's great," Mark said, still half asleep in the doorway. "You've been keeping up with it."

<br/>

"Twice a day." Garek lowered his voice. "And behind the lumber."

<br/>

"Good."

<br/>

"So," Garek said. "The knee."

<br/>

They did the knee. Mark sat him on a crate and walked him through a short set of gentle strengthening movements: slow leg lifts, careful partial squats against the wall, a stretch for the back of the thigh. Garek did them with the grim concentration of a man defusing a bomb.

<br/>

"And the back thing?" Garek asked when he was done.

<br/>

"We'll get to the back thing," Mark said. "One joint at a time."

<br/>

Garek nodded, satisfied, and left.

<br/>

Twenty minutes later, Mark looked up from the workbench and found a second enforcer standing in the doorway. Very large, very scarred, with one ear that looked like it had been folded in half at some point and never unfolded.

<br/>

"Garek said," the enforcer rumbled, a little awkwardly, "you fix shoulders."

<br/>

By noon, there were nine of them.

<br/>

By the afternoon, there was a line.

---

It stretched from the workshop's front door all the way back across the armory yard, past the four purring blowers, almost to the street. Enforcers. Dockhands. Two of Nan Brask's goat handlers. A tannery worker with a bad elbow. One of the formerly unconscious door guards from the Syndicate warehouse, who had a crick in his neck from being zapped by Duchess and had been unable to turn his head to the left for a week.

<br/>

Mark stood in the doorway and looked at the line in dawning horror.

<br/>

"This," he said slowly, "is a corporate wellness program."

<br/>

"A what?" Lyra asked.

<br/>

"In my old job," Mark said, "the company used to hire someone to come in once a quarter and tell everyone to stretch and drink more water. Everyone hated it. Nobody went." He looked at the line again. "And I've just accidentally created one. And people are *lining up*."

<br/>

The team adapted, the way they always did.

<br/>

Torren took over scheduling. He did it by walking along the line, looking at each person very carefully, and assigning them an order based on a system he never explained and that seemed, as far as Mark could tell, to rank people by how much he liked their beard.

<br/>

Legacy, as Head of Security, kept order at the front door from her web beside the carved fish. Nobody cut in line. At one point, a dockhand tried, stepping ahead of a goat handler. Legacy turned all eight eyes toward him and shifted one leg very slightly. The dockhand stepped back into his place and stayed there, very still, for the rest of the afternoon.

<br/>

Lyra set up at the end of the workbench and began carving grip trainers, small, smooth, palm-shaped blocks of oak, one for every wrist and hand complaint. She turned them out so fast and so neatly that by mid-afternoon she had a small stack, and the enforcers were comparing them with each other like trophies.

<br/>

And Mark handed out intake forms.

<br/>

He had made them himself, on parchment, clipped to brass clipboards. Simple boxes: *Name. Injury. Where does it hurt? How long?* Most of the enforcers couldn't read. So he read the questions aloud and filled them in himself, one patient at a time, sitting on a crate in the doorway, feeling more like a corporate HR representative than he ever had in forty years of corporate life.

<br/>

"Name?" he asked the enforcer with the folded ear.

<br/>

"Brann."

<br/>

"Injury?"

<br/>

"Shoulder."

<br/>

"How did it happen?"

<br/>

Brann considered. "Hit a man with a chair."

<br/>

"How many times?"

<br/>

"Lots," said Brann.

<br/>

Mark wrote down *repetitive strain injury* and moved on.

---

Vespera's role took a while to emerge.

<br/>

She'd come by in the afternoon, seen the line, and stopped in the middle of the yard with an expression of total disbelief. "What," she said, "is *happening*?"

<br/>

"Occupational health," said Mark.

<br/>

"These are *criminals*."

<br/>

"These are *patients*," Mark corrected. "Criminals with very bad posture."

<br/>

Vespera looked at the line. Then, as she watched, the enforcer at the front—an older man with a stiff, hunched lower back—lowered himself onto a crate in front of Mark with a slow, painful wince that she could feel from across the yard.

<br/>

She hesitated.

<br/>

Mark saw her hesitate. He said nothing, and waited.

<br/>

At last, Vespera walked over, slowly, and stood behind the hunched enforcer. She looked down at the stiff, knotted muscles of his lower back, visible through his shirt. Then, without a word, she raised her left wrist.

<br/>

Her silver bracelet glowed faintly blue.

<br/>

She didn't chant. She didn't reach for her staff. She laid her hand flat between the man's shoulder blades, and a gentle, steady warmth spread outward from her palm, sinking slowly into the stiff muscle beneath. It was the same quiet, perfectly controlled heat she had once used to warm a bowl of cold soup.

<br/>

The enforcer let out a long, shuddering sigh. His shoulders dropped two inches.

<br/>

"*Oh*," he said, in a very small voice for such a large man. "Oh, that's *nice*."

<br/>

Vespera blinked.

<br/>

"Low draw," Mark murmured, beside her. "Alpha mode. Gentle, steady, controlled. Perfect practice. It won't set off a single Thorne ring in the province." He paused. "And it's the exact opposite of everything Ignis teaches."

<br/>

Vespera looked at him.

<br/>

"'Aether demands suffering,'" Mark quoted. "The Guild's been telling people for five hundred years that magic has to *hurt*. And here you are, using it to make the hurting stop."

<br/>

Vespera didn't answer. But she kept her hand where it was. And when the man finally stood up, straighter than he'd sat down, and thanked her with tears in his eyes, she simply nodded and turned to the next person in the line.

<br/>

She stayed until sundown.

<br/>

By the end of the day, she was warming muscles without a flicker of hesitation: no chanting, no phantom reaching for her staff, her bracelet glowing steady and blue. Mark watched her work and realized that somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, between one sore shoulder and the next, she had stopped having to think about it at all.

<br/>

*"That's it,"* he thought. *"Muscle memory. Five thousand repetitions. She's got it."*

---

At the end of the day, Torren climbed onto a crate in the middle of the armory yard and called for everyone's attention.

<br/>

"Right," he announced to the dispersing crowd of patients. "This place needs a name."

<br/>

Mark looked up sharply from his intake forms. "Torren."

<br/>

"*You*," Torren said, pointing at him firmly, "banned me from naming the *project*. You said *nothing* about the *clinic*."

<br/>

Mark opened his mouth. Then he closed it. He went back through the founding charter in his mind, line by line, and could find no clause that applied.

<br/>

"...That's technically correct," he admitted.

<br/>

Torren beamed. He turned and pointed dramatically at the carved sign hanging over the workshop door: the fish, holding the hammer.

<br/>

"*The Fish & Hammer Infirmary*!" he declared.

<br/>

There was a short pause.

<br/>

"Why a fish?" asked Brann, the enforcer with the folded ear.

<br/>

Torren looked up at the sign with tremendous pride.

<br/>

"We fix things," he said. "And fish." He paused. "And now, *people*."

<br/>

The patients applauded. Somebody cheered. Garek, at the back, clapped slowly, and then quickly stopped when he realized his knee was feeling better and he didn't want to jinx it.

<br/>

Mark looked at the sign. He looked at Torren. He looked at the line of patients drifting off across the yard, flexing new grip trainers and rolling loosened shoulders.

<br/>

He decided to let it go.

---

The Thorne pressure, meanwhile, was getting closer.

<br/>

The message bus brought in three Geralds over the next two days, and for once, every one of them was genuine.

<br/>

The first came from Wren, the sharp-eyed eleven-year-old, who reported that two well-dressed men had spent a full hour in the market square, watching the road to the armory yard. They wore silver rings set with small gold stones. They hadn't bought anything.

<br/>

The second came from one of the goat handlers in the clinic line, who told Mark, while he was filling out her intake form, that a man with a gold-stoned ring had stopped her on the North Gate road and asked if she knew "the boy who fixes blowers." She'd said no. "He didn't believe me," she said. "Then he gave me a silver piece, and told me to forget he'd asked." She paused. "So I'm telling *you*."

<br/>

And the third came from Corwin.

<br/>

He didn't use the bus. He simply appeared at the workshop door one evening, which was so unprecedented that Mark nearly dropped his clipboard. Corwin did not leave the Academy courtyard. Corwin did not stand *up*.

<br/>

"You're vertical," Mark said.

<br/>

"It's an emergency," Corwin said. He leaned heavily against the doorframe, well away from Legacy, who regarded him with polite interest. "Julian."

<br/>

"What about him?"

<br/>

"Last night, after curfew, I was lying on the gate steps." Corwin yawned. "It's my second-favorite spot. Good stone, holds the heat. Julian came out." He paused. "He stood under the gate for an hour. In the dark. Just looking up at the inscription."

<br/>

Mark went very still.

<br/>

"He didn't notice me," Corwin said. "Nobody ever does. He just stood there. Staring at the lintel. Turning his ring." Corwin shrugged. "Then he said something. Very quiet. To himself."

<br/>

"What did he say?"

<br/>

Corwin closed his eyes, recalling it.

<br/>

"'They're getting closer to it,'" he said. "And then: 'Father won't wait much longer.'"

<br/>

The workshop was silent.

<br/>

"That's all," Corwin said. He pushed himself off the doorframe. "Now if you'll excuse me, I've been vertical for twenty minutes, and I need to go and lie down for about three days."

---

Mark had one more vault shift that week, and that night, he brought the team.

<br/>

It was Lyra's idea. "If Mott's been down there for a hundred years," she argued, "he's seen more First Era script than anyone alive. Maybe he knows what the middle glyph is."

<br/>

"Mott doesn't know what *year* it is," said Vespera.

<br/>

"Mott asks if we're new every time we see him," Lyra agreed. "But he also told us to lock the old cases. And he told us 'it doesn't like noise.' Two weeks before something came up the stairs that very much didn't like noise."

<br/>

That silenced the table.

<br/>

So the four of them went down together at eight o'clock: Mark, Lyra, Vespera, and, after considerable negotiation, Torren, who insisted on coming "in case anything needs tackling."

<br/>

The archive looked just as it had since the cleanup: immaculate, orderly, the leaning tower of plates rebuilt in its corner, propped up by its brand-new broom. Archivist Mott sat at his desk by the door, wrapped in grey wool, writing slowly in his ledger with his tiny stub of a quill.

<br/>

He looked up as they came in, and beamed.

<br/>

"Ah! Are you all new ones?"

<br/>

"Yes," they said.

<br/>

"Lovely," said Mott, and went back to his ledger.

<br/>

They set up at a table near the back with Lyra's copy of the gate inscription, and for an hour they worked quietly, comparing the three glyphs with the few scraps of First Era text that had survived on the backs of ordinary Guild plates. They found nothing. The middle glyph didn't appear anywhere else.

<br/>

At last, frustrated, Vespera sat back and rubbed her eyes.

<br/>

"Let's just say it," she said. "Out loud. Maybe hearing it will shake something loose."

<br/>

She picked up Lyra's copy and read the first and last words aloud, slowly and carefully, pronouncing them with the precise, clear cadence of a trained mage.

<br/>

"*Advenai*," she said. "...*Exori*."

<br/>

Across the room, the scratching of Mott's quill stopped.

<br/>

All four of them looked up.

<br/>

Mott was sitting very still at his desk. His quill hung motionless above the page. Slowly, very slowly, he set it down.

<br/>

Then he lifted his head and looked across the dim archive at them.

<br/>

It wasn't his usual mild, sleepy, faintly confused look. Behind the thick, bottle-bottom spectacles, his old eyes were sharp. Focused. Fixed on Vespera with an expression Mark had never seen on his face before.

<br/>

For a long moment, nobody moved.

<br/>

Mott's lips parted, as if he was about to say something.

<br/>

Then, very slowly, he closed them again. He picked up his quill. He lowered his head. And he went back to writing in his ledger, exactly as if nothing had happened at all.

<br/>

But the quill was shaking.

<br/>

Mark could see it from across the room: a faint, steady tremor in the old man's hand that hadn't been there an hour ago, the nib skittering across the page in uneven little jerks.

<br/>

None of the four of them said a word for the rest of the shift.

<br/>

When they left at midnight, Mott didn't look up. He didn't ask if they were new.

<br/>

That, somehow, was the most frightening thing of all.

---

It was nearly two in the morning when Mark sat down at his desk in the West Wing, his back to the wall. He set the wire-rimmed spectacles on the desk beside his ledger.

<br/>

In the corner of the ceiling, the lentil spider sat in its web. Mark had begun to suspect it was growing. He decided not to think about that.

<br/>

He picked up his charcoal stick and wrote:

<br/>

> *System Architecture Notes — Mark Vance*
> *Log 15: Dependency Audit — Translation Layer*
> *Summary:*
> *Translation layer (origin unknown, vendor unknown, installed without consent) is not a passive interpreter. It actively filters input. Specifically: a three-glyph term is deleted from all First Era text, and surrounding sentences are rewritten to conceal the gap.*
> *Method:*
> *Triangulation using three independent sources. (1) Self, via translation layer. (2) Lyra, transcribing glyph shapes with zero interpretation. Ideal logging system. (3) Vespera, reconstructing High Imperial roots. Torren kept the tally. Torren's tally was excellent.*
> *Tooling:*
> *Anti-reading spectacles (built by Lyra). Blur the glyph edges so the layer fails to parse while shapes remain visible. Effective. No headaches. I have glasses again. I had one perk.*
> *Findings:*
> *1. Academy gate inscription: 14 glyphs. Layer renders 11 words ('Let knowledge be held here, and let the worthy pass within'). Glyphs 9–11 silently deleted. Vespera's partial root reading suggests a different meaning: 'Let knowledge be held behind the wall... let none [of the three] pass within.' Not a motto. A warning.*
> *2. Gerald's core contains micro-etched script: 'TARGET CLASS: [3 glyphs].' Same three glyphs.*
> *3. Glyph 1 = ADVENAI ('those who arrive' — strangers, uninvited). Has been altered by a single removed stroke into ADUENAI ('honored guests'), which is recited at every Academy graduation. The Concord patched the language using the same technique as the plates: one surgical change. Five hundred years of commencement speeches. Nobody read the source.*
> *4. Glyph 3 = EXORI ('arisen from outside'). Root shared with the -orietur ending of standard incantations. Sounds like a prescription drug.*
> *5. Glyph 2 = unknown. Not present anywhere in High Imperial. Appears to have been removed from the language entirely.*
> *Working Theory:*
> *The Measure doesn't just throttle magic. It censors the script itself. The three-glyph term names whatever is on the other side of Julian's dam: 'Those who arrive, [unknown], arisen from outside.' The constructs were built to hunt it. Clean code is only a secondary trigger. The filter in my head is likely the same filter that blinds every reader of First Era script. It is not about me. It is about them.*
> *Fish & Hammer Infirmary (opened by accident):*
> *- 31 patients on day one. Intake forms on clipboards. I have reinvented the corporate wellness program. People are lining up.*
> *- Scheduling: Torren (method: beard-based, undisclosed).*
> *- Security: Legacy. Zero line-cutting incidents (one attempt; resolved by eye contact).*
> *- Grip trainers: Lyra (production rate: extraordinary).*
> *- Heat therapy: Vespera. Silent casting now fully automatic. Root cause of mastery: 5,000 repetitions on sore backs. Using the Aether to stop suffering, against 500 years of Guild doctrine.*
> *- Clinic named by Torren. The charter did not prohibit this. I checked.*
> *- Garek: wrist good, knee in progress. Back still does a thing in the mornings.*
> *Threats:*
> *- Gold-ringed men in the market square, on the North Gate road, and offering silver for information. Closing in on the workshop. Gerald.*
> *- Julian stood under the gate inscription for an hour. Said: 'They're getting closer to it. Father won't wait much longer.' Gerald.*
> *- Mott heard 'Advenai... Exori' spoken aloud and stopped writing. His hand shook for the rest of the shift. He did not ask if we were new. Gerald. Gerald. Gerald.*
> *Known Risks (updated):*
> *- Translation layer: origin unknown, vendor unknown, now known to filter input. Continue using. (No alternative.)*

<br/>

Mark set down the charcoal and read the entry through, slowly.

<br/>

His eyes lingered on the line under *Working Theory*. *It is not about me. It is about them.*

<br/>

It was a good theory. It fit the evidence. He'd have signed off on it in any design review.

<br/>

He told himself that twice. Then he closed the ledger, put the spectacles in their box, and put out the lamp.

<br/>

He lay in the dark for a long time, staring at the ceiling, thinking about the three glyphs. *Advenai. Something. Exori.* Those who arrive. Arisen from outside.

<br/>

At some point, without quite meaning to, he reached up to push his glasses up his nose.

<br/>

There was nothing there. Only bare skin.

<br/>

He lay still in the dark, his hand resting on the bridge of his nose, and found that he couldn't quite say why that small, familiar gesture left him feeling so uneasy.
