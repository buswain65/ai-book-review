### Chapter 4: The Lever and the Code

<br/>

Mark scanned the immediate surroundings, his climber’s eye spotting a fallen oak branch thick enough to support several hundred pounds and a flat, blocky piece of granite resting near the riverbank.

<br/>

He jogged over, scooped up the granite block with a lightness that still startled him, and rolled the oak log over to the cart’s listing frame.

<br/>

Garin, the merchant, crossed his arms and watched with open skepticism. "Boy, that cart weighs half a ton with the ironwork in the back. You're going to break your back—or that stick."

<br/>

"Class-one lever," Mark said, positioning the fulcrum rock two feet from the cart's iron-reinforced chassis and wedging the thick end of the oak branch underneath. "Load on one side, effort on the other, fulcrum in the middle. Basic mechanical advantage."

<br/>

Mark stepped up to the long end of the branch. He didn't just haul with his arms; he dropped his hips, engaged his core, and set his feet wide in a stable Jiu-Jitsu base, applying his body weight smoothly.

<br/>

The oak branch groaned, but physics didn't lie. With a five-to-one mechanical ratio, the heavy wooden cart lifted four inches off the ground with shocking ease.

<br/>

"Hold it right there!" Garin barked, his skepticism vanishing into sudden panic. He dropped to his knees and shoved the wooden wheel back onto the freshly mended axle, slamming the iron cotter pin into place with two heavy strikes of his mallet.

<br/>

"Done! Lower it!" Garin called out.

<br/>

Mark eased his weight off the lever. The cart settled onto its wheel with a solid, satisfying *thump*. The frame stood level once more.

<br/>

Garin wiped sweat from his forehead with the back of his sleeve, staring at Mark with a mixture of relief and bewilderment. "Well, I'll be damned. You didn't even break a sweat. Where'd a farm boy learn a trick like that?"

<br/>

"Physics," Mark said simply, brushing dirt off his palms. He looked down at his stomach, which chose that exact moment to emit a rumble like a distant thunderstorm. "Though right now, my internal combustion engine is running on empty. You wouldn't happen to have any food on that cart, would you?"

<br/>

Garin chuckled, tossing his mallet into the back of the wagon. "For saving us two hours of unloading iron bars? You can eat."

<br/>

He reached into a leather satchel near the driver’s seat and pulled out a slab of salted dried venison and a thick wedge of hard, dense bread. He tossed them to Mark, who caught them effortlessly with his youthful reflexes.

<br/>

Mark took a bite of the hardtack. It tasted like dried flour and cardboard—reminding him faintly of the cheap protein bars he used to keep in his office desk for late-night deployments—but his adolescent body seized on the calories like dry timber taking a spark. He devoured half the meat in three bites.

<br/>

The cloaked woman, who had been leaning quietly against her wooden staff, stepped closer. Her sharp brown eyes studied Mark’s face, taking in his strange, seamless efficiency and his odd, measured demeanor.

<br/>

"I am Vespera," she said, her tone cool and analytical. "And my companion is Garin. You speak strangely, boy. You move like a trained guard, but you dress like a beggar."

<br/>

"Mark," he said, swallowing a mouthful of bread. "And I'm just pragmatic."

<br/>

He gestured toward the newly repaired axle, unable to restrain his curiosity any longer. The technical itch was scratching at the back of his brain, burning brighter than his hunger.

<br/>

"If you don't mind me asking," Mark began, leaning against the cart, "about that spell you cast..."

<br/>

Vespera’s eyebrows lifted slightly. "What of it?"

<br/>

"I noticed the moss at your feet withered right when the blue light flashed," Mark said, watching her reaction carefully. "And you looked winded afterward. Am I right in assuming you didn't create new wood out of nothing? You transferred energy from the surrounding biological material, converted it, and used your voice to direct the cellular structure of the timber?"

<br/>

Vespera froze. Her hand tightened on her staff, her gaze suddenly razor-sharp.

<br/>

"Where did you learn to speak of magic like that?" she demanded softly. "Cellular structure? Energy conversion? That is not how the Academies teach the Art."

<br/>

"I’m just observing the inputs and outputs," Mark said defensively, holding his hands up. "In my... home, we study physical laws. Matter and energy can't be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. So when I saw the moss turn brown, it made sense. It’s an energy tax."

<br/>

Vespera stared at him for a long, silent moment before letting out a slow breath.

<br/>

"The Academies call it the *Law of Equal Weight*," she said slowly, stepping closer. "Magic—or *Aether*—is not free. To mend, something must yield its vitality. The incantation gives the shape, the mind gives the direction, and the world provides the force."

<br/>

Mark’s mind immediately began mapping the logic gates. *"Incantation is syntax. Intent is execution logic. Ambient Aether is the power supply."*

<br/>

"So the words," Mark pushed further, his eyes lighting up with the familiar obsession of a developer uncovering a clean API documentation, "they aren't magical themselves? They're just a syntax? A way to keep your brain from messing up the mental instructions while you route the energy?"

<br/>

Vespera blinked, clearly thrown off by his terminology, but she nodded slowly. "If a novice attempts to channel Aether without the words to bind it, the force strays. It burns the mind or shatters the spell. The words are the vessel."

<br/>

*"Boilerplate code,"* Mark thought, a wide, genuine grin spreading across his young face for the first time since he woke up by the river. *"Standardized wrappers to prevent memory leaks and mental crashes."*

<br/>

This wasn't chaos. It wasn't wild, incomprehensible mysticism. It was a structured, rule-bound system with parameters, safety protocols, and resource management.

<br/>

"Where are you two headed?" Mark asked, looking toward the road ahead.

<br/>

"Oakhaven," Garin chimed in, climbing up onto the wooden driver’s bench and gathering the leather reins. "Trading post about five miles south. We'll be there before sundown if the wheels hold." He looked down at Mark. "You got a family waiting for you there, Mark?"

<br/>

"No," Mark said softly, looking down at his unblemished, 14-year-old hands. "No family. Just me."

<br/>

Garin grunted, swinging a leg over the bench. "Well, you lift heavy things well and you don't panic around mages. Jump in the back. Help us watch for ditch-gamblers on the road, and I'll buy you a real meal at the tavern."

<br/>

Mark didn't hesitate. He hopped onto the back of the cart with a light, easy spring that would have blown out both his 60-year-old knees back in Raleigh. He leaned back against a burlap sack of grain, smelling the pine air, the damp earth, and the faint, ozone tang of lingering magic.

<br/>

Back in North Carolina, his phone was probably buzzing off the hook on his desk, Slack notifications piling up, Brad wondering why the OAuth2 pipeline wasn't refactored.

<br/>

Mark closed his eyes and let out a long, contented breath.

<br/>

*"Let it crash,"* he thought happily. *"I've got a new framework to learn."*
